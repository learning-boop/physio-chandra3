## Overview and mechanisms
Referred pain is pain felt in a region innervated by nerves other than those supplying the actual source. It follows shared spinal segments, not anatomical proximity, so the painful area is often not the problem area.

### Four pain types to keep separate
| Type | Source | Character | Neuro signs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Local (somatic) pain | Tissue at the site | Localised, mechanical, reproducible on palpation or loading | None |
| Somatic referred pain | Deep somatic tissue (facet, disc, SIJ, muscle, ligament) | Deep, dull, aching, poorly localised, diffuse borders; usually proximal to knee/elbow | None |
| Radicular pain | Ectopic discharge from an inflamed or compressed dorsal root / DRG | Shooting, lancinating, band-like (2–3 inch) running along a limb, often below knee/elbow | Radiculopathy may coexist (numbness, weakness, reflex loss) |
| Visceral referred pain | Internal organ | Vague midline pain first, then well-defined referral to a dermatome/myotome; autonomic features (nausea, pallor, sweating) | Referred hyperalgesia in skin/muscle |
| Nociplastic pain | Altered central/peripheral nociceptive processing | Widespread, disproportionate, multi-site, with fatigue, sleep and mood features | None objective; sensory hypersensitivity |

Key point (Bogduk 2009): radicular pain and somatic referred pain are different mechanisms. Radiculopathy is a loss of conduction (objective deficit), not a pain type. Pain below the knee does not by itself prove nerve root involvement.

### Why pain refers
1. Convergence–projection (Ruch, 1946): afferents from viscera or deep tissue and from skin converge on the same dorsal-horn neurons (especially wide-dynamic-range neurons in laminae I and V). The brain misattributes the input to the more familiar cutaneous or somatic field.

2. Convergence–facilitation (MacKenzie): visceral input creates an "irritable focus" in the segment that lowers thresholds for normal somatic input, producing referred tenderness.

3. Central sensitisation / unmasking of latent synapses: ongoing nociception opens silent connections from neighbouring segments, so referred areas grow in size with intensity and duration of pain (Arendt-Nielsen & Graven-Nielsen).

4. Axon branching (dichotomising afferents): a single DRG neuron sends branches to two tissues (e.g. viscus and body wall).

5. Thalamo-cortical mechanisms: convergence at supraspinal levels contributes to mislocalisation.

Clinical corollaries: referred areas enlarge with chronic pain and in sensitised patients; referral can occur to areas that are anaesthetised; and anaesthetising the source abolishes the referral, which is the basis of diagnostic blocks.

## Spinal segmental referral pain (MSK and visceral)
Every spinal segment receives input from its somatic structures and from the viscera sharing that segment. So one segment can refer from a facet, a disc or an organ to the same skin and muscle area. Maps below come from provocation and anaesthetic-block studies in volunteers and patients.

### Cervical zygapophyseal (facet) joints
| Joint | Typical referral area |
| --- | --- |
| C0–C1 / C1–C2 | Suboccipital, occipital headache, posterior auricular |
| C2–C3 | Upper posterior neck, occiput; main source of cervicogenic headache (third occipital nerve) |
| C3–C4 | Posterolateral neck, suboccipital, over levator scapulae |
| C4–C5 | Base of neck, top of shoulder (upper trapezius) |
| C5–C6 | Supraspinous fossa, lateral shoulder, upper scapula |
| C6–C7 | Infraspinous fossa, scapular body, down to inferior angle |
| C7–T1 | Interscapular, medial scapula border |

Sources: Dwyer, Aprill & Bogduk 1990; Aprill, Dwyer & Bogduk 1990; Cooper, Bailey & Bogduk 2007.

### Cervical disc (discogenic) referral
Cloward (1959) provoked cervical discs anteriorly and mapped referral to the medial scapula and interscapular region ("Cloward's areas"): C3–4 near C7 spinous process, C4–5 upper scapula border, C5–6 mid scapula, C6–7 lower scapula. Discs also refer to the shoulder and arm without root compression.

### Thoracic facets and costotransverse joints
- Thoracic facet pain is mainly ipsilateral paraspinal, spreading about one segment below and laterally; it rarely wraps to the chest wall (Dreyfuss et al. 1994).
- Upper thoracic joints (T1–T3) refer to the upper scapula and interscapular region; costovertebral joints can refer along the rib toward the anterior chest, mimicking cardiac or pleural pain.
- Thoracolumbar junction (T11–L2, Maigne syndrome): pain in the low back, iliac crest, buttock, lateral hip and groin via cutaneous branches of dorsal and ventral rami.

### Lumbar facets and disc
| Level | Typical referral area |
| --- | --- |
| L1–L2, L2–L3 | Flank, upper lumbar region, iliac crest, anterior/lateral thigh, groin |
| L3–L4, L4–L5 | Low back, buttock, greater trochanter, lateral and posterior thigh |
| L5–S1 | Lower lumbar, buttock, posterior thigh; occasionally calf |
| Lumbar disc (discogenic) | Central low back; buttock and thigh; spreads further distally as stimulus rises (O'Neill et al. 2002) |

Sources: Fukui et al. 1997; McCall, Park & O'Brien 1979. Referral below the knee is possible from facets and discs, so distance alone does not prove radicular pain.

### Sacroiliac joint
Fortin et al. (1994) mapped a roughly 3 × 10 cm zone just inferior to the PSIS. In patients confirmed by block (Slipman et al. 2000): buttock 94%, lower lumbar 72%, thigh 48%, below knee 28%, groin 14%, foot 12%. Fortin finger test (patient points within 1 cm of PSIS) supports an SIJ source.

## Nerve root (radicular) referral pain
Radicular pain comes from ectopic firing of an inflamed or compressed dorsal root or dorsal root ganglion (DRG). It is felt as shooting, electric or band-like pain in a narrow strip along the limb. Compression of a healthy root causes numbness or weakness, not pain; inflammation (e.g. from disc nuclear material) is needed for pain.

### Upper limb roots
| Root (disc) | Pain / paraesthesia distribution | Myotome (weakness) | Reflex |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| C4 (C3–4) | Neck, upper trapezius, top of shoulder | Shoulder elevation | None reliable |
| C5 (C4–5) | Lateral shoulder, lateral upper arm (deltoid patch) | Shoulder abduction, elbow flexion | Biceps |
| C6 (C5–6) | Lateral forearm, thumb and index finger | Elbow flexion, wrist extension | Brachioradialis, biceps |
| C7 (C6–7) | Posterior arm and forearm, middle finger; scapular pain common | Elbow extension, wrist flexion, finger extension | Triceps |
| C8 (C7–T1) | Medial forearm, ring and little fingers | Finger flexion, thumb extension | None reliable |
| T1 (T1–2) | Medial arm and elbow; axilla | Finger abduction/adduction (intrinsics) | None |

### Lower limb roots
| Root (usual disc) | Pain / paraesthesia distribution | Myotome (weakness) | Reflex |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| L2 (L1–2) | Groin, upper anterior thigh | Hip flexion | Cremasteric |
| L3 (L2–3) | Anterior thigh to medial knee | Knee extension, hip flexion | Patellar (partly) |
| L4 (L3–4) | Anterior thigh, medial knee, medial leg to medial malleolus | Ankle dorsiflexion, knee extension | Patellar |
| L5 (L4–5) | Lateral thigh and leg, dorsum of foot, big toe | Big toe extension, ankle dorsiflexion, hip abduction | Medial hamstring (unreliable) |
| S1 (L5–S1) | Posterior thigh and calf, lateral foot, sole, little toe | Plantarflexion, eversion, hip extension | Achilles |
| S2–S4 | Posterior thigh, perineum, saddle area | Bladder, bowel, sexual function | Anal wink, bulbocavernosus |

Red flag: saddle anaesthesia with bladder or bowel change is a cauda equina red flag and needs same-day referral.

### How much do dermatome maps predict?
- Radicular pain often does not follow textbook dermatomes. In patients with confirmed cervical or lumbar root lesions, most pain drawings overlapped several dermatomes (Murphy et al. 2009).
- Dermatomes overlap widely; the evidence-based map of Lee, McPhee & Stringer (2008) shows autonomous zones are small.
- Cervical root pain frequently includes the medial scapula (C6–C8) before arm symptoms appear.
- Proximal pain from a root (neck, buttock) is common; distal paraesthesia is the more localising sign.

### Radicular vs somatic referred pain
| Feature | Radicular pain | Somatic referred pain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Quality | Shooting, lancinating, electric | Deep, dull, aching, pressure |
| Shape | Narrow band (5–8 cm wide), travels | Broad, diffuse, fixed area |
| Depth | Superficial and deep | Deep only |
| Neurodynamic tests (ULNT, SLR, slump) | Often positive with structural differentiation | Usually negative |
| Neuro deficit | May coexist (radiculopathy) | Absent |
| Distal paraesthesia | Common | Rare |

Sources: Bogduk 2009; Murphy et al. 2009; Lee et al. 2008; StatPearls: Lumbosacral Radiculopathy.

## Sympathetic and parasympathetic referred symptoms
Visceral pain afferents travel back to the cord alongside autonomic nerves, so the route an organ's afferents take decides where its pain is referred. Most painful visceral input runs with sympathetic nerves to T1–L2; pelvic organs also use parasympathetic (S2–S4) routes, and the vagus carries mostly non-painful sensation (nausea, fullness).

### Afferent routes and where they refer
| Pathway | Organs served | Cord / brainstem entry | Referred pain and symptoms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cardiac sympathetic (cervical and upper thoracic ganglia) | Heart, aortic root | T1–T5 (also C7–C8) | Chest, left arm (T1 medial arm), interscapular |
| Greater splanchnic (T5–T9) | Stomach, duodenum, liver, gallbladder, pancreas | T5–T9 | Epigastrium, RUQ, mid-thoracic back |
| Lesser/least splanchnic (T10–T12) | Small bowel, kidney, gonads, appendix | T10–T12 | Periumbilical, flank, testis/ovary area |
| Lumbar splanchnic (L1–L2) | Distal colon, ureter, bladder dome, uterus body | L1–L2 | Groin, suprapubic, anterior thigh |
| Vagus (CN X, parasympathetic) | Heart, lungs, oesophagus, stomach, upper gut | Nucleus tractus solitarius; converges with trigeminal and upper cervical (C1–C2) neurons | Jaw, throat, ear (Arnold's branch) pain; nausea, bradycardia, vasovagal feelings |
| Pelvic splanchnic (S2–S4, parasympathetic) | Rectum, sigmoid, bladder neck, prostate, cervix, upper vagina | S2–S4 | Sacrum, perineum, posterior thigh |
| Phrenic (C3–C5, somatic but mixed) | Diaphragm, pericardium, liver/spleen capsule | C3–C5 | Shoulder tip, supraclavicular, neck |

The "pelvic pain line": organs touching the peritoneum above it refer via sympathetic routes to T10–L2; organs below it refer via parasympathetic S2–S4.

### Autonomic signs in the referred zone (viscerosomatic reflexes)
Organ disease drives reflex changes in the skin and muscle of its segment, not just pain:

- Sweating (sudomotor) changes, pilo-erection (goose-flesh) and vasomotor changes (pallor, flushing, temperature difference).
- Skin and subcutaneous hyperalgesia (Head's zones) and muscle hyperalgesia and guarding (MacKenzie's zones).
- Trophic change: thickened, less mobile subcutaneous tissue in the segment (connective tissue zones).
- Systemic autonomic response: nausea, vomiting, pallor, cold sweat, tachycardia or bradycardia, blood pressure change. These rarely come from a purely musculoskeletal source and should raise suspicion of a visceral cause.
The reverse (somatovisceral reflexes) is also described: sustained somatic input can change visceral function, but evidence for treating organ disease through spinal treatment is weak.

### Autonomic presentations of spinal or somatic origin
- T4 syndrome: upper thoracic (T2–T7) dysfunction with glove-like paraesthesia or coldness of the hands, diffuse arm pain and headache; thought to involve the sympathetic chain. Diagnosis of exclusion.
- Cervical sympathetic symptoms (Barré–Liéou / "posterior cervical sympathetic syndrome"): headache, dizziness, tinnitus, visual blurring and facial flushing after neck injury. The eponym is now considered obsolete and the mechanism unproven; screen for vestibular, vascular (cervical arterial dysfunction) and instability causes instead.
- Horner's syndrome with C8–T1 pain: ptosis, miosis and anhidrosis with shoulder/medial arm pain suggests a Pancoast (lung apex) tumour or stellate ganglion lesion. Red flag.
- Myofascial trigger points: can produce autonomic effects in the referral zone (e.g. sternocleidomastoid: lacrimation, conjunctival redness, coryza; localised sweating, goose-flesh).

### Sympathetically maintained pain and CRPS
In complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) the limb shows pain out of proportion to the injury plus sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/oedema and motor/trophic changes. Diagnosis uses the Budapest criteria (Harden et al. 2010):

1. Continuing pain disproportionate to any inciting event.

2. At least one symptom in three of four categories (sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/oedema, motor/trophic).

3. At least one sign at evaluation in two or more categories.

4. No other diagnosis better explains the picture.

Only part of CRPS pain is sympathetically maintained (relieved by sympathetic block). CRPS is increasingly understood as mixed inflammatory, autonomic and nociplastic.

Sources: Jänig 2013; Sikandar & Dickenson 2012; Harden et al. 2010; Foster & Jabbour 2007; Myers 2008; Travell & Simons.

## Somatic tissue referral pain (muscle, tendon, joint, ligament)
Deep somatic tissues refer pain to areas that are often far from the source, usually in a consistent pattern for each tissue. Kellgren (1938) first mapped this by injecting hypertonic saline into muscles and ligaments; Travell & Simons later catalogued myofascial trigger point (TrP) patterns. Referral grows with pain intensity and duration (Graven-Nielsen et al. 1997).

| Suboccipitals | Band from occiput to orbit | Cervicogenic headache, migraine |
| Levator scapulae | Angle of neck, medial scapular border | C3–C4 facet, "stiff neck" |
| Splenius capitis/cervicis | Vertex; behind eye | Headache |
| Scalenes | Anterior chest, medial scapula, lateral arm to thumb/index | C5–C6 radiculopathy, thoracic outlet, angina |
| Masseter / temporalis | Upper and lower teeth, ear, eyebrow | Toothache, TMD |

## Visceral organ referred pain patterns
Visceral pain evolves in stages (Giamberardino 2003): first vague, midline, poorly localised "true visceral" pain with strong autonomic and emotional features; then referred pain to the segmental body wall; then referred hyperalgesia in skin and muscle that can outlast the visceral problem. Parietal peritoneum or pleura involvement makes pain sharp and well localised.

### Red flags that point away from an MSK source
- Pain not altered by movement, posture or palpation, or night pain that does not ease with position change.
- Pain linked to eating, bowel, bladder, breathing, exertion or menstrual cycle.
- Systemic signs: fever, sweats, unexplained weight loss, pallor, nausea, jaundice, haematuria.
- History of cancer, age over 50 with new pain, recent trauma to abdomen or chest.
- Pulsatile abdominal mass, abnormal vital signs, pain out of proportion with guarding or rigidity.
Sources: Giamberardino 2003; Sikandar & Dickenson 2012; Heick & Lazaro 2022; Cleveland Clinic; Physiopedia (Kehr's sign).

## Centralised (nociplastic) pain
Nociplastic pain is the third mechanistic pain category, alongside nociceptive and neuropathic. IASP (2017) defines it as "pain that arises from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of actual or threatened tissue damage causing the activation of peripheral nociceptors or evidence for disease or lesion of the somatosensory system causing the pain."

### Terms that are often confused
- Central sensitisation is a neurophysiological mechanism: increased responsiveness of central nociceptive neurons to normal or subthreshold input (Woolf 2011). It can occur in nociceptive, neuropathic and nociplastic pain.
- Nociplastic pain is a clinical pain descriptor. Central sensitisation is thought to be one of its main drivers, but the two terms are not interchangeable (Nijs et al. 2023).
- Mixed pain: many patients have nociplastic pain on top of nociceptive or neuropathic pain (e.g. knee OA with widespread sensitisation, chronic low back pain after disc injury).

### Mechanisms
1. Amplified ascending signalling: dorsal-horn wind-up, enhanced temporal summation, expanded receptive fields (the same process that enlarges referred pain areas).

2. Reduced descending inhibition: weaker conditioned pain modulation (CPM), serotonergic/noradrenergic deficits; increased descending facilitation.

3. Brain changes: greater connectivity between the insula and default-mode network, altered glutamate/GABA balance, heightened salience of bodily signals.

4. Neuroimmune factors: glial activation and low-grade inflammation.

5. Contributors: poor sleep, stress, trauma history, genetics, low physical activity and catastrophising all lower the threshold.

### Clinical picture
- Pain that is regional, multifocal or widespread, often spreading beyond segmental or dermatomal boundaries, and out of proportion to tissue findings.
- Hypersensitivity to touch, pressure, heat/cold, movement, and often to light, sound and smells.
- Associated fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, memory and concentration problems ("fibro fog"), mood symptoms.
- Pain that moves around or fluctuates with stress, sleep and activity rather than with mechanical load.
- Typical conditions: fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, temporomandibular disorder, tension-type headache, bladder pain syndrome, chronic primary low back pain; also a component in many chronic MSK conditions.

### IASP clinical criteria and grading (Kosek et al. 2021)
For chronic pain affecting the musculoskeletal system:

1. Pain for more than 3 months.

2. Regional rather than discrete distribution (multifocal, widespread, or regional pain larger than a single joint or segment).

3. Pain cannot be fully explained by nociceptive mechanisms, and neuropathic pain is not present or cannot explain it.

4. Clinical signs of pain hypersensitivity in the painful region: static or dynamic mechanical allodynia, heat or cold allodynia, or painful after-sensations.

5. History of pain hypersensitivity in the region (touch, pressure, movement, heat/cold) plus at least one comorbidity: sensitivity to sound, light or odours; sleep disturbance with frequent waking; fatigue; or cognitive problems.

| Grade | Criteria met |
| --- | --- |
| Possible nociplastic pain | 1, 2, 3 and 4 |
| Probable nociplastic pain | 1 to 5 |
| Definite | Not defined yet (no gold standard) |

If nociceptive pain is also present, the patient can be graded as nociplastic plus nociceptive (mixed). Field testing has shown the system is usable but some criteria need refining. The BACPAP consortium (2024) adapted this approach to low back pain.

| Central Sensitization Inventory (CSI) | 25-item questionnaire; score of 40 or more suggests central sensitivity (Neblett et al. 2013) |
| Widespread Pain Index + Symptom Severity Scale | ACR fibromyalgia criteria (2016 revision) |
| Bedside QST | Pressure pain threshold at remote sites, temporal summation, brush allodynia, CPM |
| Screening questionnaires (DN4, painDETECT, S-LANSS) | Rule neuropathic pain in or out |
| Sleep, mood and stress measures (PSQI, PHQ-9, GAD-7, PCS) | Identify modifiable contributors |

### Links with referred pain
The size of a referred pain area reflects the gain of central processing. Patients with fibromyalgia, whiplash, knee OA or chronic low back pain show larger referred areas after standard saline injections than healthy controls (Arendt-Nielsen & Graven-Nielsen). A patient whose "referral" keeps growing, crosses the midline or ignores segmental boundaries is more likely to have a nociplastic component than a new structural source.

### Management implications
- Explain the mechanism: pain neuroscience education to reduce threat and fear.
- Graded, enjoyable exercise and activity (aerobic plus strengthening); pacing rather than boom-bust.
- Target sleep, stress and mood: CBT, acceptance and commitment therapy, pain reprocessing approaches, mindfulness.
- Medicines that act centrally (e.g. duloxetine, amitriptyline; gabapentinoids with modest benefit) rather than repeated opioids or local procedures.
- Keep local passive treatments as short-term aids linked to active goals, not the main plan.
- Treat any remaining nociceptive driver (e.g. an arthritic joint) alongside the central component.
Sources: IASP Terminology; Kosek et al. 2021; Fitzcharles et al. 2021; Woolf 2011; Nijs et al. 2023; Mayer et al. 2012; Neblett et al. 2013; Nijs et al. (BACPAP) 2024.

## Clinical reasoning: telling the sources apart
Work from the most dangerous to the most common: clear visceral and serious pathology first, then separate radicular from somatic referred pain, then judge how much central sensitisation is adding.

### Screening sequence
1. Red flags or systemic signs? Yes → medical referral for a visceral or serious cause.

2. Does pain change with movement, posture or palpation? No → suspect a visceral source; refer or investigate.

3. Neuro signs or positive neurodynamic tests? Yes → radicular pain / radiculopathy.

4. Does a local source reproduce the familiar pain? Yes → local somatic pain. No → somatic referred pain: test the spine, joints and trigger points that share the segment.

5. Is the pain widespread, disproportionate and hypersensitive? Yes → add a nociplastic component to whichever source(s) you found.

This sequence is a screening aid; mixed presentations are common, so more than one step can apply.

### Quick comparison
| Feature | Somatic referred | Radicular | Visceral referred | Nociplastic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Quality | Deep ache | Shooting, electric | Vague, deep, colicky or pressure | Variable, burning, aching, tender everywhere |
| Distribution | Segmental, diffuse, fixed | Narrow band along limb | Segmental with midline start | Widespread, non-segmental |
| Movement / load | Changes pain | Changes pain (neural loading) | Little effect | Inconsistent |
| Neurological signs | None | May be present | None | None |
| Autonomic features | Mild or none | None | Prominent | Common (sleep, fatigue, dysautonomia) |
| Key test | Local anaesthetic block / provocation | Neurodynamic tests, neuro exam, imaging when indicated | Organ-specific exam, bloods, imaging | Kosek criteria, CSI, QST |

### Checklist
- Screen red flags and systemic symptoms; ask about eating, bowel, bladder, breathing, exertion and menstrual links.
- Draw the pain on a body chart and compare with the segmental tables above.
- Test the spine and joints that share the segment (facet, disc, SIJ, hip, shoulder).
- Palpate muscles whose trigger-point patterns cover the painful area.
- Run a full neurological exam and neurodynamic tests.
- Look for signs of hypersensitivity and widespread pain; score CSI if suspected.
- Re-assess: if treatment of the suspected source does not change the referred pain, reconsider the source.
