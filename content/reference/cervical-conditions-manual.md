# Cervical conditions: AIM Theory Manual 2023, Chapter 2.1

Four parts: sensorimotor function and cervicogenic dizziness (pp. 236-254), degenerative cervical myelopathy (pp. 255-264, prepared by Teresa Gravelle, FCAMPT), cervical radiculopathy (pp. 265-277), and concussion and whiplash resources (pp. 278-279).

## 1. Sensorimotor function and cervicogenic dizziness (CGD)

**Mechanism (pp. 236-238).** Balance and head and eye control integrate visual, vestibular and somatosensory input. The upper cervical spine dominates the somatosensory input through its links with the visual and vestibular reflexes (cervico-ocular, vestibulo-ocular, vestibulocollic, cervicocollic, tonic neck, vestibulospinal). Abnormal afferent input from upper cervical joint and muscle receptors, mismatched with vision and the inner ear, is the usual explanation for CGD (Treleaven 2017). Muscle spindles may matter more than joint receptors (Kristjansson and Treleaven 2009). Trauma, sympathetic activation, stress, pain and inflammation can all alter the receptors (Grieve's ch. 32.1). There is no gold-standard test or test cluster: CGD has been a diagnosis of exclusion, with defined subjective features and impairments now emerging.

**Subjective presentation (p. 239; Treleaven 2017, Reiley 2017).** It needs concurrent neck pain or dysfunction, with the dizziness behaving like the neck pain.
- Descriptors: vague dizziness (unsteady, off-balance, light-headed, "drunk", motion-sick); rarely true spinning. Visual disturbance (needing to concentrate to read, visual fatigue, judging distances, light sensitivity). Loss of balance, possibly falls.
- Behaviour: fluctuates with the neck pain; brought on by neck movements, positions and postures; may relate to fatigue, anxiety and stress; intensity in proportion to the neck pain; episodic, minutes to hours.
- Associated: neck pain or stiffness, headache, sometimes nausea.
- After concussion, history alone could not separate central, peripheral vestibular and cervicogenic dizziness (Reneker 2015A): several systems are often involved.

**Visual symptoms (p. 244; Treleaven and Takasaki 2014).** More common and troublesome in neck pain than in controls, most of all after whiplash and with higher pain and dizziness handicap. More than half report needing to concentrate to read and light sensitivity. The most troublesome are concentrating to read, visual fatigue, judging distances and light sensitivity. Uncommon, so think of other causes: double vision, red eyes, dizzy reading, spots or words moving.

**Objective (pp. 240-247).**
- Delphi after sport concussion (Reneker 2015B): only vestibular tests reached strong utility (Dix-Hallpike, orthostatic hypotension testing, spontaneous and gaze-hold nystagmus, head impulse, roll test, saccades, VOR cancellation, head shake, smooth pursuit). No CGD-specific test did. No consensus on balance, convergence, dynamic visual acuity, dizziness on passive mobility or palpation, or JPE. Rated weak: flexion-rotation test, SPNTT, vibration tests, head-neck differentiation, DNF/DNE motor control.
- Cervical exam: whole cervical spine, focused on C0-C3; segmental mobility for painful or stiff levels. Sensorimotor deficits are greater with upper cervical than lower cervical pain, and greater again after trauma (Treleaven 2011A). The flexion-rotation test should be positive if C1-2 is involved (validated for headache, not CGD). Segmental mobility + palpation tenderness + extension-rotation test identifies facet-mediated pain (Schneider 2014). The craniocervical flexion test was impaired in 81.6% of dizzy post-concussion patients (Reneker 2018); check the suboccipitals.
- Somatosensory impairments: three groups, namely position sense, eye movement control and balance. Most marked in whiplash with dizziness, then whiplash without dizziness, then non-traumatic neck pain. In idiopathic neck pain, recruited for pain rather than dizziness, an RCT found no difference on 7 sensorimotor tests (Zoete 2019).
- Head relocation (JPE): laser on the head, target at 90 cm; more than 7.1 cm (about 4.5 degrees) is abnormal. Reliable and valid. Trunk relocation under a fixed head avoids vestibular input (promising, inconsistent).
- Movement sense (tracing): zigzag more than 9 errors outside a 5 mm band, figure-of-8 more than 10, especially if over 28 s; errors matter more than time (Ernst 2019; Werner 2018).
- Eye movement: gaze stability (reduced range and speed of head movement while fixing a target), eye-head and head-trunk coordination (poor dissociation), SPNTT (pursuit worse with the trunk rotated 45 degrees under a fixed head). Clinical finger SPNTT: sensitivity 27%, specificity 79%, so a positive test helps rule in a cervical contribution and a negative one does not rule it out (Daly 2018). Higher cervico-ocular reflex gain in chronic neck pain (Ischebeck 2017).
- Cervical torsion test: head fixed, trunk rotated 90 degrees each way, 30 s per position; positive with nystagmus over 20 per second.
- Balance: increased A/P sway in narrow, tandem and single-leg stance, or a stiffening strategy. Sway worse in torsion than with the head turned on the trunk points to the neck (Williams 2017). Static: modified BESS, torsion balance test (eyes closed). Dynamic: step test, 10 m walk with and without head turns, tandem walk, stairs, perturbations, functional reach. Only the torsion balance test helps separate cervicogenic from other causes.

**CGD vs BPPV (p. 247; L'Heureux-Lebeau 2014).** CGD more often has a "drunk" or light-headed feeling, pain reproduced on upper cervical examination, raised JPE, nystagmus on the cervical torsion test (sensitivity 72%, specificity 92%) and a positive SPNTT (56%, 88%). Head relocation plus the torsion test separates them best. These were laboratory (videonystagmography) tests, and only against BPPV.

**Reiley 2017 stepwise algorithm (p. 248).** (1) Dizziness and cervical pathology present, else CGD unlikely. (2) Triage: positive Canadian C-spine rule or arterial red flags, refer for imaging; cervical range; instability and arterial tests at the clinician's discretion, abnormal: refer to a physician; neuro screen, abnormal: refer to a physician. (3) Vestibular assessment: abnormal, treat or refer case by case, and go on to step 4 if dizziness persists. (4) Detailed cervical examination (mobilisation, manual traction, palpation). (5) Neck torsion, cervical relocation and movement sense tests: diagnose CGD if consistent.

**Management (pp. 249-250).**
- Manual therapy: positive but low-quality RCTs (Reid 2005); moderate evidence (Lystad 2011). Maitland mobilisation or Mulligan SNAGs to the upper cervical spine with a home exercise both beat control to 12 months, with no difference between them (Reid 2014, 2015).
- Exercise: neck exercise delivered with a cognitive behavioural approach (pain education, relaxation, coping) beat neck exercise alone in chronic whiplash with dizziness, though many were still dizzy at 12 months (Treleaven 2016). Low-load deep neck flexor training improved JPE, pain and disability; somatosensory retraining was marginally better (Jull 2007).
- Proprioceptive retraining: in dizzy mTBI with abnormal neck proprioception and no clear vestibular or central cause, 85% improved with head relocation practice vs 18% with vestibular rehab (Hammerle 2019, chart review).
- Multimodal and tailored (Treleaven 2017): cervical manual therapy and exercise plus sensorimotor training (relocation, eye movement control, balance) matched to the patient's impairments.

## 2. Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM)

**Background (pp. 255-256).** The commonest non-traumatic cord impairment in adults. Causes: disc degeneration, spondylosis, osteophytes, ossified or calcified posterior longitudinal ligament and ligamentum flavum. Most often C5/6. At least 4.1 to 60.5 per 100,000 in North America. Men from about 50, women from about 60; more in men of Asian descent. Risk: congenitally narrow canal, previous neck trauma. No pathognomonic sign; insidious, stepwise, episodic. Often the legs first (gait, weakness, spasticity), then LMN signs in the hands. MRI sensitivity 79-95%, specificity 82-88%; X-rays do not rule in or out. Bladder or bowel loss is less common and later.

**Diagnosis.** Clinical signs plus MRI, weighted equally. Cook cluster: gait deviation, +Hoffmann, inverted supinator sign, +Babinski, age 45 or over. 3 or 4 of 5 gives a post-test probability of 94-99%; 1 or fewer rules out. Differentials: MS, B12 deficiency, ALS, peripheral nerve entrapment; also stroke, cerebral lesions, Guillain-Barre, vertebral tumour.

**Subjective (p. 257).** Insidious; paraesthesia in 2 limbs (radicular) or 4; neck and shoulder girdle pain (not always); gait or balance problems, falls; clumsy hands, dropping things; worse dexterity (writing, buttons); neck movement brings on paraesthesia, usually at end-range flexion or extension; usually 50-70, more men.

**Objective (pp. 257-262).** Later, bilateral wasting (shoulder girdle, hand intrinsics); may have increased thoracic kyphosis. Gait: unsteady, wide-based, spastic or ataxic, symmetrical. ROM may be lost and reproduce paraesthesia. Neuro: early, upper limb LMN signs (hyporeflexia, sensory loss, key muscle weakness); advanced, more than 2 root levels, lower limb hyperreflexia, marked weakness and sensory loss in arms and legs. Slump may give Lhermitte's sign. Balance: Romberg, tandem, narrow base.

Tests (normal ageing lowers all of them):

| Test | How | Normal | Suggests DCM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hoffmann | Flick the middle finger nail, DIP held | No movement | Index and thumb flex |
| Inverted supinator | Tap the radial styloid, forearm supported | Brachioradialis jerk | Finger flexion or slight elbow extension |
| Babinski | Stroke lateral sole, heel to great toe | Toes flex | Great toe extends, others fan |
| Lhermitte | Cervical flexion | Nothing | Electric shock down the spine, possibly to the limbs |
| Grip dynamometer | Seated, elbow 90 degrees, forearm neutral, both sides | | MCID 5-6 kg |
| Foot tapping, 10 s | Seated, heel on the floor | 31 | 24 or fewer |
| Grip and release, 10 s | Mean of both hands | F 20, M 22 | F 11 or fewer, M 16 or fewer |
| Triangle step, 10 s | 30 cm triangle, each foot | 24 | 17 or fewer |
| 10 s step test | High steps in place, rail nearby | 20 | 13 or fewer |
| Tally counter, 10 s | Thumb, mean of both hands | 47 | Below 33 |

Outcome measures: JOA (upper limb 5, lower limb 7, hand sensation 3, micturition 3; 18 best); European Myelopathy Score (self-report); Nurick grade 1-5 (1: cord signs, walking normal; 5: chair-bound or bedridden). Also NDI, PSFS, NPDS (not validated for DCM). Pick one upper and one lower limb performance test.

**Management (pp. 256-259).** No consensus for mild and moderate DCM; slow deterioration with stable periods is expected. Surgery (decompression, fusion, laminectomy) for moderate to severe, better when younger and with shorter symptoms; afterwards about a third improve, a third stay the same and a third worsen. Conservative evidence is low to moderate, case by case; full recovery is rare.
- Goals: function, pain, prevent neuro deterioration, improve deficits and symptoms.
- Manual therapy: **monitor neuro status; avoid cervical manipulation** (likely UMN signs). Consider manual traction (general or specific), mechanical traction, cervical mobilisation in hypomobile regions, thoracic extension mobilisation and manipulation to improve axial extension, soft tissue release.
- Exercise: diaphragmatic breathing; cervical stabilisation with deep neck flexors; postural retraining with axial extension to off-load the neural structures; mobility (cervical, shoulder girdle, thoracic) without worsening neuro signs; balance; aerobic; upper and lower limb strength, motor control and grip; gait re-education and aids.
- Education: nature and progression; red-flag signs, seek medical help at once; posture; avoid positions and movements that worsen neuro signs; prognosis (decline with stable periods, no expectation of full recovery).
- Other: taping (postural cue, neural off-loading); IMS or dry needling for hypertonicity.

## 3. Cervical radiculopathy

**Background (p. 265).** A space-occupying lesion narrows the intervertebral foramen (IVF), compressing or inflaming a root: most often a disc herniation or facet degeneration. Neck pain radiating in the dermatome, ipsilateral scapular pain, key muscle weakness, altered sensation, reflex changes. Incidence 83 per 100,000 a year, 203 per 100,000 in the fifth decade; more in men; C6 and C7 most often. Natural history: positive but often slow; most symptoms resolve in 6 months; 50% fully recover in 6-12 months; 70-90% are symptom-free at 5-10 years; 83% with a disc herniation fully recover in 24-36 months.

**Imaging and diagnosis (p. 266).** Conservative care first. MRI for non-responders and surgical candidates; CT myelography if MRI is contraindicated or does not match. Selective nerve root block for multilevel lesions or discordant imaging. Imaging and symptoms often disagree, EMG adds little when the diagnosis is unclear, and clinical tests are imperfect: the diagnosis is mainly clinical.

**Examination and clusters (pp. 266-268).**
- Wainner 2003 variables: ULTT A (median), rotation to the affected side under 60 degrees, flexion under 55 degrees, distraction, biceps reflex and strength, "where are your symptoms most bothersome?", Valsalva, Spurling, shoulder abduction test, "do neck movements or positions improve your symptoms?", C5 dermatome. The 2017 CPG adds the flexion-rotation test and pressure pain threshold.
- Wainner cluster (ULTT A, rotation under 60 degrees, distraction relieves, Spurling provokes): 3 of 4, 65% probability; 4 of 4, 90%. ULTT sensitivity 0.97, -LR 0.12: a negative test makes radiculopathy unlikely.
- Rubinstein: suggestive history plus positive Spurling, relief with traction or distraction, and worse with Valsalva rules in; a negative ULTT rules out.
- Thoomes: history plus Spurling, relief with axial traction and a positive arm squeeze test rules in; 4 negative neurodynamic tests (ULTT 1, 2a, 2b, 3) and a negative arm squeeze test rule out.
- Favourable outcome with physiotherapy (Cleland 2007): age under 54, non-dominant arm, looking down does not worsen symptoms, multimodal care (manual therapy, traction, deep neck flexor training in over half the visits). 3 of 4, 85% success; 4 of 4, 90%.
- Poorer recovery: longer duration, higher baseline neck pain and disability, less rotation towards the affected side.
- Outcomes: NDI, PSFS (more reliable than NDI here), NPRS, NASS; MCID NDI 8.5, PSFS 2.2, NPRS 2.2.

**Treatment (pp. 269-274).**
- Principles: maximise IVF space, centralise pain, minimise neural tension, address the cause of compression. Multimodal care (mobilisation, manipulation, stretching, strengthening, motor control, neural mobilisation) helps pain and function short and long term.
- CPG 2017: acute, exercise with mobilising and stabilising elements, low-level laser, short-term collar at most (prolonged use is harmful); chronic, stretching and strengthening plus cervical and thoracic mobilisation or manipulation, education to stay active at work and in exercise, intermittent traction. Build home-exercise adherence.
- IVF: flexion with contralateral rotation and side flexion opens it; extension with ipsilateral rotation and side flexion closes it. Combined manual therapy and exercise works for acute and subacute radiculopathy, but IVF-opening techniques were not better than others (Langevin 2015). Early on, opening techniques are better tolerated and favour centralisation.
- Traction: manual first, to watch for centralisation (localised or locked, can add flexion and contralateral side bend). Mechanical evidence is inconsistent; intermittent, long hold and short rest, 2-3 times a week. Raney 2009 rule (not validated): peripheralisation with lower cervical mobility testing, positive shoulder abduction sign, age over 55, positive ULNT1, positive neck distraction; 3 of 5, 79%; 4 of 5, 95%. Adding mechanical traction to exercise reduced pain and disability, most at 6-12 months; over-door traction sat between (Fritz 2014). Traction adds to pain relief more than to function (Romeo 2018).
- Mobilisation: PPIVMs and PAIVMs to open the IVF, first in flexion with contralateral rotation and side bend; muscle energy; contralateral lateral glides if symptoms do not peripheralise. Progress to active neural mobility and exercise; once irritability settles, restore unilateral extension (straight AP first).
- Manipulation: thoracic as indicated, a single thrust better than sham in the short term (Young 2019); cervical, gapping or flexion techniques only.
- Neural mobilisation: unload first; treat the cervical interface first (mostly contralateral lateral glides); sliders, then tensioners once conduction signs settle.
- Exercise: early exercise tends to aggravate; IVF-opening exercises (SNAGs); postural correction; sliders before tensioners; strength, motor control, neck-specific training. Monitor for centralisation.
- Education: early on, an adaptive posture that unloads the nerve may be better left alone, since correcting it can peripheralise; ergonomics; unloading positions; adherence. Other: taping (ipsilateral scapular elevation and upward rotation), soft tissue release, collar.
- Medical: surgery for motor weakness or progressive deficit or symptoms despite conservative care, with questionable long-term benefit (25% still have debilitating pain at 12 months); transforaminal steroid injection; radiofrequency neurotomy.

## 4. Concussion and whiplash (pp. 278-279)

- Concussion: in depth it is outside the AIM course. A physiotherapist should be able to tell primary concussion from vestibular, cervicogenic and oculomotor dysfunction, treat the biomechanical impairments, and refer more complex cases to physiotherapists who specialise in it. Resource: Ontario Neurotrauma Foundation Guidelines for Concussion/mTBI and Persistent Symptoms, 3rd edition (2018), concussionsontario.org (now a living guideline).
- Whiplash-associated disorders: complex and multifaceted; the AIM biomechanics, manual therapy and exercise approaches apply to WAD impairments. Resource: Whiplash Injury and Chronic Pain (2019, free interactive book; Anita Gross, McMaster University).
