# Multiple sclerosis: nervous-system screen and diagnosed MS

## On the site

- **Route A, undiagnosed (not named as MS, the document's open item 1):** a final-check question, `pc-neuro` in src/data/patternChecks.js, asked of anyone who described pins and needles or numbness. It covers blurred or lost vision in one eye or double vision, Lhermitte's sign, heat-sensitive symptoms (Uhthoff), and earlier episodes that came and went (dissemination in time), unless already explained by a diagnosed neurological condition. A yes means "see your family doctor in the next few days" (lost vision in one eye: today or urgent care), with no booking until a doctor has seen them (`noBooking`).
- **Route B, diagnosed:** "Multiple sclerosis, diagnosed by a neurologist" on the "Anything else we should know?" list (`ca-ms`). It does not stop physiotherapy; the results page explains that exercise is safe and recommended, that the programme is paced around energy and heat, and when to call the MS team (a new or clearly worse symptom lasting more than a day without fever or infection).
- **Already on the site:** stroke signs (911), cauda equina and spinal cord compression (emergency), meningitis (911), spreading weakness in hands and feet with breathing or swallowing trouble (the neck's nrf-cord-legs, 911), the glove-and-stocking neuropathy check, cervical myelopathy.
- **Not built:** the document's scored block. Its questions are general "nervous system or muscle and joint" questions; the one screening question above asks the strongest of them, and routes the same way. The documented onset question ("over hours to a few days, lasting more than a day") is not asked separately.

## Recognising a possible demyelinating event

- A relapse develops over hours to days, lasts more than 24 hours (often weeks), then partly or fully settles; not linked to injury. Common first events: optic neuritis (blurred or lost vision in one eye, pain on eye movement), sensory symptoms in a limb or one side, leg weakness or heaviness, unsteadiness, diplopia. Progressive forms: walking difficulty builds over months to years.
- Pattern cues: altered feeling or weakness more than pain, not following a joint or a single nerve; patchy limb, one side, a trunk band ("MS hug"), one side of the face.
- Usually first between 20 and 50; women 2-3 times men; higher rates far from the equator, including Canada; family history, smoking, low vitamin D, previous EBV.
- Undiagnosed with UMN or sensory features: brief screen (gait, Romberg, finger-nose, heel-shin, reflexes with clonus and Babinski, sensory level, Lhermitte), document, GP letter requesting neurology referral; do not treat as mechanical. Diagnosis is the neurologist's (2024 McDonald criteria, MRI).
- Look-alikes: nerve root irritation, peripheral entrapment, peripheral neuropathy (B12, diabetes, alcohol), stroke or TIA (seconds to minutes: 911), functional symptoms or hyperventilation, migraine aura (under an hour, then headache), NMOSD and other demyelinating conditions, cervical myelopathy.

## Diagnosed MS: physiotherapy

- Measures: PDDS or EDSS from the team; Timed 25-Foot Walk, 2- or 6-Minute Walk, TUG, Berg or Mini-BESTest, 9-Hole Peg Test; Modified Fatigue Impact Scale; Modified Ashworth or Tardieu; MSWS-12; falls history; bladder, heat sensitivity, cognition and mood for pacing and safety; orthoses, FES and walking aids; relapse vs pseudo-relapse (heat, infection).
- NICE NG220: supervised exercise for mobility and fatigue; vestibular rehabilitation for balance and dizziness; spasticity: positioning, stretching, exercise and aids before medication review; MDT and MS-specialist physiotherapy where available (UBC MS Clinic, Fraser Health neuro-rehab locally).
- Exercise dose (Kalb 2020; Canadian guidelines): working toward about 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week plus strength 2-3 times a week, scaled to the person; exercise does not cause relapses. Fatigue: pacing, energy planning, graded exercise, cooling strategies.
- Secondary musculoskeletal pain from altered walking and posture: usual approaches, adapted; report neuropathic pain to the MS team. After a relapse: a focused block once the team agrees.
