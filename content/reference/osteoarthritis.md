# Osteoarthritis: shared clinical foundation

## What it is

A whole-joint condition: mechanical and biological events upset the balance of breakdown and repair in cartilage, chondrocytes, matrix and subchondral bone, with the capsule, ligaments, synovium and muscles involved (Badlissi 2017, BMJ Best Practice). The result is pain, stiffness and loss of function. It is the most common "lifestyle disease" over 65. In the USA about 37% of adults have some OA and 23% moderate to severe; the lifetime risk is 40-50% (AIM manual p. 572). Women are affected more after 50, with more joints involved (knee, hand), more morning stiffness, swelling and night pain, and more Heberden's nodes. Asians have less hip OA.

**Joints (Salter):** hand and wrist (the most common form, in up to 80% of older people), knee (nearly 50% over 74), hip, lumbar (L4/5, L5/S1), cervical (C3-5), 1st MTP; elbow and ankle mostly after trauma (ankle OA about 1%).

**Pathology (pp. 573-575).** Fibrillation (proteolytic enzymes, less shock absorption and lubrication, subchondral microfractures), cyst formation, osteophytes (peripheral cartilage ossifies as instability grows), eburnation, subchondral sclerosis, synovitis with a thickened, inelastic capsule. Pain comes from the innervated bone, synovium and capsule, not the cartilage itself; structure and pain correlate poorly.

**Primary and secondary OA.** Primary: no clear cause (hands, knee, hip, 1st MTP). Secondary: old injury, dysplasia, inflammatory arthropathy (gout, RA), Paget's, haemochromatosis.

**Risk factors.** Modifiable: overweight, muscle weakness, years of heavy repetitive loading (construction, competitive soccer), inactivity, previous joint injury (e.g. meniscal tear). Not modifiable: age, sex, heredity (especially hand OA and metabolic syndrome; Roos and Arden). Each extra kilogram multiplies joint load several times, depending on the joint.

## Diagnosis

- **Clinical, without imaging** (NICE NG226): 45 or over, activity-related joint pain, and no morning stiffness or stiffness of 30 minutes or less. Image only for atypical features or before surgery. Symptoms often precede radiographic change by 10-15 years (AIM manual p. 576).
- **Knee** (Altman 1986, ACR clinical): knee pain plus 3 of 6 (over 50, stiffness under 30 minutes, crepitus, bony tenderness, bony enlargement, no palpable warmth): sensitivity 95%, specificity 69%.
- **Hip** (Sutlive 2008): squatting aggravates, active flexion gives lateral hip pain, scour with adduction gives lateral hip or groin pain, active extension is painful, passive IR 25 degrees or less: 4 of 5, +LR 24.3. Capsular pattern IR, then flexion, then abduction.
- **Hip, JOSPT classification** (Cibulka 2017, restated in Koc 2025, A): over 50, moderate anterior or lateral hip pain on weight bearing, morning stiffness under 1 hour, IR under 24 degrees or IR and flexion 15 degrees less than the other side, and/or pain on passive IR. Imaging (ACR): radiographs first, then ultrasound, then MRI or CT. Management (Koc 2025): manual therapy (A, including long-axis distraction and mobilisation with movement), exercise 1-5 times a week for 5-16 weeks (A), dry needling (A), education with pain-coping skills (B), weight loss 5-7.5% (B), gait aids (C), ultrasound by shared decision only (D), no brace first line (F).
- **Measures:** NPRS; KOOS, HOOS or WOMAC; PSFS; the OARSI performance set (30-s chair stand, 40-m fast-paced walk, stair climb), TUG, balance. Hand: AUSCAN or FIHOA, grip and pinch.

## Stages (AIM manual pp. 576-580)

| Stage | Joint | What patients notice | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early | Surface fibrillation, fewer cells and proteoglycans; bone unaffected | Slight loss of movement, morning and evening stiffness, aching sometimes at night, arthrogenic muscle inhibition | Find it early; education, exercise, weight; active range |
| Moderate | Thinner cartilage, instability, synovitis, capsular and ligament fibrosis, osteophytes, fibrosed mechanoreceptors | More capsular loss, a joint that gives way and cannot be trusted, more night ache, reduced proprioception; trouble with socks, standing after rest, stairs, floors, getting up from the floor; jars, writing, buttons (hands) | Neuromuscular and strength work through range for confidence; manual therapy and myofascial work for range; restore muscle balance (aligned loading, mild discomfort at most, enough repetitions for a muscle "burn") |
| Advanced | Marked cartilage loss, clefting into bone, thickened trabeculae | Joint thickening, crepitus, deformity (e.g. varus) | Function in the best alignment, strength around the joint and neighbouring joints, range; prepare for surgery if needed; walking aids, unloader braces, wedge insoles in some cases |

## Management

- **First line at every stage:** education, exercise (strength, neuromuscular, aerobic) and weight management (OARSI 2019; ACR 2019; NICE NG226; AAOS 2021). For most people with early to moderate OA these are enough; medication and passive treatment (mobilisation, massage) are added to make exercise possible, not instead of it.
- **Neuromuscular exercise (NEMEX, Ageberg 2010; GLA:D, Skou and Roos 2017):** functional exercises with hip-knee-foot alignment (step-ups, chair squats), carried into sit-to-stand, toilet transfers and getting up from the floor; 2-3 sessions a week (GLA:D: 2 education sessions and 12 supervised sessions over 6 weeks), each leg worked on its own; other activity on the days between.
- **Pain monitoring:** up to 5/10 during exercise is acceptable, as long as pain, swelling and stiffness are back to usual by the next morning.
- **Education:** avoid "wear and tear" framing, which makes people fear loading the joint (Hendry 2006); explain the whole joint and how strength protects it, bone density and fall risk. Self-management works best once people are well informed and have felt the effect of a good programme.
- **Other:** stretches (hamstrings, calf, hip rotators, quadriceps), movement in and out of close-packed positions for joint nutrition, a cane in the opposite hand, a tibiofemoral or unloader brace, thumb base orthosis (EULAR 2018), footwear and rocker soles for the foot and ankle. Thumb base: manual therapy and exercise improved pain but not pressure pain threshold, pinch or grip (Villafane 2013).
- **Not recommended routinely** (NICE NG226): acupuncture, glucosamine, strong opioids; manual therapy only alongside exercise.

## Screen and refer

Inflammatory arthritis (morning stiffness over an hour, several swollen joints, MCPs and wrists), gout or pseudogout (sudden hot swollen joint), septic arthritis (hot joint with fever: emergency), polymyalgia rheumatica (over 50, both shoulders and hips), avascular necrosis or stress fracture (rapid severe pain, night pain, steroids, alcohol, osteoporosis), referred pain (lumbar spine for hip and knee; hip for knee), fracture after a fall.
