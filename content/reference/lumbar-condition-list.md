## 3. Condition summaries

### 3.1 Lumbar radiculopathy (lumbo-sacral radiculopathy)
Definition: Loss of conduction in a spinal nerve root: numbness when the sensory branch is involved, weakness when the motor branch is involved. Not defined by pain — radicular pain is nerve-root irritation; somatic referred pain can coexist and is not radiculopathy (Bogduk 1999).
Causes: Mechanical compression or inflammation of the root. Lumbar disc herniation is the most common cause; others include foraminal stenosis (osteophytes, subluxation, buckled ligamentum flavum, Paget’s), epidural disorders (lipoma, infection), meningeal cysts, and neurological disorders (diabetes, cysts, tumours).
Epidemiology: Prevalence 2.2% (general population) to 34% (specific working populations). Men typically in their 40s, women in their 50s–60s; overall prevalence likely equal. L4, L5 and S1 roots account for ~90%. Lifetime surgery prevalence 10% vs 1–2% for low back pain.
Risk factors: Prior axial low back pain, prior cervical radiculopathy, reduced canal size, manual labour, sustained flexion/rotation, prolonged driving.
Presentation: Varies by root; weakness, numbness, paraesthesia or a combination. Pain is common but does not define the diagnosis.
Clinical examination: 
- Reflexes (patellar, Achilles), key-muscle testing for fatigable weakness, dermatomal sensation, neurodynamic tests (SLR, Slump).
- Accuracy (Tawa 2017): sensation sens 0.08–0.61 / spec 0.63–1.00; motor sens 0.13–0.61 / spec 0.47–0.97 (L4, L5 most specific); reflexes sens 0.14–0.67 / spec 0.60–0.93; SLR sens 0.29–0.84 / spec 0.21–1.00; Slump sens 0.84–1.00 / spec 0.83–0.83.
- NASS 2012: use key muscle, reflex and supine SLR for suspected disc herniation with radiculopathy.
- Imaging for recent-onset LBP without red flags is not supported; for radiculopathy its benefit is uncertain — offer only after due consideration (Stochkendahl 2018).
Treatment (Stochkendahl 2018, recent onset): 
- Recommend normal physical activity; avoid bed rest.
- Offer supervised exercise therapy in addition to usual care.
- Offer manual therapy in addition to usual care; no clinically relevant difference between the two.
- Directional-preference or motor-control exercise may be offered; neither is superior.
- Routine acupuncture not supported.
- Manipulation: moderate evidence in acute radiculopathy, low in chronic (Leininger 2011). Neural mobilisation: low evidence as an adjunct. Spinal mobilisation with leg movement: moderate evidence vs neural mobilisation, exercise or TENS alone (Satpute 2019).
Management thresholds: Persistent, debilitating pain not responding to conservative care → refer to orthopaedic surgeon within 12 weeks. Extraforaminal glucocorticoid injection: very low, short-term effect — only after due consideration.
Engine notes: Already represented in the low-back prototype as the radicular/sciatica entry. Use for neuropathic-pattern wording (“can be associated with nerve irritation”), the stay-active/no-bed-rest home-care line, and a 12-week “not improving” escalation prompt. Cross-check against current NICE NG59 and the NASS guideline update.

### 3.2 Spondylolysis and spondylolisthesis
Definition: Spondylolysis: a defect/stress fracture of the pars interarticularis (L5 most often, then L4), uni- or bilateral. With a bilateral defect, the sacral-base angle can allow forward slip of the vertebra = spondylolisthesis.
Classification (Wiltse 1976): 
- Type I Dysplastic — congenital sacral-base/L5 arch lesion; continued slip can compress the cauda equina.
- Type II Isthmic — pars defect: A lytic (fatigue fracture; most common under 50, rare under 5), B elongation without separation, C acute traumatic pars fracture.
- Type III Degenerative — long-standing segmental instability; rarely under 50.
- Type IV Traumatic — acute fracture of a structure other than the pars; severe trauma.
- Type V Pathological — local or systemic bone disease (Paget’s, arthrogryposis, Albers-Schönberg).
- Meyerding grade: 1 = <25% slip, 2 = 26–50%, 3 = 51–75%, 4 = 76–100%; grades 1–2 “low grade”, 3–4 “high grade”.
Etiology: Repetitive flexion/extension loading (e.g. gymnastics); repeated impaction of the inferior articular process into the pars below. Genetic component: occurs in 15–70% of first-degree relatives.
Epidemiology: Spondylolysis 4.6% by age 6; 6% in a selected adult group; 11.5% in an unselected adult population; up to 47% of athletic adolescents with low back pain. Male:female — spondylolysis 3:1, isthmic 2:1, degenerative 1:3.
Presentation: 
- Spondylolysis: low back pain, worse with extension, possible palpable step.
- Spondylolisthesis: low back pain ± radiculopathy ± neurogenic claudication (relieved by rest/flexion); symptoms often worsen through the day; bowel/bladder change with severe stenosis; degenerative type usually >50. Moller: LBP with sciatica 62%, LBP only 31%, sciatica only 7%; lumbosacral tenderness 68%, reduced side-bending 46%, hamstring tightness 22%.
- Objective: shortened trunk, horizontal abdominal creases, flattened lordosis (or hyperlordosis with a large sacral angle), palpable step, segmental tenderness, hip-flexor contracture, tight hamstrings; neuro signs depend on stenosis severity. Isthmic type: extension more provocative than flexion.
- Diagnosis: standing lateral lumbar X-ray (slip may reduce when supine).
Treatment: 
- Conservative management first: 83.9% non-operative success at ≥1 year in children/young adults (Klein 2009); physiotherapy moderately effective for low-grade slips (McNeely 2003).
- Flexion exercises more effective for pain than extension (Sinaki 1989); abdominal strengthening where no neurological/autonomic involvement; specific transversus abdominis + multifidus co-activation progressing to limb loading and function (O’Sullivan 1997); general strengthening, stretching, motor control (Hardwick 2012).
- Surgery considered when radicular/claudication symptoms persist >12 weeks with imaging evidence of stenosis/degenerative slip (SPORT trial, Abdu 2018).
Engine notes: Not yet in the engine. Suggests a new low-back entry keyed to extension-provoked pain in an adolescent/young athlete (gated by age and sport/onset chips), routed to physio with a flexion-biased home-care line and an X-ray-via-doctor prompt where pain is persistent and focal. Degenerative slip overlaps the stenosis entry. Patient copy must avoid “slipped/unstable vertebra” framing — describe as a stress reaction/forward shift that is usually managed well without surgery.

### 3.3 Lumbar spinal stenosis
Definition: Narrowing of the central canal, lateral recess and/or intervertebral foramen. Chief complaints are pain, paraesthesia and/or weakness into the legs, usually bilateral, from neurogenic claudication (compression and/or vascular compromise of neural elements).
Causes: Congenital/developmental (small canal) or, far more commonly, acquired/degenerative: disc-height loss with bulging, thickened lamina, osteophytes, ligamentum flavum hypertrophy and buckling, hypertrophic facets. Canal and foraminal space increase in flexion (foramen +12%) and decrease in extension (−15%). Associated with degenerative spondylolisthesis and scoliosis.
Epidemiology: Prevalence 9.3%, most in their 50s–60s; slightly more men; uncommon under 50; the most common reason for lumbar spine surgery over 65. L4/5 most affected; foraminal compression most often at L5/S1 (L5 root).
Presentation: 
- Many have imaging stenosis with no symptoms — diagnosis rests on history and examination.
- Low back pain often eased by rest; key feature is neurogenic claudication: uni- but more often bilateral leg pain, paraesthesia, weakness, fatigue; radiates proximal → distal; provoked by standing, walking, walking downhill (extension); relieved by sitting, bending forward, walking uphill; slow to settle once provoked; walking distance shortens progressively.
- Differential — vascular claudication: provoked by exercise regardless of posture, relieved by stopping (not specifically by flexion/sitting), settles faster, calf cramping, reduced pulses ± trophic skin changes; both can coexist.
- Examination: reduced ROM, symmetrical diminished reflexes, SLR usually negative; may need a 30-second standing extension test or walking to provoke symptoms, then re-examine. N-CLASS criteria (Genevay 2018): 6 variables (4 subjective, 2 tests) — specificity 92.1%, sensitivity 80.0%.
- Imaging: X-ray for spondylosis, CT for bony anatomy, MRI gold standard — always correlate clinically.
Treatment: 
- Physiotherapy indicated: flexion stretches, spine/limb mobility, lower-limb strengthening, flexion-biased cardio (cycling, body-weight-supported treadmill), lumbar stabilisation, manual therapy to thoracic/lumbar spine, pelvis and hips (Whitman 2006), education on patho-anatomy and natural history.
- Symptoms often remain stable or improve over time (Benoist 2002). Measure change with self-paced walking distance and Oswestry.
- Comprehensive supervised programmes beat self-directed home exercise for walking distance (Ammendolia 2018; Minetama 2019).
- Surgery vs non-surgical: Cochrane 2016 inconclusive; surgical side effects 10–24%, none reported for conservative care; laminectomy vs exercise show similar effects (Mo 2018). Surgery considered for progressive neurological compromise (weakness, bowel/bladder) or failed conservative care.
Engine notes: Already in the prototype (age-gated >50). Use the walking/standing-vs-sitting pattern as the discriminating chips, add the vascular-claudication differential as a doctor-first cue (calf cramping relieved by stopping, cold/pale feet, smoker/diabetes), and include the “flexion-biased activity like cycling is well tolerated” home-care line. Patient copy: avoid “narrowing/crumbling” fear framing; emphasise that symptoms frequently stay stable or improve and exercise is proven. Cross-check against the 2024 NASS lumbar stenosis update.

### 3.4 Cauda equina syndrome (CES)
Anatomy: Nerve-root bundle below the conus medullaris (ends L1–2).
Causes: Trauma or a space-occupying lesion — tumour, haematoma, lumbar disc herniation (~2% of herniations). A medical emergency and indication for spinal surgery.
Presentation: Severe low back pain with radicular symptoms PLUS saddle paraesthesia and bladder dysfunction (urinary retention first, then overflow incontinence). Bowel dysfunction occurs but is under-reported early; sexual dysfunction appears later.
Examination: Lower-limb sensory loss, weakness and reduced reflexes (as in radiculopathy) plus perianal sensory loss and loss of rectal tone.
Imaging / prognosis: MRI confirms the cause (disc, neoplasm, infection); CT myelography if MRI contraindicated. Recovery depends on the stage at recognition (retention vs incontinence) and time from onset.
Engine notes: Already the low-back emergency route. This chapter supports the current flag wording; keep the patient-language set (numbness around the saddle/genitals, trouble starting or controlling urine, new bowel accidents, both legs weak) and route to 911/ED with no physio prompt. Verify wording against current cauda equina pathway guidance (e.g. UK GIRFT/NHS CES pathway, BC emergency guidance).

### 3.5 Cord compression myelopathy (CCM)
Anatomy / causes: Cord compression within the canal above the cauda equina — most often cervical, also thoracic or upper lumbar. Degenerative change is the usual cause (present in 90% over age 70, though myelopathy itself is less common); also trauma, tumour, infection, disc herniation.
Presentation: Neck/shoulder pain (cervical) or back pain (thoracic/lumbar), radicular pain, weakness or clumsiness distal to the lesion, non-dermatomal paraesthesia, balance or gait difficulty; bowel/bladder dysfunction later.
Examination: Long-tract signs: hyper-reflexia, clonus, Babinski, Hoffman, L’hermitte; non-myotomal weakness, non-dermatomal sensory change, ataxic gait. No single test or cluster is highly sensitive — any positive or unclear neurological finding warrants referral.
Imaging: MRI gold standard for degree of compression; EMG to exclude peripheral nerve dysfunction.
Engine notes: Not yet in the engine as a named entry. Best handled as a GLOBAL doctor-first red-flag cluster across neck, upper-back and low-back regions: new clumsiness of hands or feet, unsteady walking, tingling in both hands/feet in no clear pattern, electric shocks down the spine on neck bending, new bladder change → see a doctor promptly (urgent, not 911 unless rapidly worsening). No physio booking prompt on that screen.

### 4.1 Draft card copy
1 — Non-specific low back pain: See your doctor if you are not clearly better after 4 to 6 weeks, or if the pain is getting worse. See your doctor sooner if the pain is constant and wakes you at night, or if you have a fever, unexplained weight loss, or had a fall onto your back (especially if you are over 50).
2 — Nerve-related leg pain: Go to the emergency department now if you notice numbness around your saddle area or genitals, any new change in bladder or bowel control, or leg pain that spreads to both legs. See a doctor the same day if your leg or foot is getting weaker.
3 — Spinal stenosis: See your doctor if your legs ache or cramp when you walk but ease only when you stop (not specifically when you sit or bend forward), or if your feet are often cold, pale or have sores that are slow to heal. Go to the emergency department now if both legs are weakening or you notice a new change in bladder or bowel control.
4 — Positional disc pain: See a doctor the same day if numbness or weakness is spreading or getting worse. [Backbend advice shown only when answers fit:] Some people find gentle standing back-bends ease this pattern. Stop if the pain or tingling moves further down your leg or gets stronger — that is a sign to see a physiotherapist or doctor rather than keep going.
5 — Inflammatory back pain: If you develop a new painful or red eye, or sudden blurred vision, see a doctor or an eye emergency service the same day.
6 — Young athlete, extension pain (doctor-first): Back pain that comes on with arching or extending your back in sport, and has lasted more than two to three weeks, is worth having checked with an X-ray before you train through it. See your doctor first; a physiotherapist can then help you return to sport safely.
7 — Movement-control line (inside NSLBP card): Some backs feel tired, or seem to “give” a little, after holding one position for a while. This is common and not a sign of damage — control and strength work usually helps.
