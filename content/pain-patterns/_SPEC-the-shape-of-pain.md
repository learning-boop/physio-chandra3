THE PREMISE

## The drawing is data
Distribution independently signals both the pain mechanism and the psychosocial load carried with it — before a single question is answered.
When someone drags a finger across the body to show where it hurts, the shape they leave behind carries information the words often don’t. A tight blot behaves differently from a line running down a limb, and both behave differently from pain scattered in six places. Large studies bear this out: a widespread drawing reliably screens for central sensitisation and psychosocial burden, data-driven analysis of drawings in tens of thousands of spinal-pain patients recovers distinct patterns, and 2025 work confirms that drawings patients capture themselves carry genuine clinical value.
So the engine treats the drawing as the lead hypothesis and the follow-up questions — onset, duration, quality, 24-hour pattern — as the way to confirm or unseat it. A line down the arm could be a trapped nerve or a referred ache from the joint; only the quality answer tells them apart.
THE MECHANISM

## Why pain shows up where it does
Referred pain isn’t mysterious — it’s a wiring feature.
Sensory signals from very different tissues — skin, muscle, joint, and internal organs — funnel onto many of the same neurons in the spinal cord before travelling up to the brain. This is convergence–projection. Because the brain has no dedicated line for each source, it attributes the danger signal to the input it meets most often: the skin and the musculoskeletal surface. A deep or visceral problem therefore gets projected outward onto the body-surface map the patient draws on.
Skin → dermatome  ·  Muscle → myotome  ·  Joint/bone → sclerotome  ·  Organ → viscerotome
This is why a gallbladder can hurt at the shoulder blade, and why deep joint pain spreads into a limb without any nerve being trapped.
ACCURACY CAVEAT — BUILD THIS IN
Real radicular pain does not reliably follow textbook dermatomes. Only the L5 and S1 patterns show even partial correspondence, and cervical patterns vary widely. Flag “nerve” on a looser rule — a line down the limb plus burning / shooting / pins-and-needles / numbness — never on an exact dermatomal match. A strict map would miss most real nerve pain.
THE PATTERN MAP

## Three shapes, and the split that keeps it safe
Localized, radiating, and widespread — but “radiating” must split three ways.
PATTERN 01
Localized
One compact blot over a single region
  PHYSIO-APPROPRIATE  
A tight, single-region mark that arrived with an activity and eases or worsens with movement points to a nociceptive / mechanical source — the local joint, muscle, or tendon. The reassuring default, but not a free pass.
Localized does not automatically mean simple. A small mark that is constant, dominates at night, or is out of proportion to any injury is not a routine sprain — proportionality and 24-hour pattern re-open the question.
Confirm with: onset (activity-related) · quality (mechanical ache) · eases with rest/position
PATTERN 02 · SPLITS ×3
Radiating / linear down a limb
A line or streak travelling along an arm or leg
  ROUTE DEPENDS ON QUALITY  
Three very different problems all draw as “a line down the limb.” The quality answer and the presence of nerve symptoms decide which.
2a · Neuropathic / radicular     PHYSIO · NERVE-FLAGGED  
Follows a nerve line (neck→thumb, buttock→calf→foot) and burns, shoots, or comes with pins-and-needles, numbness, or weakness. True nerve pain — radiculopathy or entrapment.
2b · Somatic referred     PHYSIO-APPROPRIATE  
A deep dull ache spreading from a joint into the proximal limb, not tracking a clean nerve line, rarely past the elbow or knee, no pins-and-needles. Still nociceptive — managed like Pattern 01. The classic tool error is mislabelling this as “nerve pain.”
2c · Visceral referred     DOCTOR / EMERGENCY  
Matches an organ map — left arm/jaw/central chest, shoulder tip, right shoulder blade, flank→groin — not brought on by movement or pressing, and may carry nausea, sweating, breathlessness, or fever. Routes to care, never physio.
The fork: nerve quality → 2a  ·  dull ache, no neuro signs → 2b  ·  organ map + not mechanical → 2c
PATTERN 03
Widespread / multiple / non-anatomical
Many separate regions, both sides, migratory, “everywhere”
  PHYSIO + PAIN-SCIENCE  
Pain marked in many places that don’t respect one nerve or one joint is, itself, a recognised signal of nociplastic pain / central sensitisation with a strong psychosocial association — formalised in IASP’s 2021 criteria and applied in a 2025 field study.
Here the tool stops chasing “which structure.” It shifts to the alarm-system / “sensitivity can wind back down” explainer and routes to Chandra, nudging toward a GP if systemic features appear. Because central sensitisation is a mechanism, not a measurable diagnosis, it surfaces this as a pattern possibility only — never “central sensitisation” or a psychological label on the person.
Confirm with: duration (months+) · constant / non-mechanical · disproportion to any injury

## Front, back, and the midline
A 3D model captures which surface and which side the patient marked. Both change the differential — sometimes from physio to emergency.
Because the model rotates, the engine knows whether a mark sits on the front or the back of a region, and whether it’s left, right, or across the midline. At several regions the same spot on opposite surfaces points to different tissues, and side can be the difference between a muscle and an organ.

NEW IN V3 · CONTEXT OVERLAYS

## Three contexts that reweight everything
The same drawing means different things after surgery, in a child, or in an older adult. Each overlay shifts the tool toward caution.
After surgery or injury     WATCH THE SITE  
Pain at a recent surgical or injury site, easing week on week, is expected nociceptive healing → reassure + gentle movement. Pain that spreads beyond the site, burns, or brings pins-and-needles suggests nerve involvement (persistent post-surgical pain) → nerve-flag + physio. Red, hot, swollen, fever, or wound discharge → doctor same day (infection). One question — “recent surgery or procedure here?” — switches this overlay on.
Children & teens     EXTRA CONSERVATIVE  
Bilateral leg aches at night with a normal day and normal walking are often benign “growing pains.” But be deliberately cautious with under-18s: a persistent focal pain, a limp, refusal to use a limb, night pain that wakes them, swelling, or any systemic feature → route to a doctor, not a physio booking. Never reassure a child’s focal bony pain away online.
Older adults     HIGHER PRETEST RISK  
Osteoarthritis and mechanical pain are common and very treatable — but a new pain in an older adult carries a higher baseline chance of serious causes. New unexplained focal bone pain, night pain, unexplained weight loss, or a fall onto bone (fragility fracture) raise the threshold to see a doctor first. Age is a weight on the safety side of the scale, not a diagnosis.
CROSS-CUTTING DIMENSIONS

## Four questions the shape can’t answer alone
Distribution names the mechanism; these overlays decide how confident, how urgent, and how the message is pitched.
01   Proportionality — the master gate
Does the pain and disability exceed what the drawn problem should cause? Disproportion is the single strongest pointer toward nociplastic pain and yellow flags. When proportion and shape disagree, proportion wins.
02   Time changes the meaning of the same shape
Widespread at three days ≠ widespread at three years. And direction matters: pain that centralises (retreats toward the spine) is a good sign; pain that peripheralises, marching down the limb, is the opposite.
03   Intensity & density, not just area
A hot core with a faint halo reads differently from a solid dense block. Digital manikins quantify pain extent, region count, and intensity — the draw tool can capture the same.
04   Laterality & the midline
One-sided limb pain leans peripheral/radicular; symmetry leans systemic/nociplastic; a single blob crossing the midline non-anatomically is a nociplastic clue in itself.
THE HONEST REALITY — MIXED PATTERNS
Real people rarely sit in one box. Most presentations have a dominant mechanism plus others riding along. This is now explicit in the IASP descriptors themselves — they overlap and evolve and cannot yet be cleanly discriminated. The engine names the dominant pattern to shape the message, but should acknowledge overlap and never force a single tidy label. That humility is the clinical reasoning.
NEW IN V4 · THE NEUROSCIENCE UNDERNEATH

## The science behind the message
Why the tool can reassure honestly. Three findings from the JOSPT Pain Science in Practice series turn our patient messaging from assertion into mechanism — and set the limits on what we may claim.
01   Nociception is not pain
Nociception is the nervous system encoding possible danger; pain is the conscious experience the brain then produces. They usually travel together but come apart — pain can exist with little nociception, and nociception need not produce pain. Two rules follow directly: a person’s pain is always real (never “in your head”), and hurt does not reliably mean harm. This is the evidence under every reassurance line.
02   “Sensitisation” and the pain-types are mechanisms, not diagnoses
Peripheral and central sensitisation are neurophysiological terms — central sensitisation cannot even be measured directly in people, and is only one of many coexisting mechanisms, never “the cause” of chronic pain. The three descriptors (nociceptive / neuropathic / nociplastic) overlap, cannot yet be cleanly told apart, and shift over time — a fresh tissue injury can grow nociplastic features. Neuropathic in particular requires a demonstrable nerve lesion; symptoms alone don’t earn the label. This is why the tool says a pattern can be associated with nerve irritation or a more sensitive alarm system, and never “you have central sensitisation” or “you have neuropathy.”
03   The body has its own pain-control system — and you can turn it up
A descending pain-modulatory system continuously dials nociception up or down. A single bout of exercise raises most people’s pain tolerance (exercise-induced hypoalgesia); regular activity strengthens the system over time, while inactivity lets the pain-amplifying side dominate. Expectation, understanding, and a trusted clinician measurably reduce pain — placebo analgesia is a real, opioid-mediated effect. This is the mechanism under “motion is lotion” and “sensitivity can wind back down,” and the reason routing to Physio Chandra — a clear plan from a trusted expert — is itself part of the treatment.
THE LIMITS — APPLY IT PROPORTIONATELY
Pain-mechanism language describes persisting pain and is a clinician-reasoning tool. For a fresh, proportionate injury, keep it simple — heavy pain-neuroscience is off-putting and unnecessary. The patient-facing tool always translates this into plain, non-threatening language: the science justifies the reassurance; it is never the script.

## The words patients actually type
Each pattern → a cluster of real search phrases → one page, with the two-sentence direct answer an AI assistant lifts.
Localized — e.g. shoulder      /pain/shoulder
Searches:  shoulder pain when lifting arm  ·  sharp pain in shoulder  ·  rotator cuff pain  ·  shoulder hurts to reach overhead  ·  shoulder pain no injury
DIRECT ANSWER (AI-QUOTABLE)
Pain felt in one spot on the shoulder that worsens when you lift or reach is most often a local muscle, tendon, or joint problem such as rotator-cuff irritation. It is common, rarely serious, and usually responds well to guided physiotherapy.
Neuropathic / radicular — sciatica      /conditions/sciatica
Searches:  pain going down my leg  ·  sciatica symptoms  ·  tingling down leg to foot  ·  shooting pain from back to leg  ·  numbness in leg and back pain
DIRECT ANSWER (AI-QUOTABLE)
Pain that travels from the low back or buttock down the leg, often with tingling or numbness, is commonly called sciatica and usually means a spinal nerve is being irritated. Most cases settle over weeks, and physiotherapy can calm the nerve and restore movement.
Widespread / nociplastic      /conditions/widespread-pain
Searches:  pain all over my body  ·  aching everywhere and tired  ·  widespread pain no cause  ·  why does my whole body hurt  ·  fibromyalgia type pain
DIRECT ANSWER (AI-QUOTABLE)
Pain felt in many parts of the body at once often reflects a nervous system that has become more sensitive — turning the volume up on pain — rather than many separate injuries. This sensitivity can be turned back down, and an individualised physiotherapy plan focused on gentle activity and pain education is a proven approach.
Understanding pain — the science hub      /understanding-pain
Searches:  does hurt mean harm  ·  can pain exist without injury  ·  why does exercise help pain  ·  is my pain real  ·  why does my pain move around
DIRECT ANSWER (AI-QUOTABLE)
Pain is produced by the brain as a protective signal, not a direct readout of tissue damage — so hurting does not always mean harm, and pain can persist after an injury has healed. The nervous system can become more sensitive and, importantly, can also settle back down, which is why gentle movement, understanding your pain, and a clear plan with a physiotherapist are effective.

ANSWER-ENGINE BLOCKS

## FAQs written for the way AI quotes
Real patient questions, answered in two or three sentences. These become the FAQPage schema. (Sample — sciatica page.)
Q.  Is sciatica serious?
Most sciatica is not dangerous and improves over a few weeks with movement and physiotherapy. Seek urgent care if you develop numbness around the groin or saddle area, difficulty controlling your bladder or bowel, or new weakness in both legs — these are rare emergencies.
Q.  How long does sciatica take to go away?
Many people notice meaningful improvement within four to six weeks, and the majority settle within three months. Staying gently active — rather than resting in bed — tends to speed recovery.
Q.  Should I rest or keep moving with sciatica?
Gentle, regular movement is usually better than rest. Prolonged bed rest tends to stiffen and weaken the area and can slow recovery. A physiotherapist can show you which movements calm your symptoms.
Q.  What does sciatica feel like?
Typically a sharp, shooting, or burning pain running from the low back or buttock down the back of the leg, sometimes to the foot, often with pins-and-needles or numbness. Pain that stays only in the back, without travelling down the leg, is usually something different.
Q.  Can a physiotherapist in Surrey or Burnaby help with sciatica?
Yes — a physiotherapy assessment can identify what’s irritating the nerve and build a plan to calm it and restore movement. Physio Chandra offers individualised assessment in Surrey and Burnaby, BC. This information is educational and not a diagnosis.

BEFORE PUBLISHING

## The CHCPBC compliance pass
Every patient-facing page and tool screen clears this checklist. It protects patients and keeps the marketing within the BC standard.
✓  Possibilities, not diagnosis.  “Pain here can be associated with…”, never “you have…”. A persistent educational-only disclaimer on the tool and every page.
✓  Red-flag routing before any booking prompt.  Emergency and doctor-first patterns show “seek care” messaging instead of a physio CTA — never alongside it.
✓  No superlatives or guarantees.  No “best physio in Surrey,” no “guaranteed relief.” Use “evidence-informed,” “individualised assessment.”
✓  No free-consultation offers.  BC treats these as inducements. “Book an assessment” is the CTA.
✓  No solicited testimonials.  Let reviews accumulate organically; don’t build a review-collection campaign into the pages.
✓  Named, dated authorship.  “Written & reviewed by Chandra Matla, Registered Physiotherapist · reviewed Sep 2026” on every condition page.
✓  Language audit.  No “slipped disc,” “bone on bone,” or “wear and tear = damage.” Pair every cause with a hurt-≠-harm, movement-positive line.
