## blurb
Overload of the inner shin-bone lining from a jump in running volume, harder surfaces, or worn shoes — pain starts during exercise and can become persistent if pushed through.

## noticed
- Aching along the inner edge of the shin bone
- Starts during running, eases with rest, returns with activity
- Tender to press along several centimetres of the inner shin

## homeCare
- Cut running volume/intensity temporarily — swap in cycling or swimming
- Check shoe age and surface; increase training gradually (≤10%/week) when returning
- Calf strengthening and gradual reloading

## seePhysioIf
- Shin pain persists despite 2 weeks of reduced load
- Pain is becoming sharper and more focal (needs a stress-fracture check)
- You want a running load and biomechanics review
- Pain in one small spot on the shin bone that you can cover with a fingertip, pain when you hop, or pain at rest or at night: stop running and see a doctor, as a stress fracture needs ruling out

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check your shins and explain why they hurt when you run.
- Calf strengthening and a short cut-back in running can help the shins settle.
- You get a gradual return-to-running plan, with tips on shoes, surfaces and training load.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 4 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Diagnosis (Winters 2020; NHS GGC): exercise-induced pain along the posteromedial tibial border, usually the distal two-thirds, with recognisable palpation pain over ≥5 consecutive cm. Inter-rater reliability of a history and exam diagnosis k = 0.89 (95% CI 0.74–1.00) (Winters 2018). Differentiate from tibial stress fracture (CPA Theory Ch 2.8 pp. 552–557): local bony tenderness along the shaft is the key BSI feature; jumping, hopping and running provoke; shin splints are differentiated with resisted and length-tension tests of the lower-extremity muscles (p. 554). Focal tenderness <5 cm → treat as BSI until ruled out (NHS GGC). Posteromedial tibia = low-risk BSI site; anterior tibial cortex = high-risk (Table 2, p. 555). Hop test (Hoenig 2025 Delphi text, statements not fully verified): in one study of 80 adolescents with tibial BSI the vertical single-leg hop test had the highest sensitivity; no single test was highly sensitive or specific, and combining tests improved accuracy. MRI is the imaging modality of choice for BSI. Risk factors: higher BMI, greater navicular drop, greater ankle PF ROM and greater hip lateral rotation ROM (CPA Theory p. 553, citing Hamstra-Wright, whose 2015 meta-analysis is on MTSS); female sex, previous MTSS, orthotic use and less running experience (NHS GGC). Pronated foot posture is a risk factor (Neal 2014; MP 485, 546) – FPI-6 (Table 7-6, MP 522), navicular drop (MP 524). Muscle tests (CPA): resisted tibialis posterior (PF + inversion), soleus (prone, knee 90°) and gastrocnemius (MP 497); length – DF knee extended vs knee flexed ≥20° (soleus), tibialis posterior DF + eversion (MP 506); lunge test (Table 7-1, MP 488). MTSS differential diagnosis is a Level 3 workshop topic (MP 549). CECS (NHS GGC): exercise-induced, often bilateral, relieved by rest; may include paraesthesia, pallor or cold skin; confirmed by compartment pressure testing. Check pulses. Ultrasound not recommended (common incidental findings). Recovery (NHS GGC): often 90–120 days, especially with repeat episodes; mean ~89 days in women vs ~64 days in men; recovery defined as completing an 18-minute run with pain <4/10. Refer to orthopaedics after 3–6 months of failed conservative care.
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 5 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Key finding: local bony tenderness along the tibial shaft; less often redness, swelling and warmth; jumping, hopping or running provoke symptoms. Include gait and a targeted hip, knee, foot and ankle assessment (Theory p. 554; Barrack 2014). BSI continuum (Theory Fig 1, p. 552): accumulation of micro-damage → crack initiation → crack propagation, with depressed remodelling, continued stress or inadequate repair; a 'stress reaction' = increased remodelling with periosteal and/or marrow oedema. Fatigue (repetitive load beyond threshold) vs insufficiency (normal load on abnormal bone) (p. 553). Imaging (Theory p. 555): X-ray usually negative early (later periosteal thickening, discrete fracture, callus); MRI has the highest sensitivity and specificity (most expensive). Kaeding–Miller (Table 1): I asymptomatic stress reaction; II symptomatic stress reaction; III non-displaced fracture; IV displaced fracture; V nonunion. Site risk (Theory Table 2, p. 555, developed for runners): posteromedial tibia and fibula/lateral malleolus = low risk; anterior cortex of the tibia and medial malleolus = high risk. Highly acute or high-risk: NWB + immobilisation 4–6 weeks (Warden 2014; p. 556) with medical/orthopaedic review. Low-risk: activity modification is key; pain during or after activity = load beyond the bone's capacity → modify further (p. 556). Risk factors (Theory pp. 553–554): training errors, hard surfaces, footwear change or wear, prior stress fracture, female sex (Wright 2015); amenorrhoea + low bone mass + low energy availability (Barrack 2014); insufficiency causes – metabolic bone disease, endocrinopathy, chronic renal disease, post-radiation, smoking, infection. Runner factors (higher BMI, greater navicular drop, more ankle PF ROM, more hip lateral rotation ROM) are cited to Hamstra-Wright, whose listed reference is an MTSS meta-analysis – use with caution. DDx: shin splints (MTSS) – differentiate with resisted and length-tension tests of the lower-limb muscles (Theory p. 554); MTSS DDx is an L3 workshop topic and a pronated foot is an MTSS risk factor (Neal 2014; MP 485, 549). Compartment syndrome is listed by name only (MP 531). External (Schneiders 2012 SR): clinical tests are not standalone – therapeutic ultrasound pooled Sn 64%, Sp 63%, +LR 2.1, −LR 0.3; tuning fork Sn 35–92%, Sp 19–83%, +LR 0.6–3.0, −LR 0.4–1.6 → imaging confirms the diagnosis. International Delphi consensus on BSI in athletes (Hoenig 2025) provides a framework for diagnosis, management and return to sport – see 9a.
