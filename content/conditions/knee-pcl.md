## blurb
This ligament sits deep at the back of the knee and can be strained by a hard blow to the front of the shin (such as hitting a car dashboard), a fall onto a bent knee, or the knee being forced backwards. It is often subtle at first.

## noticed
- A deep ache at the back of the knee, especially when kneeling, going downstairs, or slowing down quickly
- The injury often followed a direct hit to the shin, a fall onto a bent knee, or the knee being over-straightened
- Some swelling and stiffness, and the knee may give way at times

## homeCare
- Avoid kneeling or twisting on the knee while it recovers
- Use ice and gentle elevation to manage early swelling, and keep the knee moving within comfort
- Thigh (quadriceps) strengthening helps support the knee; a physiotherapist can guide it

## seePhysioIf
- The knee swelled within 2 hours of the injury, you could not take 4 steps, or cannot bend it to a right angle: see a doctor the same day
- Your foot is cold, pale or numb, or the knee looks out of shape: go to the emergency department now
- Deep knee pain or swelling continues beyond a couple of weeks
- The knee feels like it shifts or gives way during normal daily movement
- After a car or motorbike crash or a fall from a height, or the knee feels loose in more than one direction: see a doctor the same day, as other ligaments may be hurt too

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check your knee and the other ligaments, and explain the injury plainly.
- A thigh-focused strengthening programme, with brace advice if needed, can help support the knee as it heals.
- You get a step-by-step plan to get back to daily life and sport.

## clinicNotes
- Knee document form 6 (Lower Quadrant Clinical Manual pp. 383-385, 405, 424-426, checked): seldom a pop, may carry on playing; up to 95% combined (Pache 2018), often posterolateral corner. Posterior drawer (Logerstedt) sens 90, spec 99, +LR 90; posterior sag sens 57-91, spec 85-100; quadriceps active test spec 96-100; no test enough alone (Kopkow 2013). Grade by posterior translation vs the other side: I 0-5 mm, II 6-10, III over 10. Isolated I-II: brace 2 weeks, progressive weight bearing, early quadriceps, return to sport 2-4 weeks; III: extension brace 2-4 weeks, return after 3 months. Phases (Wang 2018, Table 6-8): protective 1-6 weeks (avoid hyperextension and posterior translation; CKC to 70 degrees), transitional 6-12, functional 12-16, return 16-24 weeks. The injury screen asks after a crash (I1 vehicle, I10).
- Mechanism (AIM manual pp.61-62): dashboard injury (posterior force to the anterior tibia with the knee flexed), a fall onto the flexed knee with the foot plantarflexed, or hyperextension. Often subtle ("slight oedema, slight discomfort, slight stiffness"). Most are aged 20-29; up to 95% occur with other ligament injuries (posterolateral corner, ACL, MCL): check them.
- Tests: posterior drawer sens 90%, spec 99%; supine internal rotation test 95.5% / 97.1%; posterior sag; MRI close to 100% when acute (AIM manual).
- Management: isolated grade I-II PCL injuries are managed without surgery (quadriceps-dominant rehabilitation, a dynamic brace); return to sport after about 3 months with a dynamic brace (LaPrade, p.62). Combined or grade III injuries: orthopaedic opinion.
- Sources: AIM Theory Manual 2023 Ch. 2.7 pp.61-62; Logerstedt DS et al., JOSPT 2017 (ligament sprain CPG).
