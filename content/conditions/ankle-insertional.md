## blurb
Where the Achilles tendon attaches to the heel bone, the tendon, a small fluid sac, and sometimes a bony bump can become irritated. It is sore at the back of the heel, stiff for the first steps in the morning, and worse with shoes that press on it and with uphill running. It is treated a little differently from pain higher up the tendon.

## noticed
- Pain right at the back of the heel, where the tendon attaches
- Stiff and sore for the first steps in the morning
- Worse with shoes pressing on it, and with hills or stairs

## homeCare
- Wear shoes with a soft or low heel counter, or a small heel raise
- Avoid stretching the calf hard over a step while it is sore
- Ease off hills and stairs for a while, and build back gradually
- Gentle calf raises on flat ground, not letting the heel drop below the level of a step

## seePhysioIf
- A snap or a kick feeling at the back of the ankle, and you cannot rise onto your toes on that leg: see a doctor the same day
- You recently took a quinolone antibiotic (such as ciprofloxacin) or steroid tablets and now have Achilles pain: tell your doctor or the prescriber now
- Pain in both heels, with back stiffness in the mornings, psoriasis, sore red eyes or other swollen joints: see your doctor
- The back of the heel is hot, red and swollen, or you have a fever: see a doctor the same day
- It has not settled after 2 to 3 weeks of changes
- It is limiting walking or running
- You want a graded tendon-loading plan

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check the back of your heel and explain what is irritated there.
- Heel lifts, footwear changes and a graded tendon-strengthening programme can help ease the pain.
- You get a plan to build back walking, running and hills without stirring it up.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 11 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Clinical diagnosis (CPG 2024): localised midportion pain, load-provoked pain, palpation pain and tendon thickening; Royal London test and painful arc sign. Imaging is selective. Hutchison et al. (n = 21, midportion AT; via CPG JOSPT 2018): self-reported pain 2–6 cm proximal to the insertion, extending to the calcaneus, Sn 84%, Sp 73%; pain on midportion palpation Sn 78%, Sp 77% – 'accurate and reliable' (Theory p. 518). Insertional AT: TOP over the distal 2 cm of the tendon and posterior calcaneus; often diagnosed when the tendon is >6 mm thick; aggravated by prolonged rest, pain increases with activity (Chimenti 2017; Theory p. 517). Kvist 1991: 20–25% insertional, 66% non-insertional (Theory p. 516). Rule out rupture: Thompson test (MP 507; Table 7-4, Schwieterman 2013: about 96% sensitive and 93% specific in Maffulli 1998 (the form's "Sn 29–76%, Sp 96–100%, +LR 3.3–43.1, −LR 0.3–0.8" are the knee manual's quadriceps active test figures, corrected 26 Oct 2026)); the ankle may plantarflex with a torn tendon if the sheath is intact, so also assess single-leg heel raise. Posterior ankle DDx (CPG 2018; Theory p. 517): acute rupture, partial tear, retrocalcaneal bursitis, posterior impingement, sural nerve irritation/neuroma, os trigonum, accessory soleus strain, tendon ossification, systemic inflammatory disease, plantaris, fascial tears, insertional AT. Risk factors (Young 2007; Theory p. 516): intrinsic – age, sex, overweight, hyper/hypo-pronation, hindfoot/forefoot varus/valgus, LLD, reduced flexibility, laxity, weakness; extrinsic – corticosteroids, fluoroquinolones, anabolic steroids, training error, surface, excessive load, fatigue. Check DF knee straight and bent (MP 485–486), lunge test (MP 487–488; Table 7-1) and calf strength (MP 497). Outcomes: VISA-A ('only valid and reliable' tool for midportion AT; Murphy 2019; Theory p. 521; MP 482); FAAM or LEFS; heel-raise endurance and hop tests; Limb Symmetry Index for heel-raise reps and height (Silbernagel 2010). Imaging: US and MRI correlate only moderately with clinical status; normalisation of tendon appearance is not needed for clinical improvement (Rio 2018; Theory p. 518).
- Insertional pain is outside the 2024 midportion Achilles CPG revision. Loading without end-range dorsiflexion (not below level; Jonsson 2008; Chimenti 2019); heel lifts; shockwave as an add-on (low to moderate evidence). The Royal London and arc signs are for midportion pain only. Haglund's deformity and retrocalcaneal bursitis are included in this card.
- Screen for spondyloarthritis (enthesitis), recent fluoroquinolone or steroid use, and calcaneal stress fracture (squeeze test; af-stress and ft-stress).
