## blurb
A sprain can also bruise or damage the cartilage and bone on top of the ankle bone. Weeks after the sprain, when the ligaments should have settled, the ankle keeps aching deep inside, swells after activity, and may catch or lock. This needs assessment and often imaging to confirm.

## noticed
- A deep ache inside the ankle, weeks or months after a sprain
- Swelling after activity
- Sometimes catching or locking

## homeCare
- Ease off the running and jumping that bring on the swelling
- Keep the ankle moving, and use cycling or swimming to stay fit
- Note what brings on the ache, to discuss at your assessment

## seePhysioIf
- The ache or swelling is still there 6 weeks after a sprain
- The ankle catches or locks
- You cannot get back to sport
- Still aching deep inside, swelling or catching 6 weeks after a sprain: your doctor may arrange an X-ray and then an MRI, and we can help with that referral
- The ankle locks and will not free up: see a doctor the same day

## physioHelps

- A physiotherapist can check your ankle and arrange imaging or a surgeon's opinion if needed.
- A paced programme of movement, strength and balance can help ease the ache and swelling.
- You get a step-by-step plan back to activity that respects how the joint surface is healing.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 19 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): OCL (Theory p. 538): lesion of articular cartilage and subchondral bone from trauma, repeated trauma or a genetic cause, eventually detaching a fragment; considerable WB pain limiting sport, walking and ADL; ↓ function and ROM, occasionally catching or locking and oedema. Talus (p. 539): most frequent in the 20s–30s; generally a history of trauma; posteromedial and anterolateral sites most frequent. Frequency: 6.5% of all ankle sprains, possibly under-diagnosed – masked by more serious concomitant injuries or radiographic limits (Theory p. 539); 'up to 14% of patients' in the complex ankle sprain section (Meehan 2017, p. 526) – the two CPA figures differ. Presentation (Theory pp. 526, 539): persistent chronic ankle pain after a sprain, localised at the lesion site, lesion palpable; recurring oedema, weakness, stiffness and catching. Medial dome lesion → tenderness posterior to the medial malleolus; lateral dome → anterior to the lateral malleolus; unstable fragments → intra-articular loose bodies (pain, locking, crepitus, swelling). Imaging (Theory p. 538): difficult to detect on conventional radiography; MRI confirms the diagnosis, stages it (6 stages), contributes to differential diagnosis, detects OCLs early and correlates with arthroscopy. MRI revealed associated osteochondral lesions after a simple ankle sprain (Großterlinden 2016; MP 503). Screening and exam (MP 482–497): Ottawa Ankle Rules after acute injury (adults Sn 93%–100%, Sp 49%; children under 15 Sn 80%–98%, Sp 40%–53%; MP 483); TC joint compression stresses cartilage and subchondral bone (MP 496); TC traction (MP 495); lunge test (Table 7-1: intra-rater ICC 0.65–0.99, inter-rater 0.80–0.99); anterior drawer and medial talar tilt (Table 7-2); syndesmosis cluster (Table 7-3); single-leg balance eyes open ≥30 s (MP 486); outcome measures e.g. FAAM, FAOS, LEFS (MP 482). Non-surgical management (Theory p. 539): rest, limitation of intense activity, cast with an initial NWB period, then gradual WB with mobilisation; low-resistance ROM activities (e.g. cycling) may promote cartilage healing; if conservative care fails, surgery. Ankle OCD: stage 1 conservative, otherwise surgical (Zwingmann 2012, p. 538). Advanced talar OCL → orthopaedics, then rehabilitation. Manual Level 2 lists TC and ST osteochondritis dissecans and loose body among treatment-discussion conditions (MP 531); TC loose body manipulation is indicated for restriction from a loose body (MP 536–537) – use only after medical review of the lesion. Rehab: strengthening and proprioceptive progression (MP 541–542, 564–565); cross-training such as aquatic exercise and cycling (MP 565).
- JOSPT lateral ankle sprain CPG 2021: osteochondral lesions and talar bone bruises are listed copathologies of acute sprains (MRI copathology in 92%), and chondral lesions and loose bodies of chronic instability. Medial tibia or talus bone bruise within 2 weeks: longer return to walking (25 vs 16 days) and sport (92 vs 56). Imaging (ACR via the CPG): symptoms over 6 weeks, radiographs; if negative with suspected lesion, impingement or instability, MRI. Refer when symptoms or function do not fully recover. Source: Martin RL et al., JOSPT 2021.
- Incidence and natural history: lesions in about 6.5% of sprains (AIM manual p. 538; p. 526 says up to 14%), usually posteromedial or anterolateral talar dome. Non-operative care: pooled clinical success about 45%; most lesions unchanged on MRI (84%); OA progression about 9% (2023 systematic review, PMC10356662).
- In children and teenagers, osteochondritis dissecans of the talus can start without an injury; conservative care does better than in adults (AIM manual p. 538).
