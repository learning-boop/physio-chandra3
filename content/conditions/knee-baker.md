## blurb
Pain at the back of the knee often comes from a fluid-filled swelling there (a Baker's cyst), which usually forms when the knee joint itself is irritated, for example by arthritis or a meniscus problem. The tendons that cross the back of the knee can also be sore. If the calf becomes swollen, warm, and tender, a doctor needs to check it, because a burst cyst and a clot look the same.

## noticed
- A feeling of fullness or a soft lump at the back of the knee
- Tightness when bending or fully straightening the knee
- Often alongside other knee aches, such as arthritis

## homeCare
- Keep the knee moving gently; avoid long spells of deep bending
- Ease off the activities that make the knee swell
- A cold pack can ease the fullness after activity

## seePhysioIf
- The calf becomes swollen, warm, or tender: see a doctor the same day, even if you know you have a cyst
- The calf is swollen and you are short of breath or have chest pain: call 911
- The lump pulses, or the calf cramps on walking and eases with rest: see a doctor
- The fullness keeps coming back
- Bending or straightening is getting harder

## clinicNotes
- Popliteal cyst: in adults usually secondary to a joint problem (a meniscal tear or OA) through the semimembranosus-gastrocnemius bursa; in children usually a primary cyst that tends to resolve. Confirm with ultrasound when needed. Treat the underlying joint problem (OA or meniscus); aspiration or injection are medical options.
- Differentials: DVT (duplex ultrasound; a cyst and a clot can occur together, so a swollen calf needs an ultrasound even with a known cyst), a ruptured cyst (pseudothrombophlebitis), popliteal artery aneurysm (a pulsing lump; vascular claudication), popliteus or hamstring tendinopathy (pain on resisted knee flexion or tibial internal rotation, local tenderness, no lump), gastrocnemius trigger-point referral.
- Sources: Baker's cyst versus DVT review (PMC8567966); duplex series of cysts with DVT; AIM Theory Manual 2023 Ch. 2.7.
