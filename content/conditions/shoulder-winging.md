## blurb
Sometimes a nerve to one of the muscles that holds the shoulder blade flat against the ribs is stretched, pressed or inflamed, for example after a viral illness, a heavy backpack, an operation near the neck or chest, or a lot of overhead work. The shoulder blade then lifts away from the back, most clearly when you push or lift the arm forward, and the arm tires and feels weak. Nerves often recover by themselves over months, and physiotherapy can help keep the shoulder moving and strong while that happens.

## noticed
- The shoulder blade sticks out, especially pushing on a wall or lifting the arm forward
- Weakness or heaviness lifting the arm forward or overhead
- An ache around the shoulder blade and neck; sometimes it began with a few days of sharp shoulder or neck pain

## homeCare
- Avoid carrying a heavy bag on that shoulder, and limit pushing until it has been checked
- Keep the shoulder moving gently every day
- Note any change in strength or shape, to tell your doctor and physiotherapist

## seePhysioIf
- It started after an operation on your neck, chest or armpit: contact your surgeon or doctor soon
- The weakness is getting worse, or it followed several days of severe pain: see a doctor first
- It isn't clearly improving after 4 to 6 weeks
- You would like a plan to keep the shoulder strong while the nerve recovers

## doctorFirst
If this fits, please see your family doctor in the next few weeks as well as booking physiotherapy: a shoulder blade that sticks out with weakness usually means the nerve to one muscle is not working well, and a doctor can arrange nerve tests.

## physioHelps

- A physiotherapist can explain why the shoulder blade sticks out and how long nerves usually take to recover.
- Exercises can help keep the shoulder moving and strengthen the muscles around it while the nerve recovers.
- You get advice on protecting the shoulder, and a referral for nerve tests if it is not improving.

## clinicNotes
- Medial or inferior-angle winging: serratus anterior (long thoracic nerve); lateral winging with a drooping shoulder and weak shrug: trapezius (spinal accessory nerve, often after neck surgery or node biopsy). Wall push-up, resisted flexion, shrug; cervical and brachial plexus screen; dorsal scapular nerve (rhomboids). EMG/NCS if persistent; recovery 6-24 months. Bilateral winging with gradual weakness elsewhere: muscular dystrophy, medical review. Severe pain for days then weakness: neuralgic amyotrophy (srf-pta). Altered blade movement WITHOUT weakness is scapular dyskinesis (a treatment target, shoulder-rc).
