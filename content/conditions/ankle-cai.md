## blurb
This is when an ankle carries on rolling or giving way, or never feels quite trustworthy, after a sprain that did not fully settle. It is common, and balance and strength training help most people.

## noticed
- A feeling that the ankle might roll or 'give way', especially on uneven ground, turns, or in sports
- Repeated minor sprains or rolls over months or years
- Occasional mild swelling, aching, or weakness after activity, with a general lack of confidence in the ankle

## homeCare
- Practice balance exercises, such as standing on one leg or using a wobble cushion, to retrain the ankle's sense of position
- Build strength in the ankle, outer leg, and hip muscles with simple resistance exercises
- Gradually reintroduce turning, running, or uneven-ground activities as your confidence and control improve
- For sport, a brace or tape helps prevent another sprain while you rebuild balance and strength

## seePhysioIf
- Your ankle keeps giving way or rolling despite weeks of steady home exercise
- You've had several sprains and still don't feel confident or steady walking on uneven surfaces
- The ankle also aches deep inside, catches or locks, or keeps swelling: see us, as other problems inside the joint or a tendon may need checking, sometimes with a scan
- It still keeps rolling after 3 to 6 months of a steady exercise programme: ask us or your doctor about a surgeon's opinion

## physioHelps

- A physiotherapist can check why your ankle keeps giving way and explain it in plain words.
- Balance and strength training, with hands-on work to free up stiffness, can help steady the ankle.
- You get a plan back to sport and uneven ground with more confidence.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 9 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Definition (JOSPT 2021): persistent symptoms ≥12 months after injury; International Ankle Consortium criteria – at least one significant sprain, giving way, recurrent sprain or perceived instability, reduced function (FAAM). Use a discriminative instrument – CAIT (cut-off ≤25) or IdFAI (≥11) – plus functional performance tests (B). CPA list (MP 482): IdFAI, Chronic Ankle Instability Scale (CAIS), Ankle Instability Index (AII), FAAM, FADI, AJFAT, LEFS; SEBT and lunge test. CAI associations (Kobayashi andGamada 2014; MP 500–501, 506, 545): anterior talar position and DF ROM deficit; muscle weakness with ligament laxity; delayed and lower peroneal activation on landing; increased inversion angle; loss of joint reposition sense; reduced static and dynamic postural control; reduced hip add/abd/ext strength; increased inversion with lateral centre of pressure in walking and running; decreased DF in running; more inversion before contact in drop-jump and side hop; decreased knee flexion before contact in vertical jump. Mobility/stability: talar swing test (MP 500–501); lunge test Table 7-1 (MP 488): intra-rater ICC 0.65–0.99, inter-rater 0.80–0.99, MDC 4.7°/1.9 cm intra, 4.5°/1.6 cm inter. Table 7-2 (MP 490): anterior drawer Sn 43–60, Sp 69–87, +LR 1.58–3.84, −LR 0.50–0.76; medial talar tilt Sn 22–78, Sp 47–99, +LR 0.59–27.25, −LR 0.31–1.04; talar tilt positive >23° or 10° side-to-side (Roosen 2013; MP 493). Subtalar instability is hard to distinguish from TC instability; ST valgus stability (medial glide), Hertel grading 0–3 (MP 512–514). Spring ligament WB tolerance test (MP 519). Measures (JOSPT 2021): swelling, ROM, talar translation and inversion, single-leg balance, WB lunge, SLB eyes closed, SEBT anterior/anteromedial/posteromedial/posterolateral (A); hip abd/ext/ER strength (C); timed single-limb hopping (B); FAAM, LEFS, PROMIS (A); TSK-11 and FABQ in CAI (C). CPA: repeated single-leg hopping assesses stabiliser pre-activation (MP 500); SLB eyes open ≥30 s (MP 486). Screen look-alikes: OCL of the talus (6.5% of all ankle sprains; persistent localised pain, recurrent oedema, catching – Theory p. 539); chronic syndesmosis (long-standing pain, instability, recurrent swelling – MP 504); cuboid subluxation, anterolateral impingement (~3% of sprains), peroneal subluxation/tear, sinus tarsi syndrome (Theory pp. 527–528). Treatment (JOSPT 2021): proprioceptive and neuromuscular training (A); graded joint mobilisation, manipulation and MWM to improve WB DF and dynamic balance short term (A); external support not as a stand-alone treatment for balance (B); multiple interventions may supplement balance training (B); bracing plus balance training to prevent further sprains (A); dry needling of the fibularis group with proprioceptive training (C) is listed but left out of patient-facing text until Chandra confirms he offers it. CPA: L3 17-step progression (MP 564–565); hip and trunk strength and coordination (Martin 2013; MP 564); balance/neuromuscular programme ≥3 months (Kaminski 2018; MP 564); talus posterior thrust and WB MWM for DF restriction (MP 535–536, 550). Manual therapy safety: talus anterior thrust lists anterior TC instability (e.g. recurrent sprains) as a safety consideration (MP 552); ST manipulation to restore inversion lists lateral ankle sprain/laxity (MP 538). Refer for orthopaedic opinion when symptoms or function do not recover with a full programme (JOSPT 2021).
- Definition (JOSPT 2021): instability or giving way persisting 12 months or more after the first sprain, with activity limitation; mechanical and/or functional (sensorimotor). About 40% of first-time sprains.
- Discriminative tools (B): CAIT 25 or less (sens 96.6%, spec 86.8%, LR+ 7.31; MCID 3); IdFAI 11 or more; AII 4 or more yes. Functional tests that discriminate: side hop, timed and multiple hop, foot-lift, SEBT (medial, anteromedial, posteromedial).
- Exam (A): weight-bearing lunge, talar translation and inversion, eyes-closed single-leg balance, SEBT; hip abduction, extension and ER strength (C); TSK-11 or FABQ for fear of re-injury (C).
- Copathology to refer if not recovering: fibularis tendon problems, impingement, osteochondral or chondral lesions, synovitis, loose bodies, syndesmosis, arthritis, os trigonum.
- Treatment: proprioceptive and neuromuscular training (A); manual therapy (graded mobilisation or thrust, weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing MWM) for dorsiflexion and balance, short term (A); combine exercise and manual therapy with balance training (B); fibularis dry needling with proprioceptive training (C); brace or tape not as a stand-alone for balance (B). Balance-training responders: SEBT posteromedial reach 85% or less and FAAM-ADL 92.6% or less (70% success). Nonsurgical care for 3 to 6 months before surgery is considered.
- Source: Martin RL et al., JOSPT 2021;51(4):CPG1-CPG80 (lateral ankle sprain revision 2021), grades in brackets.
- International Ankle Consortium definition (Gribble 2013, JOSPT 43:585): a significant sprain at least 12 months ago; at least 2 episodes of giving way in the last 6 months and/or recurrent sprains and/or feelings of instability; plus a questionnaire cut-off (IAC cut-offs differ slightly from the CPG summary, e.g. AII 5 or more "yes"; not re-checked against the full text).
