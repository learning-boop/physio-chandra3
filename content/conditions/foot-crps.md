## blurb
Complex regional pain syndrome is a condition where, after an injury, operation or time in a cast, the limb stays far more painful and sensitive than the injury alone would explain, and the skin changes too: it may look redder or paler, feel warmer or colder, sweat or swell more than the other side, and the hand or foot can become stiff and hard to use. It happens because the body's protective systems (the nerves, blood-flow control and the brain's map of the limb) have become over-protective, not because the original injury has not healed. It is uncommon, it is real, and it is treatable: most cases recognised early improve substantially within the first year.

## doctorFirst
If this fits, please see your family doctor or surgeon within the next few days. CRPS is diagnosed by a doctor, who will also check for other causes such as an infection, a blood clot or a problem with healing. Book physiotherapy straight away alongside: early treatment matters.

## noticed
- It started within days to weeks of an injury, an operation or time in a cast, and has not settled as expected, or has got worse
- Constant burning or deep aching across the whole hand or foot, not just one spot; a cast or bandage can feel far too tight when it is not
- The skin looks redder or paler, feels warmer or colder, or sweats or swells more than the other side
- Light touch from clothes, sheets or the shower hurts
- The hand or foot feels stiff, weak, swollen, strange or "not mine"; nails, hair or skin can change
- Worse letting the limb hang down, with cold or heat, stress or poor sleep; easier when it is raised and supported

## homeCare
- Keep the hand or foot in your daily routine (dressing, washing, eating, light tasks) even if it is slow; avoiding it tends to increase sensitivity and stiffness
- Touch the sensitive skin several times a day with soft materials, moving gradually to rougher textures as it becomes easier
- Keep the limb raised when resting if it is swollen, and keep a cold limb warm
- Look after sleep and stress where you can: both turn the body's protective "volume" up or down

## seePhysioIf
- A hand or foot is still much more painful, swollen, sensitive or discoloured than you would expect a few weeks after an injury, operation or cast: see your doctor and book physiotherapy now rather than waiting
- You would like help with graded movement, desensitisation, and brain-training approaches such as graded motor imagery and mirror therapy
- Any wound that is red, discharging or getting worse, or a fever, needs your doctor the same day

## clinicNotes
- Budapest clinical criteria (Harden 2010): continuing disproportionate pain + at least one symptom in 3 of 4 categories (sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor/oedema, motor/trophic) + at least one sign in 2 of 4 at examination + no better explanation. CRPS I without, CRPS II with a major nerve lesion. Valencia update (Goebel 2021, Pain): "CRPS-NOS" is kept for people never documented as meeting the criteria; people who met them before and now meet only some are "CRPS with remission of some features".
- Measure side to side: skin temperature (infrared thermometer), girth, colour, sweating; allodynia (light touch, brush) and hyperalgesia (pinprick) map; goniometry; grip and pinch or toe strength; tremor and dystonia; left/right judgement accuracy and speed (Recognise) as a GMI baseline; Bath body perception disturbance scale; TSK-11, PCS; sleep.
- Exclude and refer (GP or surgeon): infection, DVT, arterial insufficiency, compartment syndrome, non-union or hardware problems, nerve entrapment; also brachial neuritis, Raynaud's, thoracic outlet syndrome, lymphatic or venous obstruction, small-fibre neuropathy (AIM manual p. 611).
- Epidemiology (AIM manual p. 608): 4-7% after a limb fracture or surgery, fractures 44% of cases; women 3:1; upper limb more often in adults, lower limb 6 times more often in children; older adults after a Colles fracture and immobilisation, younger after a peripheral nerve injury; most resolve within a year, 15% still have symptoms at 2 years; warm (inflammatory) CRPS can become cold (autonomic) CRPS; 74% report the limb feels disconnected.
- Management: early diagnosis and an interdisciplinary team; education (an over-protective nervous system, not ongoing damage); early gentle range and use, normal movement, progressive loading; desensitisation; graded motor imagery (left/right judgement, imagined movement, mirror therapy); elevation and compression for oedema; pain-related fear, sleep, stress, smoking and general exercise; psychology for coping. Cochrane 2022 (Smart): evidence for individual physiotherapy treatments is very uncertain; earlier low-quality support for GMI and mirror therapy; electrotherapy and lymphatic drainage not supported.
- Medication is a physician decision (RCP 2018): early short-course oral steroid, neuropathic agents, bisphosphonates in selected cases; simple analgesics often unhelpful; interventional options.
- Sources: Harden 2010 (Budapest, Pain 150:268); Goebel 2018 (RCP UK guideline, 2nd ed.); Smart 2022 (Cochrane CD010853); AIM Theory Manual 2023 pp. 608-612.
