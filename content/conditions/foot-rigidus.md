## blurb
Arthritis in the big toe joint makes it stiff, especially when bending the toe up to push off, and a bony lump often forms on top of the joint. It is painful when rising onto the toes, walking uphill, or running. Stiff-soled shoes or inserts, and keeping the joint moving, often help.

## noticed
- A stiff big toe that will not bend up well
- Pain pushing off, rising on the toes, or walking uphill
- A bony lump on top of the joint that shoes can rub

## homeCare
- Wear stiffer-soled shoes, or ask about a stiff (carbon) insert or a supportive insole; rocker soles help some people
- Avoid high heels and very flexible shoes
- Gentle big toe movements within comfort
- During exercise, some discomfort (up to about 5 out of 10) is fine if it settles by the next morning; if the joint is worse the next day, ease back a little rather than stopping

## seePhysioIf
- The big toe joint is suddenly hot, red and very painful, or you feel unwell or feverish: see a doctor the same day
- Pain is limiting walking or sport
- The toe is getting stiffer
- You want advice on shoes, insoles, or other options

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check how your big toe moves and explain the arthritis in plain words.
- Joint mobilisation, toe and calf strengthening and a stiff insert or rocker shoe can help ease pain.
- You get a plan to keep walking and staying active while managing flare-ups.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 16 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Definition (Theory p. 530): progressive change of 1st MTP cartilage and bone from mechanical restriction or trauma; OCD may be present. Hallux limitus = restricted sagittal-plane motion (mainly DF/extension); hallux rigidus = absent motion from end-stage joint disease and ankylosis. Functional (extension limited in WB/gait only, full in NWB) vs structural hallux rigidus (Maceira & Monteagudo 2014; MP 525). Epidemiology (Theory p. 530): 46% of women and 32% of men at age 60 (Van Saase 1989); Framingham: 29% of women and 19% of men have foot pain most days of the month (Menz 2006). Risk factors (Reid 1991): trauma (single or repetitive), long 2nd metatarsal (Morton's foot), abnormal gait, first-ray hypermobility, OCD of the 1st MT head, narrow footwear. History and findings (Theory p. 531): 1st ray/MTP pain and swelling, worse with push-off and prolonged walking; high heels aggravate (Dufour 2009); narrow/shallow toe box irritates the joint and dorsal cutaneous nerve; pain may lessen as the condition progresses. ↓ MTP extension; ↑ pronation, plantarflexed forefoot, 1st ray dorsiflexed relative to 2nd (Zammit 2009); ↓ great-toe flexion strength; positive grind test (no accuracy data given); gait – ↓ toe-off with prolonged pronation and no re-supination, or lateral weight shift without return to midline; ↓ push-off strength. Forefoot exam (MP 525–530): standing on toes bilateral/unilateral and toe-off; great toe MTP flexion ~45°, extension ~65°–75°; full 1st MTP extension needs 1st TMT plantarflexion because plantar fascia tension restricts it (Cruz 2016); 1st TMT total ROM 4°–6°; dorsal/plantar and medial/lateral MTP glides; MTP traction and compression (cartilage, subchondral bone); FHL, FHB, EHL, abductor and adductor hallucis length and strength. Level 3 (MP 547–549): normal toe-off needs 1st MTP extension to 70°, mobile sesamoids and balanced extrinsic/intrinsic strength; first ray and hallux functional stability test (single-leg toe rise: pivoting around toes 1–2, ↑ dorsal tendon activity, cannot hold heel lift, ↑ WB on the medial 1st MTP); hallux rigidus impact on gait is part of the regional interdependence workshop – check ankle DF (lunge test, Table 7-1) and midfoot. Imaging (Theory p. 531): early cartilage change not visible on X-ray; later joint-space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis and dorsal joint-line osteophytes. Surgical options (Theory p. 532): dorsal cheilectomy (early; extension pain; shoe-wear irritation; not if passive mid-range pain; 70–90% extension range intra-operatively); Moberg osteotomy (runners/walkers or failed cheilectomy, <40° extension); Keller implant arthroplasty (elderly, low activity; contraindicated with rigid hyperextension deformity; push-off weakness risk); arthrodesis (severe; most common; 70–100% fusion rate; ~15% further joint change).
- Osteoarthritis foundation (content/reference/osteoarthritis.md; "Osteoarthritis.docx" intake v1.0, signed by Chandra 2 Oct 2026; AIM Theory Manual 2023 pp. 572-581): clinical diagnosis without imaging (NICE NG226: 45 or over, activity-related pain, morning stiffness 30 minutes or less); stage early / moderate / advanced. First line at every stage: education, strength and neuromuscular exercise (2-3 times a week, 6-12 weeks), weight management where relevant; manual therapy only alongside exercise. Pain monitoring: up to 5/10 during exercise, back to usual by the next morning. Avoid "wear and tear" and "bone on bone" framing. EULAR 2023 hip and knee update (Moseng 2024): self-management education, reinforced at follow-up. Dry needling: JOSPT hip OA 2025 grade A (short term, trigger points) vs NICE NG226 (do not offer); a shared decision, adjunct only.
- Big toe: rocker sole or a stiff insert; 1st MTP mobilisation and toe and calf strength. A sudden hot, red toe may be gout (gout favours joints with OA, and the two coexist): see a doctor.
- Evidence: carbon-fibre stiffening inserts beat sham (SIMPLE trial, Munteanu 2021: global improvement 61% vs 34%, NNT 4); rocker soles and prefabricated orthoses equally effective, rocker soles with more adverse events (39% vs 16%) and lower adherence (Menz 2016). Grind test; reduced hallux flexion strength; sesamoid mobilisation, FHL strengthening and gait training helped in a small trial (Shamus 2004).
