## blurb
After a torn Achilles, whether it is treated in a boot or cast with the foot pointed down or repaired with surgery, a staged programme helps the tendon heal at the right length and the calf get strong again. Most people get back to walking normally within a few months; strength and sport take longer, often up to a year.

## noticed
- Weakness pushing off and rising onto the toes on that leg
- Stiffness and swelling around the ankle, worse at the end of the day
- A tight calf, and walking that is slower or flat-footed at first

## homeCare
- Follow your treating team's plan for the boot, heel wedges and weight bearing, for as long as you were told
- Avoid stretching the calf hard or standing on a step with the heel dropped until your team says it is safe
- Move the toes and knee often, and keep the leg raised when it swells
- Build up walking a little at a time, as your team allows

## seePhysioIf
- You would like a staged rehabilitation programme that follows your surgeon's or fracture clinic's protocol
- Progress has stalled, or you are not back to the walking, work or sport you want
- A new snap, sudden pain, or loss of push-off during your recovery: see a doctor the same day, as the tendon may have torn again
- After surgery, the wound is red, hot, leaking or opening, or you have a fever: see a doctor the same day
- Your calf becomes swollen, warm or tender, especially in a boot or cast: see a doctor the same day; if you are also short of breath or have chest pain, call 911
- The boot or cast feels too tight, with numbness, tingling, or a cold, pale or blue foot: go to the emergency department

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 26 Oct 2026 (in chat; written from the thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 12) -->
- A physiotherapist can check your walking, ankle movement and calf strength, and follow your treating team's plan.
- A staged programme, from early protected movement to strength and balance, helps the tendon and calf get stronger.
- You get a gradual plan back to walking, work and sport, including the heel-raise and hop tests that show when you are ready.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 12 (26 Oct 2026): a new rupture is doctor first, same day (sudden onset with a "kick" or snap, weak or absent single-leg heel raise, a palpable gap); keep weight off with the foot pointed down. AAOS diagnosis: 2 or more of a positive Thompson test, decreased plantarflexion strength, a palpable gap, increased passive dorsiflexion (via Mittal 2024). The ankle may still plantarflex on the Thompson test with a torn tendon if the sheath is intact, so assess the single-leg heel raise (Lower Quadrant Clinical Manual Ch. 7 p. 507).
- Thompson test (manual Table 7-4; Schwieterman 2013, data from Maffulli 1998, n=161): high sensitivity and specificity, about 96% and 93% in Maffulli. The form's "Sn 29-76%, Sp 96-100%" matches the knee manual's quadriceps active test (PCL, Table 6-7), not the Thompson test: not used.
- After the treatment decision (boot or cast in equinus, or repair): functional rehabilitation following the treating team's protocol; protect from early overlengthening (avoid aggressive dorsiflexion stretching); progressive calf strength, single-leg heel-raise endurance, balance, then running and plyometrics by criteria. Watch for re-rupture (a new snap or loss of push-off), wound problems after surgery, DVT in a boot or cast, and a tight cast.
