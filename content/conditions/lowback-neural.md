## blurb
Nerves run from the low back to the toes and have to slide and stretch every time you bend, sit or straighten your leg. Sometimes a nerve becomes more sensitive to that stretch or to pressure, often after a spell of back pain, a hamstring or ankle injury, or long sitting, and positions that put it on stretch can then bring on pulling, tingling or burning along the leg. A sensitive nerve is usually not a damaged nerve: nerves have a good blood supply and adapt well, and gentle movements that help them glide, with a break from the positions that irritate them, usually help them settle.

## noticed
- Pulling, burning or tingling along a line in the leg; a "stretch" that does not feel like a muscle, and can feel unusual, deep or hard to describe
- Brought on by sitting with the leg straight out, getting into a car, slumping in a soft chair, reaching for your toes, or a hamstring stretch
- Worse when you also drop your head forward or pull your toes up, and easier when you lift your head or bend the knee a little
- It can take a little while to fade after you come out of the position, unlike a muscle stretch that eases straight away
- Sometimes tender to press deep in the buttock, behind the knee, or just below the outer knee

## homeCare
- For now, ease off the positions that bring it on: long sitting with the legs straight, deep slumping, and holding strong hamstring stretches
- Change position often; a short walk every half hour of sitting helps
- Keep the leg moving gently: easy, comfortable movements that do not bring on tingling help the nerve glide
- Do not push into a stretch that brings on tingling or burning; it tends to make a sensitive nerve more sensitive

## seePhysioIf
- Numbness around your saddle area or genitals, new trouble starting or controlling urine, a new bowel accident, or new loss of feeling during sex: go to the emergency department now
- Leg pain or weakness that has started in both legs at once, or weakness that is getting worse day by day: go to the emergency department now
- A foot that has started to slap or drag, or toes that catch: see a doctor the same day
- Numbness that does not go away, or weakness: book promptly; you do not need a referral to see us, and we will involve your doctor if it persists or gets worse
- The pulling, tingling or burning keeps returning, or is affecting sitting, sleep, work or sport: a physiotherapy assessment can check how the nerve is moving and give you a plan

## clinicNotes
- Physio-led. Two questions drive reasoning (as for the arm, content/reference/neural-mechanosensitivity.md): is the nerve SENSITIVE (pain with stretch or pressure) and is it CONDUCTING poorly (numbness, weakness, reflex change)? Then where along the tract (root, lumbosacral plexus, piriformis, hamstrings, fibular head, tarsal tunnel), and more than one site?
- Shacklock's response classes: musculoskeletal (structural differentiation does not change it), normal neurogenic (symmetrical, like normals: posterior thigh to knee, sometimes upper calf; SLR range about 50-100 degrees), abnormal neurogenic OVERT (reproduces the clinical pain) or COVERT (abnormal, does not reproduce it but is relevant). Do not use "positive"; then decide relevance and look for the cause - an abnormal test does not name it.
- Tests (Shacklock; Butler): SLR (differentiate proximal symptoms with dorsiflexion; NOT active neck flexion - the pelvis rotates and gives false results), slump (release neck flexion; contralateral knee extension reduces sensations normally), tibial DF/EV/SLR (calf, heel, sole), fibular PF/IN/SLR (anterolateral leg, dorsum of the foot; sometimes more sensitive than SLR for L4-5), sural DF/IN/SLR (lateral ankle and foot: sprains, S1, cuboid, peroneal tendon). Sensitise with hip adduction and medial rotation, contralateral lateral flexion. Palpate sciatic (between trochanter and ischial tuberosity), tibial (popliteal fossa, tarsal tunnel), common fibular (fibular neck), superficial fibular (its fascial tunnel), deep fibular (between EHL and EDL), sural (lateral to the Achilles); compare sides.
- Exam level (Shacklock): 0 contraindicated (severe pain, rapidly worsening, acute foot drop, CNS pathology); 1 limited for irritable or latent pain, neurological deficit or interface pathology (differentiating movement FIRST as the off switch, to first onset only, once); 2 standard; 3 advanced only when level 2 is normal, the problem stable, conduction normal and demands high. The clinician summary suggests a level from the answers (src/data/neurodynamics.js).
- Categories (Shacklock): interface reduced closing (extension or same-side bend brings on distal symptoms; suspect a space-occupying cause; treat the nerve physiology first with openers, neurological exam before and after), reduced opening (often with tension signs), neural tension dysfunction (tension movements increase, release decreases), sliding dysfunctions (rare; a tension movement may ease it), pathoanatomical and pathophysiological (venous congestion).
- Hamstrings: abnormal slump in 57% of recurrent hamstring strains between episodes (Turl and George 1998) and 76% during an episode (Kornberg and Lew 1989): check the slump after any recurrent strain.
- Management: education; unload the provoking positions, then reintroduce them; sliders first (more hypoalgesic, less tension), progress to tensioners as irritability falls; treat the interface (openers, closers) and innervated tissues; reassess neurodynamics and neurology after each session. Neural mobilisation helps nerve-related low back and leg pain (Basson et al. 2017, JOSPT meta-analysis; newer: a 2023 meta-analysis of 20 RCTs, 877 people with lumbar radiculopathy, large reduction in pain, Hedges g -1.10, alone or with other care - Life 2023;13(12):2255); JOSPT 2021 low back CPG: neural mobilisation as an adjunct for chronic low back pain with leg pain (B).
- Look-alikes: lumbar radicular pain (dermatomal, cough or sneeze, conduction loss; both often coexist - Shacklock's tension dysfunction is common in radicular pain); deep gluteal syndrome (hip guide); hamstring strain or tendinopathy (thigh and hip guides); stenosis (walking, both legs, eases sitting); fibular nerve at the knee and tarsal tunnel (leg and ankle guides); peripheral neuropathy (both feet, stocking pattern: GP); vascular claudication (eases standing still).
- Sources: Shacklock M, Clinical Neurodynamics (Elsevier 2005) and NDS lower-quarter course manual 8th ed 2017-18; Butler DS, The Sensitive Nervous System (2000) and the NOI neurodynamic techniques workbook; Coppieters and Butler 2008; Basson et al. 2017; Turl and George 1998; Kornberg and Lew 1989; Shacklock et al. 2016 (Spine, contralateral slump); George SZ et al., JOSPT 2021.
