## blurb
Just above the ankle, the two bones of the lower leg are held together by strong ligaments. A twist with the foot planted and turning outwards, common in football and rugby, can sprain them. The pain sits higher than an ordinary sprain, at the front between the two bones, and it often takes longer to recover. Some need a surgeon's opinion, so it is worth having it assessed.

## noticed
- Pain at the front of the ankle, just above the joint, between the two leg bones
- Worse pushing off, twisting, or going up on the toes
- Slower to settle than an ordinary rolled ankle

## homeCare
- Protect it early: walk only as comfort allows, with a boot or crutches if needed
- Avoid twisting and pushing off hard while it settles
- Keep the rest of the leg moving and strong

## seePhysioIf
- Have it assessed within the first week, especially if walking is hard or the pain is climbing
- It is limiting walking or sport
- You need a graded plan back to twisting sport
- You could not take 4 steps straight after the injury, or the bone is tender to press: see a doctor for an X-ray first
- The bone on the outside of your leg is tender to press just below the knee: see a doctor the same day for an X-ray
- Your calf becomes swollen, warm or tender, especially in a brace, boot or cast: see a doctor the same day; if you are also short of breath or have chest pain, call 911

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check for a high ankle sprain and arrange a surgeon's opinion if needed.
- Protected walking, then graded strength and balance work, can help the ligaments recover.
- You get a step-by-step return-to-sport plan based on how the ankle is doing.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 8 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Mechanism DF + ER or PF + inversion; incidence ~20% (Delahunt 2018; Theory p. 527); 1–24% of lateral sprains; recovery up to 4× longer than a lateral sprain; pain or dysfunction often seems out of proportion (MP 503). Palpation tenderness most sensitive (0.92); squeeze test most specific (0.88) (Delahunt 2018; Theory p. 527). Recommended combination (Sman, Hiller andRefshauge 2013; MP 503): inability to hop; syndesmosis ligament tenderness; DF-ER test (sensitive); squeeze test (specific); pain out of proportion. Positive = reproduction of symptoms, not perceived laxity. Clinical tests insufficient to detect injury within the first 24 h; use multiple tests to rule out; MRI also revealed associated osteochondral lesions (Großterlinden 2016; MP 503). Table 7-3 vs MRI (MP 504): squeeze – Sman Sn 15–42, Sp 76–94, +LR 0.86–5.39, −LR 0.68–1.04; Großterlinden Sn 44, Sp 56, κ 0.45. DF-ER – Sman Sn 55–83, Sp 49–75, +LR 1.28–2.94, −LR 0.27–0.79; Großterlinden Sn 56, Sp 48, κ 0.40. DF lunge with compression – Sman Sn 53–82, Sp 28–56, +LR 0.86–1.64, −LR 0.41–1.35. ITFJ palpation – Sman Sn 79–97, Sp 18–42, +LR 1.06–1.58, −LR 0.09–0.89; Großterlinden Sn 42, Sp 53, κ 0.61. Inability to hop – Sman Sn 76–96, Sp 18–42, +LR 1.02–1.54, −LR 0.13–1.03. Techniques: DF-ER – crook lying or sitting knee 90°, full passive DF then ER of the talus; note pain location (MP 505). DF lunge with compression – any change with manual ITFJ compression = positive (MP 505). Squeeze above mid-calf; positive = pain near the distal syndesmosis; correlates with extended recovery and heterotopic ossification (Mulligan 2011; MP 495). ITFJ compression: pain may be relieved in a syndesmosis sprain or provoked acutely (MP 494). Fibular translation: high false-positive rate; excess translation only with a severe sprain (MP 488–489). Doctor-first: Ottawa positive (MP 483), unable to weight-bear, or marked swelling with a strongly positive cluster → X-ray/imaging for a possible unstable mortise. JOSPT 2021: syndesmotic injury is a copathology of LAS; imaging usually appropriate when an alignment abnormality suggests syndesmotic injury. Palpate the proximal fibula after a DF/ER injury (a high fibular fracture can accompany syndesmosis injury – clinical consideration, not in the CPA sources). Chronic pattern (Beumer 2002; MP 504): long-standing pain in the syndesmosis region, a sensation of instability and recurrent swelling. Check for an associated deltoid tear (33–35% follow inversion injuries; Theory p. 527) and OCL. Treatment (stable injury): LAS principles (MP 541–544; JOSPT 2021) – external support and progressive WB, structured exercise, balance, at a slower pace. Fibular anterior/posterior glides and TC glides once settled (MP 501–502, 532–533); talus posterior thrust lists transverse tib-fib/PTFL integrity as a safety consideration – defer until the syndesmosis is stable (MP 535–536). Progress loading into end-range DF and ER gradually, as the DF-ER position stresses the syndesmosis restraints (MP 505). L3 stability progression (MP 564–565). Outcomes: FAAM, LEFS, SEBT, lunge test (MP 482).
- JOSPT lateral ankle sprain CPG 2021: syndesmosis injury is a key differential and copathology of acute and persisting sprains; apply the full Ottawa ankle rules first. Imaging (ACR, cited in the CPG): alignment change suggesting syndesmosis injury warrants leg and stress radiographs, MRI or CT; refer with any diastasis concern or failure to settle. (The CPG's "more than 3 weeks of sport" figure refers to severe multi-ligament sprains, not syndesmosis injuries specifically.)
- Tests (Sman 2015, BJSM 49:323; AIM manual p. 527): AITFL (syndesmosis) tenderness most sensitive (92%, LR- 0.28); squeeze test most specific (88%, LR+ 2.15); dorsiflexion-external rotation test sensitivity 71%; unable to hop sensitivity 89%; pain out of proportion specificity 79%. Use a combination, not one test. About 20% of ankle sprains (AIM manual).
- Maisonneuve: palpate the whole fibula after an external rotation injury; proximal fibula tenderness needs a full-length X-ray (injury screen I10).
