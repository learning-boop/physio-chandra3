## blurb
A deep ache in the middle of the back of the wrist, with no lump, can come from a strained ligament between the small wrist bones, a small hidden ganglion, or, less often, a problem with the blood supply of one wrist bone. These are easy to miss, and an early X-ray can look normal, so it is worth having it assessed if it does not settle.

## noticed
- A deep ache in the middle of the back of the wrist
- Worse taking weight through the hand (push-ups, getting up from a chair) or bending the wrist back
- Sometimes a click or clunk, or the wrist gives way when you push up
- A weaker grip
- It may come on slowly over months without an injury, often in younger adults

## homeCare
- Avoid taking full weight on the bent-back wrist for a while; use fists or an angled support
- Keep the fingers and wrist moving within comfort
- A wrist support for heavy tasks can help in the short term
- Avoid push-ups, planks and heavy gripping until it has been checked

## seePhysioIf
- It started with a fall in the last 6 weeks: see a doctor the same day for an X-ray first
- After an older fall, it clicks, clunks or gives way, or it has never been X-rayed: see your doctor within a week for an X-ray, as a torn ligament between the wrist bones is easy to miss and is best found early
- It has not settled after 2 to 3 weeks: see us or your doctor, as an X-ray or MRI may be needed
- Your grip is getting weaker

## physioHelps

- A physiotherapist can check your wrist and help you know if an X-ray or a doctor's review is needed.
- Avoiding weight on the bent-back wrist, and a short-term support, can help it settle.
- A gradual strengthening plan aims to help you get back to weight-bearing and heavy tasks.

## clinicNotes
- Scapholunate injury: often after a fall on the outstretched hand; Watson (scaphoid shift) test of limited value; clenched-fist PA X-ray for widening; MRI or arthroscopy; refer early (Aspetar review).
- Kienböck's: ages about 20-40, dorsal central pain, early X-ray normal: MRI (PMC12301778). Occult dorsal ganglion: ultrasound.
- Form 14 (20 Oct 2026): SL: tenderness just distal to Lister's tubercle, Watson shift, SL ballottement; clenched-fist PA (SL gap) and lateral (SL angle, DISI); dissociative / non-dissociative / combined, dynamic vs static; MRI or arthroscopy; complete or static tears: early hand-surgery referral (repair options narrow with time; untreated SL tears can progress to SLAC wrist, Kitay and Wolfe 2012; clinic note only). Midcarpal (non-dissociative, often lax joints): midcarpal shift / catch-up clunk; proprioceptive and dart-thrower's-plane strengthening, splinting. LT: see the TFCC card. PRWHE. No "instability" in patient text.
- Kienböck's (form 10): lunate tenderness just distal to Lister's tubercle; reduced range and grip; X-ray (ulnar negative variance, sclerosis, collapse; Lichtman stage), MRI for early disease; hand-surgeon referral for staging; splint and activity modification (Lichtman 2017). Imaging goes through the doctor.
- Extensor tendinopathy (form 3; ECRL/ECRB at the 2nd compartment, EDC/EIP at the 4th): pain on resisted extension with full passive range, against the deep loaded pain of the ligament causes. Non-capsular restriction and carpal fixation (forms 7, 12): dorsal capsule adhesion limiting extension; a springy block at end range after a fall; X-ray first to exclude a fracture or lunate dislocation; lunate-on-radius glides (Magee 2014; Heiser 2013). Clinic findings, not cards.
