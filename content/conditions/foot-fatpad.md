## blurb
The natural cushioning pad under your heel bone can become bruised and sore, or thin out over time, leaving less shock absorption when you stand or walk.

## noticed
- A deep ache in the heel that's worse after long periods of standing, rather than first thing in the morning
- Pain that can affect both heels at once
- Discomfort that may bother you at night while resting
- Tender when you press firmly in the middle of the heel

## homeCare
- Try a cushioned heel pad or shock-absorbing insole in supportive footwear
- Avoid walking barefoot on hard floors
- Choose well-cushioned shoes and pace out long periods of standing
- Taping that holds the heel pad together can ease it for a while; ask us to show you

## seePhysioIf
- Pain at night that does not ease, pain when the sides of the heel are squeezed, or heel pain after a jump or fall: see a doctor first
- Pain in both heels, with back stiffness in the mornings, psoriasis, sore red eyes or other swollen joints: see your doctor
- Heel pain doesn't ease after a few weeks of cushioning changes
- You're unsure if it's this or another heel problem and want a clear plan

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check whether your heel pain comes from the fat pad or something else.
- Heel cushions, taping and footwear advice can help protect the heel pad and ease the pain.
- You get a plan to pace standing and walking so you can keep doing your daily activities.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 14 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Features (Tu & Bytomski 2011; Theory p. 568): deep, bruise-like mid-heel pain; reproduced by firm palpation in the centre of the heel; aggravated by walking barefoot or on hard surfaces; loss of heel pad thickness and elasticity with increasing body weight and age. Often misattributed to plantar fasciitis. Yi 2011 (retrospective, n = 250 with plantar heel pain; Theory p. 567): 53.2% plantar fasciitis, 15% fat pad atrophy; fat pad atrophy more likely with pain aggravated by prolonged standing (OR 20.91), night pain (OR 20.94) and bilateral pain (OR 24.95), without first-step pain. Symptomatic heel pad has a reduced ability to dissipate energy vs the uninvolved side (Wearing 2009; Theory p. 567); altered fat pad compressive properties are associated with symptoms (Martin 2014 CPG; Theory p. 565). Fat pad palpation is part of sole surface anatomy (MP 480). Ask about past heel injections: subcutaneous fat atrophy is a listed adverse effect of corticosteroid injection for plantar heel pain (Theory p. 547). External evidence (Chang 2022 scoping review; 7 original studies, mostly low on the evidence hierarchy): HFPS may be the second commonest cause of plantar heel pain (2 studies); some pain features may help separate it from plantar fasciopathy; a thinned heel pad on ultrasound may corroborate the diagnosis; no RCTs of viscoelastic heel cups or arch taping; research described as sparse. Screen (Theory pp. 563–568): plantar fasciopathy (proximal insertion tenderness, windlass; MP 516); calcaneal stress fracture (point tenderness; X-ray may be negative early); tarsal tunnel (Tinel, DF-eversion; MP 508–509); spondyloarthritis, infection, diabetic foot, vascular causes; lumbar radiculopathy (LQ scan). Consider fat pad atrophy when plantar fasciitis features do not fit or are not resolving (Theory p. 567). Outcomes: FAAM, FAOS or MFPDI (MP 482); NPRS with standing and walking tolerance; footwear and surface review (MP 543).
- Heel pain CPG 2014: in a cohort of 250, fat pad atrophy 15% vs plantar fasciitis 53%. Fat pad cases were far more likely to have pain with long standing (OR 20.9), night pain (OR 20.9) and pain in both heels (OR 25), WITHOUT first-step morning pain. Ask about earlier steroid injections (fat atrophy). Consider when the picture is not plantar fasciitis-like or fascia-directed care fails (C). Source: Martin RL et al., JOSPT 2014;44(11):A1-A23 (heel pain revision 2014; superseded by Koc TA et al., JOSPT 2023;53(12), which lists heel fat pad syndrome under plantar heel pain and examines the plantar calcaneus to rule out fat pad atrophy).
