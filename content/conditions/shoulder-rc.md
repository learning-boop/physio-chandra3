## blurb
The most common shoulder problem: the tendons that lift and steady the arm become sensitive to load, sometimes after a spike in activity, often for no clear reason. Pain is typically felt in the outer upper arm when reaching, and sometimes at the front of the shoulder.

## noticed
- Painful arc when lifting the arm up or out
- Pain in the outer upper arm rather than the joint itself
- Night pain when lying on that side

## homeCare
- Keep using the arm within tolerable comfort: total rest slows recovery
- Temporarily reduce (not stop) overhead activity
- Try supported reaching: slide the hand up a wall or table
- Sleep with a pillow supporting the arm
- Some pain during exercise is fine if it settles by the next day
- Most people improve over 6 to 12 weeks; some take longer

## seePhysioIf
- After a fall or sudden pull, your arm became much weaker or you cannot lift it: see a doctor within a few days, as a repair, if needed, works best within a few weeks
- Your arm will not lift on its own but goes up when you help it: see a physiotherapist or doctor within a week
- The shoulder is hot, red or swollen, or you have a fever: see a doctor the same day
- A new lump, weight loss you cannot explain, or night pain that no position eases: see a doctor
- Pain that comes on with effort, or with chest tightness or breathlessness: call 911
- Numbness or weakness spreading down the arm: see a doctor
- It isn't clearly improving after about 2 weeks, or reaching, dressing or sleep stay limited
- Not better after about 3 months of exercise: ask about a review with a sports physician or surgeon
- You want a graded strengthening plan, the treatment with the best evidence

## clinicNotes
- Scope (JOSPT 2025): rotator cuff tendinopathy includes subacromial pain (formerly "impingement"), subacromial bursopathy, long head of biceps tendinopathy (front of the shoulder, down the front of the arm) and partial-thickness tears; calcific when imaging shows a deposit. Full-thickness tears are excluded by the CPG; the AIM manual (p.287) manages atraumatic full-thickness tears with rehabilitation in the same pathway.
- Tests (B): painful arc to rule in (sens 0.62, spec 0.82, +LR 3.44); Hawkins-Kennedy to rule out (sens 0.83, -LR 0.25); 3 of 5 (Hawkins-Kennedy, Neer, painful arc, empty can, resisted ER) +LR 2.93, -LR 0.34. Never diagnose on tests alone; screen the neck and red flags (F).
- Full-thickness tear clusters (AIM manual p.286, from Murrell and Walton 2001, Litaker 2000): painful arc + drop arm + external rotation weakness, +LR 15.6; age over 65 + external rotation weakness + night pain, +LR 9.8. If positive, or after trauma with weakness or pseudoparalysis: ultrasound and early orthopaedic review (acute traumatic tears: repair best within about 3 weeks; early-repair meta-analysis 2025; BESS/BOA 2015 red flags).
- Measure: ROM by goniometer, inclinometer or phone app (A); not dynamic scapular motion (A); isometric strength by handheld dynamometer (A), not manual muscle testing (the manual and Chandra's protocols use manual testing). PROMs (A): SPADI (MCID 8), QuickDASH (13.4), DASH, WORC, ASES, OSS.
- Imaging not in initial management (F); consider after up to 12 weeks of appropriate care without improvement, or with trauma or a suspected full-thickness tear, ultrasound first (F). Refer to a sport physician, physiatrist or orthopaedics with severe, persistent pain or disability after up to 12 weeks (F).
- Rehabilitation: active exercise (motor control and/or resistance) first line (A), supervised not better than home; spinal and upper-limb manual therapy for short-term pain (B); individualised education (C); acupuncture as an adjunct (C); taping (D); work ergonomics (C). No shockwave (C) and no therapeutic ultrasound (B) for non-calcific tendinopathy (Chandra's protocols use ultrasound, laser and shockwave here: the CPG position is followed on this card). Scapular dyskinesis is a treatment target, not a diagnosis (scapular measures are unreliable, A).
- Surgery: subacromial decompression is not recommended (A; CSAW; FIMPACT at 10 years, BMJ 2025, no benefit over placebo surgery or exercise).
- Medical (for the doctor): acetaminophen (C); short-term oral NSAIDs (B); corticosteroid injection may be used (B) but not first line (C), ultrasound-guided subacromial preferred (B); not opioids first line (C); PRP and hyaluronic acid not first line.
- Differentials: full-thickness tear, frozen shoulder, calcific tendinopathy, AC joint, instability, glenohumeral OA, cervical radiculopathy, suprascapular neuropathy (painless or posterior pain, wasting behind the shoulder blade, overhead athletes), neuralgic amyotrophy.
- Prognosis (B): persistent pain more likely over 55 (OR 3.8) and with high job demands (OR 4.1); also long duration, high pain, distress, catastrophising, kinesiophobia, compensation. Improvement at 6 and 12 weeks, sometimes 12-18 months (AIM manual p.287). Return to sport (F) by load tolerance with PROMs and performance measures.
- Sources: Desmeules F et al., JOSPT 2025;55(4):235-274 (rotator cuff tendinopathy), grades in brackets; AIM Theory Manual 2023 Ch. 2.2 pp.284-287; Kulkarni R et al. (BESS/BOA), Shoulder Elbow 2015; Paavola M et al. (FIMPACT 10-year), BMJ 2025; AAOS rotator cuff CPG 2025.
