## blurb
Axial spondyloarthritis is a form of inflammatory arthritis that mainly affects the spine and the sacroiliac joints at the base of the back. Unlike the common "mechanical" back pain that follows a lift or a long day, the pain and stiffness come from inflammation, so they are worst after rest and sleep and better once you get moving. Ankylosing spondylitis is the name used when the changes can be seen on an X-ray. It is a long-term condition, but it is well understood and treatable: regular exercise and, where needed, medication from a rheumatologist help most people stay active, flexible and working.

## doctorFirst
This pattern is worth checking with your family doctor, who can arrange blood tests and imaging and consider a referral to a rheumatologist. Early diagnosis makes a real difference. Physiotherapy can start alongside: you do not need to wait.

## noticed
- A deep, dull ache in the low back and buttocks that crept up gradually, often before the age of 40, with no injury
- Stiffness in the morning that lasts more than 30 minutes and eases as the day goes on
- Worse after resting, sitting or lying still, and in the second half of the night; better with movement, exercise or a warm shower
- Buttock pain that can switch from one side to the other
- Sometimes a tight band around the chest, or pain at the heel or where tendons join bone
- More likely with psoriasis, Crohn's or colitis, a past painful red eye (uveitis), or a close relative with the condition

## homeCare
- Move every day: a walk, a swim or a cycle, plus a few minutes of spinal and hip stretches, especially in the morning when you are stiffest
- Break up long spells of sitting; stand, stretch and take a few deep breaths every half hour
- Sleep on a firm, supportive mattress with a thin pillow, and spend some time lying flat on your front or back each day
- A warm shower or bath before stretching can make the morning easier

## seePhysioIf
- Your back pain started before 40, came on slowly, is stiff for more than half an hour each morning and feels better with movement than rest, especially if it has lasted more than 3 months: we can check the pattern, start you on the right exercise plan, and write to your doctor about further tests
- You have been diagnosed and would like a personalised programme for mobility, strength, posture, breathing and fitness, adjusted during flares
- Any new painful red eye, or new severe neck or back pain after even a minor fall, needs medical care first

## clinicNotes
- ASAS inflammatory back pain (Sieper 2009): 4 or more of 5 (onset before 40, insidious onset, better with exercise, not better with rest, night pain improving on getting up): sensitivity 77%, specificity 91.7% (AIM manual p. 616). Chronic back pain (3 months or more) is the frame. A short-term response to NSAIDs also points to inflammation.
- SpA features: enthesitis (Achilles, plantar fascia), dactylitis (about 6%), uveitis, psoriasis, IBD, family history, peripheral arthritis (ankles 40%, hips 36%, knees 29%, shoulders 19%, SC joints 14%; Sampaio-Barros 2001). Hip involvement in 25-35%, with a poorer prognosis. Extra-articular: cardiovascular risk, restrictive lung disease from a rigid chest.
- Measure: BASMI (tragus-to-wall, modified Schober, lateral flexion, cervical rotation, intermalleolar distance), chest expansion (under 3 cm is limited), occiput-to-wall; SIJ provocation cluster; BASDAI and BASFI at baseline; enthesis palpation; detailed neuro exam.
- Imaging and bloods via the GP: HLA-B27 (85-95% in AS, 75-85% without X-ray changes; neither rules in nor out), CRP and ESR; MRI of the SIJ shows inflammation years before X-ray change (X-ray changes in 36% at 5 years, 59% at 10; Rudwaleit 2005), so a normal X-ray does not exclude it.
- Management (ASAS-EULAR 2022): education, regular exercise (mobility, strength, aerobic, posture and breathing; supervised or group exercise and hydrotherapy), from the earliest symptoms; smoking cessation; NSAIDs first-line and TNF or IL-17 inhibitors are physician decisions. Goals: pain, stiffness and fatigue; function; prevent kyphosis and contractures; work and participation; extra-articular disease.
- Complications (AIM manual p. 619): osteopenia, fractures (minimal trauma in an ankylosed spine, especially lower cervical), cord injury, atlantoaxial subluxation, cauda equina. No spinal thrust manipulation in an ankylosed or osteopenic spine; care with end-range cervical techniques.
- Look-alikes: mechanical low back pain (after a lift, better with rest, little morning stiffness); mechanical SIJ pain (one-sided, after pregnancy, a fall or a change in activity); nerve-root pain (below the knee with pins and needles); DISH or spinal OA (usually over 50, no night-pain pattern).
- Sources: Ramiro 2023 (ASAS-EULAR 2022 update, Ann Rheum Dis 82:19); Sieper 2009 (ASAS IBP, Ann Rheum Dis 68:784); Ortolan 2023 (non-pharmacological SLR, Ann Rheum Dis 82:142); Rudwaleit 2005, 2006; Sampaio-Barros 2001; AIM Theory Manual 2023 pp. 616-619.
