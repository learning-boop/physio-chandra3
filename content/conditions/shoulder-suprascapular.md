## blurb
A nerve runs across the top and back of the shoulder blade to the muscles that lift and turn the arm outwards. In people who do a lot of overhead sport or work (volleyball, throwing, tennis, weightlifting), or after a fall or a cyst near the shoulder, it can be stretched or pinched, giving a deep ache at the back of the shoulder and weakness turning the arm out, sometimes with the muscle behind the shoulder blade becoming thinner. Caught early, it usually responds well to a change in load and exercise.

## noticed
- A deep, hard-to-pin-down ache at the back or top of the shoulder
- Weakness or tiredness turning the arm outwards or lifting it, with the movement often still full
- The muscle at the back of the shoulder blade may look hollow or thinner than the other side
- Common in overhead athletes and workers

## homeCare
- Ease back the overhead sport or work that brings it on, rather than stopping all activity
- Avoid carrying heavy bags on that shoulder, and long spells with the shoulder pulled down
- Keep the shoulder moving gently through its range

## seePhysioIf
- The muscle at the back of the shoulder blade looks thinner, or the weakness is not improving: book promptly
- Severe shoulder pain that eased and was followed by weakness days later, with no injury: see a doctor
- Weakness after a fall or a shoulder dislocation: see a doctor, as the tendons or a nerve may be injured
- Pain spreading down the arm with tingling, or neck movement changes it: the neck may be the source

## clinicNotes
- Suprascapular nerve (C5-C6, upper trunk): compression or traction at the suprascapular notch (supraspinatus and infraspinatus) or the spinoglenoid notch (infraspinatus only, often painless wasting in volleyball players); paralabral cysts from SLAP tears, fractures, repetitive overhead traction.
- Neurodynamics (Butler): neck lateral flexion away with shoulder girdle depression, then horizontal adduction or a force down the humeral shaft; scapular rotation as a mobilisation. Palpate the suprascapular notch; compare infraspinatus bulk; ER strength at 0 degrees.
- Imaging: MRI for a cyst; EMG and nerve conduction to confirm. Refer with progressive wasting or a cyst.
- Management: load modification, scapular control, posterior shoulder mobility, nerve sliders; surgical decompression for structural causes or failed rehabilitation.
- Look-alikes: rotator cuff tear (shoulder-cufftear.md: drop arm, trauma, age), rotator cuff related pain, C5-C6 radiculopathy (neck-led, dermatomal), neuralgic amyotrophy (severe pain then weakness: doctor), quadrilateral space syndrome (axillary nerve: posterior shoulder, deltoid and teres minor, overhead throwers), myofascial pain.
- Sources: Butler DS, NOI neurodynamic techniques workbook (other nerves: suprascapular); Boykin RE et al., J Shoulder Elbow Surg 2010 (suprascapular neuropathy review).
