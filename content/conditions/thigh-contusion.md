## blurb
A hard knock to the thigh, such as a knee in football or a tackle, bruises the muscle underneath: the "dead leg". The thigh is sore, swollen, and stiff, and how far the knee will bend is a good guide to how bad the bruise is. Gentle movement early, and not massaging or heating it in the first days, helps it settle.

## noticed
- Pain, swelling, and bruising where the thigh was hit
- The knee is stiff and will not bend fully
- Limping or pain when running

## homeCare
- In the first day, rest it with the knee gently bent, and use a cold pack
- Avoid massage, heat, and alcohol in the first few days: they can increase bleeding in the muscle
- Start gentle knee bending as soon as it is comfortable

## seePhysioIf
- The knee still will not bend past halfway after a few days
- You feel a hard lump in the muscle weeks later
- You want a plan to return to sport
- The pain and tense swelling keep building over the hours after the knock, or the leg goes numb: go to the emergency department now

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check your thigh and use how far your knee bends to judge the bruise.
- Gentle early movement, building to strengthening, can help you regain knee bend and strength.
- You get a step-by-step plan to get back to sport.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 2 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Mechanism (Lamplot 2016): strain – sudden forceful eccentric contraction; rectus femoris most often (superficial, crosses two joints, type II fibre predominance), mostly mid-to-proximal RF; recent then past same injury the strongest risk factors (AFL data). Contusion – direct blow (helmet, knee, shoulder); swelling, pain and loss of knee flexion may develop within 24 h. Contusion grading by knee motion (Jackson & Feagin, as cited in Lamplot 2016): grade 1 >90°, no gait change; grade 2 ≤90°, antalgic gait, difficulty with deep knee bends and stairs; grade 3 ≤45°, severe limp, prefers crutches. Re-check over the first 24 h as swelling builds. Strain grading (Kary 2010, as cited in Lamplot 2016): grade 1 minor fibre tearing, no palpable defect; grade 2 moderate strength loss, possible defect; grade 3 complete tear, complete strength loss, defect or haematoma. Palpate the full muscle length; test strength in sitting and prone with the hip flexed and extended. CPA tests: RF – supine, hip ~60°, knee ~90°, resist hip flexion and knee extension (MP 275); vasti – resisted knee extension (MP 387); quadriceps length – knee flexion with hip extended vs hip flexed (MP 408); Thomas test RF component (MP 292). Adolescents: vibration (US or tuning fork) reproducing pain at the ASIS or AIIS suggests avulsion fracture (MP 334). Acute compartment syndrome (Lamplot 2016): pain out of proportion, worse with passive quadriceps stretch, progressively firm compartment, neurovascular deficit; severe contusion can look similar → emergency referral. Check distal pulses and sensation, and active SLR for the extensor mechanism. Myositis ossificans (Lamplot 2016): 9–20% in US military academy series; risk rises with injury severity; may show on plain X-ray from 2–4 weeks if pain persists or a mass is palpable; can mimic sarcoma (MRI or CT may be needed) → refer to doctor. Management outline (Lamplot 2016): contusion phase 1 RICE ~2 days with the knee rested flexed (Aronen 2006 described immediate 120° flexion; not reviewed in session), phase 2 restore knee and hip flexion, phase 3 strengthening and sport drills. Strain: relative rest 24–72 h, active phase usually within 5 days, avoid ballistic stretching. RTP: full pain-free knee and hip motion, near-normal strength vs the other side, satisfactory sport-specific tasks.
