## blurb
The most commonly injured ligament in the ankle, stretched or torn when the ankle rolls inwards. Many settle well, but without balance and strength exercises about 4 in 10 people go on to have an ankle that keeps rolling or giving way.

## noticed
- Outer ankle pain and swelling after a roll/twist
- Bruising in the first days
- A feeling the ankle could give way, or a fear of rolling it again
- Repeated sprains if past ones weren't rehabbed

## homeCare
- First days: protect it with a brace or tape, keep it raised when resting, and move it gently; a badly sprained ankle sometimes needs a boot for a short time
- Put weight on it as comfort allows, once the bone questions in the injury check were clear or an X-ray was normal
- Then balance work — single-leg standing — is the key to preventing re-sprains
- Keep up the balance and strength exercises after the pain has gone; return to sport when you can hop, land and change direction confidently, not just after a set time

## seePhysioIf
- You could not take 4 steps straight after the injury, or the back edge or tip of either ankle bone, or the bony knob on the outer edge of the foot, is sharply tender to press: see a doctor the same day for an X-ray
- You have diabetes or numb feet: after any injury, see a doctor for an X-ray, even if you can walk on it
- A snap or a kick feeling at the back of the ankle, and you cannot rise onto your toes on that leg: see a doctor the same day
- Your calf becomes swollen, warm or tender, especially in a brace, boot or cast: see a doctor the same day; if you are also short of breath or have chest pain, call 911
- You can't walk comfortably within a few days
- The ankle still feels like it could give way after 2 weeks
- You've sprained the same ankle more than once — balance retraining prevents the cycle
- Weeks later there is a deep ache, swelling or catching inside the ankle: see us, as the joint surface may need checking
- A sore spot just below the tip of the outer ankle bone, deep at the back of the ankle in front of the heel cord, or on the outer side of the foot just in front of the ankle, that is not easing 1 to 2 weeks after the sprain: see your doctor, as an X-ray or scan may be needed

## physioHelps

- A physiotherapist can check your sprain, rule out a break, and explain how ligaments heal.
- Taping or a brace, gentle hands-on care and early exercise can help you walk comfortably sooner.
- Balance and strength training aims to lower the chance of the ankle rolling again.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 7 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Ottawa Ankle Rules (Stiell 1996; MP 483): ankle X-ray if malleolar-zone pain plus inability to bear weight immediately and in the ED (4 steps) or bone tenderness at the posterior edge or inferior tip of either malleolus; foot X-ray if midfoot-zone pain plus inability to bear weight (4 steps) or tenderness at the base of the 5th MT or navicular. Adults Sn 93–100%, Sp 49%; children <15 Sn 80–98%, Sp 40–53%. Ankle/foot fracture with ankle sprain ~9–15% (Polzer 2012). Avulsion fractures also hurt on stability tests (MP 490). Timing: assess ATFL tears 4–6 days post-injury; anterior drawer Sn 0.96, Sp 0.84 (Delahunt 2018 ROAST; Theory p. 527). JOSPT 2021: exam most accurate 4–5 days after injury; use anterolateral talar palpation and the reverse anterolateral drawer in addition to the anterior drawer (B). ATFL tears: 86% mid-substance, 14% avulsion; most common <30 y (Safran 1999; Theory p. 527). Table 7-2 (MP 490): TC anterior drawer (Schneiders andKaras) Sn 43–60, Sp 69–87, +LR 1.58–3.84, −LR 0.50–0.76; medial talar tilt (Schwieterman) Sn 22–78, Sp 47–99, +LR 0.59–27.25, −LR 0.31–1.04. Use ≥2 tests in combination and compare sides (MP 489–490). Drawer needs a relaxed patient; false negatives common in inexperienced hands (Roosen 2013; MP 490–492). Talar tilt better when not used in acute ankles; positive >23° or 10° side-to-side difference (MP 493). DF/ER mechanism or anterior ITFJ pain → syndesmosis tests (fibular translation, squeeze, DF-ER; MP 488–489, 503–505) – see high-ankle-sprain. Slow recovery → suspect a complex sprain (Theory pp. 525–528): lateral/posterior talar process, anterior calcaneal process, 5th MT or navicular fracture; bone bruise (up to 50%); OCL (up to 14%); deltoid tear; syndesmosis (~20%); cuboid subluxation; anterolateral impingement (~3%); peroneal subluxation/tear; neural injury (esp. grade II–III); sinus tarsi syndrome. Measures (JOSPT 2021, A): swelling, ROM, talar translation and inversion, single-leg balance at baseline and ≥2 times; WB lunge, SLB eyes closed, SEBT (anterior, anteromedial, posteromedial, posterolateral); FAAM, LEFS, PROMIS. Lunge test Table 7-1 (MP 488): intra-rater ICC 0.65–0.99, inter-rater 0.80–0.99; MDC 4.7°/1.9 cm intra, 4.5°/1.6 cm inter. SLB eyes open should be ≥30 s (MP 486). CPA list also FADI, FAOS, AJFAT (MP 482). Whitman 2009 CPR (MP 484): predicts short-term response to thrust and non-thrust manipulation plus exercise after inversion sprain; criteria are not reproduced in the CPA chapter – check the original before applying. Treatment (MP 498–499, 531–532, 541–544, 550, 564–565; JOSPT 2021): external support and progressive WB (A); severe injuries may need a semi-rigid brace to below-knee cast (Martin 2013; JOSPT 2021 up to 10 days); structured exercise (A); lymphatic drainage, soft tissue and joint mobilisation incl. AP talar mobilisation within pain-free range with exercise (A); WB MWM for DF (MP 550); ATFL DTF (MP 499); repeated intermittent ice (C); ultrasound not recommended (A; MP 544). Post-acute: functional WB and single-limb balance on unstable surfaces (MP 542); L3 17-step stability progression (MP 564–565); hip and trunk training (hip strength deficit is a risk factor; MP 564). Prognosis and prevention (JOSPT 2021): return to participation from 1 day to a little over 3 weeks; full symptom recovery can take months; 40% of first-time sprains developed CAI in a prospective cohort (n = 70); inability to jump and land within 2 weeks predicted CAI at 6 months. Bracing plus proprioceptive/balance training after a first sprain (A); balance/neuromuscular programme for ≥3 months after ankle injury (Kaminski 2018; MP 564). No static stretching without a warm-up – dynamic warm-up (MP 541).
- Diagnosis (B): inversion injury, Ottawa ankle rules negative, then the reverse anterolateral drawer test (sens 0.89, spec 0.90) and anterolateral talar palpation added to the traditional drawer (sens 0.22). Examination is most accurate 4 to 5 days after injury.
- Ottawa ankle rules applied in full: sensitivity 92-100%; weight bearing alone drops sensitivity to about 88%. They cover the distal tibia and fibula, base of the 5th metatarsal and navicular; under 18, the full rules (sens 100%) beat the low-risk ankle rules.
- Copathology on MRI in 92% of acute sprains: syndesmosis, osteochondral lesion, talar bone bruise, deltoid sprain, Achilles rupture, fibularis tendon or retinaculum injury, os trigonum, midfoot sprain, growth plate injury.
- Measure at baseline and at least twice more (A): swelling, weight-bearing lunge, talar translation and inversion, single-leg balance eyes closed, SEBT, timed hop; PROMIS, FAAM or LEFS. PSEQ (C).
- Prognosis (B): record age, BMI, coping, instability, previous sprain, weight-bearing pain, dorsiflexion, medial joint-line tenderness, balance and jump-landing. About 40% of first-time sprains develop chronic ankle instability; inability to jump and land at 2 weeks predicts it at 6 months.
- Treatment: brace or tape with progressive weight bearing (A); severe sprains, a semi-rigid brace up to a below-knee cast for up to 10 days (A); structured exercise (protected active ROM, stretching, neuromuscular and balance, clinic plus home) (A); manual therapy including lymphatic drainage and AP talar mobilisation, pain-free (A); early return to work or sport in a brace (B). After a first sprain, brace plus balance training to prevent the next (A). Ice with exercise, pulsed shortwave, laser (C); electrotherapy and acupuncture (D); no ultrasound (A); psychologically informed care (E).
- Source: Martin RL et al., JOSPT 2021;51(4):CPG1-CPG80 (lateral ankle sprain revision 2021), grades in brackets.
- Return to sport: the PAASS framework (Smith 2021, BJSM 55:1270): Pain, Ankle impairments, Athlete perception, Sensorimotor control, Sport and functional performance; criteria rather than time.
- AIM manual p. 527 quotes the anterior drawer test at sensitivity 0.96 and specificity 0.84 when done at 4 to 6 days (delayed examination, ROAST, Delahunt 2018); the CPG figure (0.22) is for early testing.
- Your ATFL protocol differs from the CPG: it allows ultrasound as "mixed" evidence (CPG: do not use, A) and ice 15 to 20 minutes every 2 hours (CPG: ice only alongside exercise, C).
