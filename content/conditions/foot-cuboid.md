## blurb
On the outer side of the middle of the foot, the cuboid bone and its joints can become sore and stiff after an ankle sprain or from repeated load, as in dancing and running. It hurts on the outer edge of the foot when pushing off or walking, and often eases quickly with hands-on treatment, taping and strengthening.

## noticed
- Pain on the outer edge of the middle of the foot
- Worse pushing off, walking on uneven ground or rising onto the toes
- Often after an ankle sprain, or in dancers and runners

## homeCare
- Supportive shoes; avoid uneven ground for a while
- Ease off the running or jumping that brings it on
- Gentle ankle and foot movement and calf strengthening within comfort

## seePhysioIf
- After an injury, the bony knob halfway along the outer edge of the foot is sharply tender, or you cannot take 4 steps: see a doctor the same day for an X-ray
- After an injury, there is bruising on the sole in the middle of the foot: see a doctor the same day
- One spot of bone on the outer edge of the foot hurts with every step or hop, or aches at night, after more running, walking or training: see a doctor first (possible stress fracture)
- It is not settling after 1 to 2 weeks
- You want hands-on treatment and a plan back to sport

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check the outer side of your foot and rule out other causes of the pain.
- Hands-on treatment of the joint, taping or a small pad, and strengthening can help ease the pain.
- You get a plan to return to walking, running or dancing step by step.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 18 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Clinical diagnosis – no definitive diagnostic procedure (Durall 2011) and no reliable imaging (Theory pp. 558, 560). Mechanism unknown; proposed: CC joint subluxation causing incongruity or altered arthrokinematics (Blakeslee & Morris 1987); fibroadipose synovial folds between the CC and cubometatarsal joints impinging (Lidtke & George 2004); peroneus longus everting the cuboid on an inverting/supinating calcaneus, the lateral cuboid rotating dorsally and medially (Jennings & Davies 2005; Brukner 2016). Triggers (Theory p. 559): single inversion sprain; repetitive eversion moments on a supinating foot (sudden lateral movements); severe over-pronation. Epidemiology (Jennings & Davies 2005): 4% of 3600 athletes with foot injuries; 17% of professional ballet dancers with foot/ankle injuries; 6.7% of PF/inversion ankle sprains. Contributors: midtarsal/lateral ankle instability, excess body weight, ill-fitting orthoses/shoes, excessive training or uneven surfaces, prior sprain (Blakeslee & Morris 1987), pronated (longer peroneus longus moment arm) or supinated feet (Newell & Woodle 1981), ballet (Marshall 1992). Findings (Theory pp. 559–560): antalgic gait, most difficulty at push-off; slight depression over the dorsal-medial cuboid and/or plantar-medial fullness; PF and inversion may be restricted; resisted inversion and/or eversion may reproduce pain (Marshall 1992); palpation – slight sulcus over the dorsal CC joint, local oedema and tenderness over the dorsal/plantar CC joint, peroneus longus tendon or EDB origin (Blakeslee & Morris 1987). No validated tests; two manoeuvres (Jennings & Davies 2005): midtarsal adduction test – stabilise calcaneus and STJ, adduct the midtarsal joint in the transverse plane (compresses the CC joint medially, distracts it laterally); midtarsal supination test – same stabilisation, supinate. CC dorsal/plantar glides may be painful, or restricted if 'locked', but are unreliable (hyper- or hypomobility). Manual Level 2 midfoot (MP 517–519): midtarsal AROM/PROM incl. fanning/folding; plantar and dorsal glides of the cuboid on the calcaneus; midtarsal traction and compression; no midfoot mechanical stability tests are described – spring and bifurcate ligaments can be injured in ankle sprain. After acute injury, Ottawa foot rule first (adults Sn 93%–100%, Sp 49%; MP 483); anterior process of calcaneus fracture is tender over the CC joint just distal to the ATFL insertion (Judd 2002, Theory p. 526). Manipulation is the treatment of choice if no contraindications and the ATFL etc. have healed enough to tolerate it (Theory p. 560): cuboid whip (Newell & Woodle 1981; prone, knee flexed 70–90°, ankle in neutral DF, thumbs on the plantar cuboid, low-amplitude high-velocity thrust as the ankle is 'whipped' into PF and supination); cuboid squeeze (Marshall 1992; prone, knee flexed, maximal ankle PF and foot/toe flexion while squeezing the cuboid dorsally); dorsal parallel thrust – Level 3 alternate cuboid technique (MP 560–562: prone, knee 90°, stabilise the calcaneus, pisiform on the cuboid, dorsal/lateral thrust along the joint plane; keep the TC joint neutral, do not let it plantarflex on the thrust; extra safety – prone knee bend, lumbar comfort, TC/ITFJ tolerance to splay, PFJ compression). CC distraction (MP 559–560) and plantar parallel thrust (MP 562–563) for CC articular restriction. Often a single manipulation is enough; more may be needed depending on symptom duration (Newell & Woodle 1981). Post-manipulation: local modalities and glides to restore CC supination (Mooney & Maffey-Ward 1994); cuboid pad taping, low-dye taping, lateral calcaneal wedge (Caselli & Pantelaras 2004); reassess mobility and stability, stabilise if needed, warn that post-treatment soreness is a common adverse effect (MP 535, 551). Recurrence prevention: stretch gastrocnemius, soleus and peronei; strengthen intrinsics and tib post; foot orthoses.
- Lateral midfoot pain over the calcaneocuboid joint or 4th and 5th TMT joints; pain with lateral column loading and push-off; often after inversion sprain or in dancers. No validated test (evidence low: case series and reviews); use history, palpation, midfoot stress and response to taping or padding. Exclude a 5th metatarsal base fracture, a Lisfranc injury (plantar ecchymosis) and stress fracture.
- Management: cuboid manipulation (whip or squeeze) with consent, taping or a cuboid pad, peroneus longus and intrinsic strengthening, footwear; the protocol expects improvement in 2 to 6 weeks in mild cases (figures not independently sourced).
