## blurb
This is osteoarthritis behind the kneecap: a slow change in the cartilage and bone there over many years, sometimes in people who've had kneecap pain on and off for a long time. Strengthening the thigh and hip muscles often helps.

## noticed
- Pain behind or around the kneecap that has been present for many months or longer
- A short period of stiffness in the morning that eases once you get moving
- Discomfort going down stairs, squatting, kneeling, or standing up after sitting a while

## homeCare
- Stay active with low-impact movement like walking, cycling, or swimming
- Build strength in the thigh and hip muscles to support the joint
- If relevant, gradual weight management can ease load on the knee
- During exercise, some discomfort (up to about 5 out of 10) is fine if it settles by the next morning; if the joint is worse the next day, ease back a little rather than stopping

## seePhysioIf
- The knee becomes hot, red and swollen, especially with a fever: get urgent medical care
- Other joints swell too, or stiffness lasts well over half an hour each morning: see a doctor
- It is getting quickly worse over a few weeks: see your doctor
- The pain or stiffness is starting to limit your daily activities or sleep
- Symptoms keep getting worse despite gentle activity changes

## clinicNotes
- Osteoarthritis foundation (content/reference/osteoarthritis.md; "Osteoarthritis.docx" intake v1.0, signed by Chandra 2 Oct 2026; AIM Theory Manual 2023 pp. 572-581): clinical diagnosis without imaging (NICE NG226: 45 or over, activity-related pain, morning stiffness 30 minutes or less); PFOA is not separately defined in NICE: see the international PFOA consortium consensus (van Middelkoop 2018). First line at every stage: education, strength and neuromuscular exercise (2-3 times a week, 6-12 weeks), weight management where relevant; manual therapy only alongside exercise. Pain monitoring: up to 5/10 during exercise, back to usual by the next morning. Avoid "wear and tear" and "bone on bone" framing.
- Kneecap: quadriceps and hip strengthening are the core; patellar taping helps short term; brace evidence is mixed (pooled trials not significant; ACR 2019 conditional for a patellofemoral brace). Measures: KOOS-PF, stairs and squat tolerance. Crepitus often accompanies patellofemoral degenerative change (AIM manual p.42); patellofemoral x-ray views if imaging is needed (p.8). About 40% of older adults with knee pain have both tibiofemoral and patellofemoral OA on x-ray (p.5).
- The PFP link, two views: the patellofemoral pain CPG 2019 (which excludes PFOA) calls the link from PFP to PFOA unproven prospectively; retrospectively, adolescent anterior knee pain (OR 7.5) and past patellar dislocation (OR 3.2) are associated with isolated PFOA, and in chronic PFP over 40 about a quarter have isolated PFOA and 44% combined patellofemoral and tibiofemoral OA. The AIM manual calls persistent PFP "a frequent precursor" of patellofemoral degenerative disease (p.42), with 71-91% still in pain years later (p.41), and links patellar dislocation to PF OA (p.64). The CPG's 'no brace' recommendation does not carry over to PFOA.
- Sources: content/reference/osteoarthritis.md; Willy RW et al., JOSPT 2019;49(9):CPG1-CPG95; van Middelkoop M et al., 2018 (PFOA consensus); device-therapy meta-analysis (PMC8191025); Kolasinski SL et al. (ACR/AF) 2020; AIM Theory Manual 2023 Ch. 2.7.
