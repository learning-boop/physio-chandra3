## blurb
Sometimes a small piece of cartilage or bone comes loose inside the hip joint. It can move around and now and then get caught between the joint surfaces, giving a sudden sharp pain or a jamming feeling that can stop you mid-step. Between catches the hip may feel almost normal.

## noticed
- Sudden, severe, sharp twinges in the hip that stop you mid-step, sometimes shooting down the front of the thigh toward the knee
- The hip jams or catches in certain positions, especially when twisting
- Shaking or gently pulling the leg can free it up
- More likely after an old hip injury or dislocation, or with hip arthritis

## homeCare
- If the hip catches, gently shaking the leg or easing into a comfortable position may help it release
- Avoid sharp twisting on that leg for now
- Keep active with comfortable, straight-line activities

## seePhysioIf
- Your hip catches or gives way: a physiotherapy assessment can help, and can tell you whether a medical opinion is needed
- The catching keeps coming back or is not improving: your doctor can arrange imaging and a specialist opinion
- The hip is locked and you cannot move it: see a doctor the same day
- The pain is constant rather than coming in sudden catches, or you have deep pain at night: see us or your doctor, as something else may be going on

- A physiotherapist can talk through your catching episodes, check how your hip moves and explain what may be happening.
- With your consent, a specific hands-on technique that gently pulls and turns the hip may help ease the catching.
- Exercises keep the hip moving and strong, and we can tell you if you need to see a doctor for imaging.

## clinicNotes
- Presentation (AIM Theory Manual 2023 p. 406; Lower Quadrant Clinical Manual Ch. 5 p. 311): non-capsular limitation; painful bony end-feel in certain positions; springy or early capsular end-feel; pain at extreme flexion or ER; ROM generally full. Arthroscopy is the gold standard; CT and plain films underestimate loose bodies (Khanna 2014, as cited).
- Loose-body manipulation (manual pp. 311-312): supine, hip 60 degrees of flexion, knee extended; strong long-axis traction to R2; rotate into the most comfortable rotation and back while extending to neutral; informed consent before the thrust. Before: neural mobility (slump, SLR), tibiofemoral stability (cruciates), pain on traction in the lumbar spine or SIJ. After: reassess, avoid stress positions, warn of soreness; refer for imaging or an orthopaedic opinion if no change.
- Causes: hip OA, a previous injury or dislocation, synovial chondromatosis, osteochondritis dissecans. Differentials: labral tear (hip-fai), snapping hip, microinstability, lumbar referral.
- No CPG exists (searched 26 Oct 2026); the JOSPT nonarthritic hip CPG 2023 allows joint mobilisation where pain or capsular restriction limits mobility (F).
