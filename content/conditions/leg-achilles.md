## blurb
The Achilles dislikes sudden jumps in training load. It responds with pain and morning stiffness in the tendon a few centimetres above the heel — and, importantly, it recovers through graded loading, not rest.

## noticed
- Pain and stiffness in the tendon 2 to 6 cm above the heel, worst on first steps
- Worse after running, hills, or jumping
- Local tenderness or slight thickening of the tendon
- Tight calves

## homeCare
- Trim (don't stop) the aggravating training; avoid sudden spikes in load
- Begin gentle calf raises within comfortable pain and progress gradually
- A small heel raise in the shoe can ease it short-term

## seePhysioIf
- Heel/calf pain lasts more than 1–2 weeks or keeps flaring
- Stairs, walking, or running are limited
- You want a structured loading program — the proven treatment for tendinopathy

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check your Achilles and explain why the tendon has become sore.
- A step-by-step calf and tendon loading programme can help the tendon get stronger.
- You get a gradual plan to adjust your training and get back to running and sport.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 11 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Clinical diagnosis (CPG 2024): localised midportion pain, load-provoked pain, palpation pain and tendon thickening; Royal London test and painful arc sign. Imaging is selective. Hutchison et al. (n = 21, midportion AT; via CPG JOSPT 2018): self-reported pain 2–6 cm proximal to the insertion, extending to the calcaneus, Sn 84%, Sp 73%; pain on midportion palpation Sn 78%, Sp 77% – 'accurate and reliable' (Theory p. 518). Insertional AT: TOP over the distal 2 cm of the tendon and posterior calcaneus; often diagnosed when the tendon is >6 mm thick; aggravated by prolonged rest, pain increases with activity (Chimenti 2017; Theory p. 517). Kvist 1991: 20–25% insertional, 66% non-insertional (Theory p. 516). Rule out rupture: Thompson test (MP 507; Table 7-4, Schwieterman 2013: about 96% sensitive and 93% specific in Maffulli 1998 (the form's "Sn 29–76%, Sp 96–100%, +LR 3.3–43.1, −LR 0.3–0.8" are the knee manual's quadriceps active test figures, corrected 26 Oct 2026)); the ankle may plantarflex with a torn tendon if the sheath is intact, so also assess single-leg heel raise. Posterior ankle DDx (CPG 2018; Theory p. 517): acute rupture, partial tear, retrocalcaneal bursitis, posterior impingement, sural nerve irritation/neuroma, os trigonum, accessory soleus strain, tendon ossification, systemic inflammatory disease, plantaris, fascial tears, insertional AT. Risk factors (Young 2007; Theory p. 516): intrinsic – age, sex, overweight, hyper/hypo-pronation, hindfoot/forefoot varus/valgus, LLD, reduced flexibility, laxity, weakness; extrinsic – corticosteroids, fluoroquinolones, anabolic steroids, training error, surface, excessive load, fatigue. Check DF knee straight and bent (MP 485–486), lunge test (MP 487–488; Table 7-1) and calf strength (MP 497). Outcomes: VISA-A ('only valid and reliable' tool for midportion AT; Murphy 2019; Theory p. 521; MP 482); FAAM or LEFS; heel-raise endurance and hop tests; Limb Symmetry Index for heel-raise reps and height (Silbernagel 2010). Imaging: US and MRI correlate only moderately with clinical status; normalisation of tendon appearance is not needed for clinical improvement (Rio 2018; Theory p. 518).
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 12 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Thompson test (MP 507): prone, foot off the plinth, squeeze the mid calf; no spontaneous PF = tendon likely ruptured. The ankle may still plantarflex with a torn tendon if the sheath is intact, so also assess single-leg heel raise. Table 7-4 (Schwieterman 2013 SR; original data Maffulli 1998, n = 161): about 96% sensitive and 93% specific in Maffulli 1998 (the form's "Sn 29–76%, Sp 96–100%, +LR 3.3–43.1, −LR 0.3–0.8" are the knee manual's quadriceps active test figures, corrected 26 Oct 2026) (as printed in the manual). AAOS diagnostic criteria (via Mittal 2024 – external): ≥2 of positive Thompson, decreased PF strength, palpable gap, increased passive DF. Imaging not required for most; ultrasound if the diagnosis is uncertain; non-WB X-ray if a calcaneal avulsion is suspected; MRI rarely (e.g. proximal rupture) (Mittal 2024). Posterior ankle pain DDx (CPG JOSPT 2018, as cited in Theory p. 517): acute rupture, partial tear, retrocalcaneal bursitis, posterior impingement, sural nerve irritation/neuroma, os trigonum, accessory soleus strain, tendon ossification, systemic inflammatory disease, plantaris, fascial tears, insertional AT. Extrinsic risk factors for tendon problems include corticosteroids and fluoroquinolones (Theory p. 516). Clot screen during immobilisation: Wells model ≥2 = DVT likely, <2 = unlikely (bedridden ≥3 days or major surgery, localised tenderness, generalised leg swelling, calf swelling >3 cm 10 cm below the tibial tuberosity, pitting oedema, collateral superficial veins, prior history; MP 507). Homans test is not a useful clinical test (MP 508). Medical team may consider DVT prophylaxis (Mittal 2024). Treatment evidence (Cohen 2023): incidence 11–37/100,000 and rising; non-operative treatment with early functional rehabilitation supported for most; surgery ~1.6% absolute reduction in re-rupture but complications 4.9% vs 1.6% (mostly infection; ~1 per 30 operated); higher non-operative re-rupture only without early ROM rehab; insertional/avulsion tears and possibly athletes are exceptions. Diagnosis and start of functional rehab within 48 h, foot immobilised in PF, non-WB initially; supervision by an experienced physiotherapist considered essential to avoid weakness from overstretching the tendon. Initial medical care (Mittal 2024): PF (equinus) cast or slab ideally within 24–48 h, non-WB for at least 2 weeks, then orthosis or below-knee cast in PF; heel wedges acceptable but may give midfoot rather than hindfoot PF. RCT n = 526 (as cited): re-rupture 6.2% non-operative vs 0.6% in each surgical group; nerve injury 5.2% minimally invasive vs 2.8% open. Physio after the treatment decision: follow the team's protocol for WB, wedge removal and DF limits; avoid early stretching into DF beyond the protocol (Cohen 2023). Loading principle from the tendinopathy section – seated calf raise as a starter, progressing to unilateral standing heel raise (Revak 2017; Theory p. 519) – applied only within protocol limits. Outcomes: heel-rise endurance and Limb Symmetry Index (Silbernagel 2010; Theory p. 521), hop tests later, FAAM, LEFS (MP 482).
