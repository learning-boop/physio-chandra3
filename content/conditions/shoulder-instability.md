## blurb
The shoulder slips, or has come out of place (dislocated), most often in younger, active people; some people have naturally loose joints. Some shoulders slip a little with no injury, after many repetitions of a sport movement, or backwards when pushing. After a first dislocation, a strengthening programme can help; if you are young and play contact or overhead sport, or it has come out more than once, we will also talk about whether a surgeon's opinion is worthwhile.

## noticed
- A sense of slipping or apprehension in certain positions (often reaching up and out, or up and back)
- A previous dislocation or 'dead arm' moments
- It slips during sleep or everyday tasks, or clicks with a feeling of looseness
- A clunk, shift or 'giving way' when you push (push-ups, bench press, a heavy door), with an ache at the back of the shoulder
- The arm feels heavy, tired or loose, or aches when carrying heavy bags; sometimes both shoulders
- It slips or catches during a repeated sport movement such as swimming, throwing or serving, often worse as you tire

## homeCare
- Avoid the specific positions that feel apprehensive for now
- Keep general shoulder strength work within confident range
- Don't repeatedly 'test' the slip
- Some people have naturally loose joints; a specific exercise programme can help with this
- After a dislocation, use a sling for comfort for a few days (usually no longer than a week), and keep the elbow, wrist and hand moving every day
- Pause push-ups, bench press and heavy pushing until the shoulder has been checked
- Split heavy loads between both hands, or use a backpack
- Avoid deep stretching at the end of the shoulder's range; it is flexible enough already
- Cut back the repeated sport movement (about half, for two weeks) and avoid training to exhaustion

## seePhysioIf
- It is still out of place or looks a different shape: go to the emergency department; do not try to put it back yourself
- It came out for the first time and has not been X-rayed (for example it went back by itself): see a doctor for an X-ray before starting exercises
- A numb patch on the outer upper arm, or you cannot lift the arm since it came out: see a doctor the same day
- You are 40 or over and the arm is weak after it came out: see a doctor, as a rotator cuff tear is common at this age
- It came out during a seizure (fit) or an electric shock: see a doctor for an X-ray, as a shoulder that slips out backwards is easily missed
- It has slipped or dislocated before: a structured strengthening programme is first-line care
- After any dislocation or partial slip, book in the first week or two so exercises can start early
- It is not getting better after 4 to 6 weeks of exercises: see your doctor as well
- Apprehension limits sport or work, or you want assessment before returning to overhead or contact sport

## physioHelps

- A physiotherapist can check how your shoulder moves and explain why it slips.
- A tailored strengthening programme aims to help the muscles hold the shoulder steady and build confidence.
- You get a plan back to sport, and advice on when a surgeon's opinion may be worthwhile.

## clinicNotes
- JOSPT adhesive capsulitis CPG 2013 differential (F): instability fits age under 40, a history of dislocation, excess glenohumeral accessory motion and apprehension in flexion, horizontal abduction and/or external rotation; global motion loss and no apprehension point away.
- First dislocation: X-ray before and after reduction (BESS 2016; Dutch guideline, Acta Orthop 2026). At 40 or over: screen for a rotator cuff tear (about 57% full-thickness on ultrasound after a first dislocation over 40) and axillary nerve injury, and image early if weakness persists (BESS 2016; Lizzio 2017 via AIM manual p.304; the site's injury screen I4, I5).
- Recurrence: about 39% at 1 year overall (Olds 2015), 44% at 40 or under versus 11% over 40, about 50% at 15-30; 72% at 12-22 over 25 years (Hovelius 2008, JBJS). Discuss primary surgery for young contact or overhead athletes, recurrent dislocation or bone loss (AIM manual pp.309-311; Dutch guideline 2026). A sling gives no benefit beyond about a week (Paterson 2010). Voluntary instability responds poorly to surgery (p.304). Atraumatic or multidirectional instability: a specific programme (Watson MDI programme, Warby 2018).
- Tests (AIM manual pp.305-308, OCR figures to be checked against the printed page): relocation sens 0.64; surprise (release) sens 0.82; bony apprehension sens 0.94, spec 0.84, +LR 7.1; load and shift spec 0.78-1.00; apprehension plus relocation cluster sens 0.81, spec 0.98, +LR 39.7, -LR 0.19 (Hegedus 2012). Outcome: WOSI. Posterior instability (seizure, electric shock, push-ups or blocking): see the injury screen I6.
- Classification (forms 10-14; Sadi 2020): traumatic, microtraumatic, atraumatic, voluntary. Anterior (form 10): age at the FIRST event is the recurrence factor; Gagey hyperabduction > 15°; surgical opinion for a competitive athlete aged 14-35, bone loss, recurrence or HAGL (Hovelius 2016). Posterior (form 11): jerk and Kim tests (Kim 2005), posterior load-and-shift, symptoms in flexion, adduction and internal rotation (pushing, bench press); WOSI or MISS. Multidirectional (form 12): Beighton, sulcus, load-and-shift in three directions, scapular resting position, symptom modification guiding the Watson programme (Parts 1 and 2, 2016-2017); scapular control is treated within the programme, not as a separate diagnosis (JOSPT 2025). Microtraumatic (form 14): ER/IR ratios, fatigue, technique review with the coach (Kibler 2013).
- Sources: Kelley MJ et al., JOSPT 2013; BESS/BOA traumatic anterior instability 2016; Dutch guideline, Acta Orthop 2026; Olds M et al., BJSM 2015; Hovelius L et al., JBJS 2008; Warby SA et al., Am J Sports Med 2018; Paterson WH et al., JBJS 2010; AIM Theory Manual 2023 Ch. 2.2 pp.301-311; "PhysioChandra Shoulder Conditions.docx" forms 10, 11, 12 and 14 (v1.0, 19 Oct 2026).
