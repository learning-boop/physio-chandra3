## blurb
The nerves that supply the front of the thigh leave the spine higher up in the low back than the ones that cause sciatica. When one of them is irritated, often by a disc or narrowing where it leaves the spine, pain, tingling or numbness can be felt across the front of the thigh, sometimes into the knee or the inside of the shin, and the thigh muscle can feel weak. Like sciatica, most cases settle over weeks to a few months with the right advice and exercise.

## noticed
- Pain, burning or tingling across the front of the thigh, sometimes to the knee or the inside of the shin
- Brought on by lying on your front, a long stride, or leaning back with the hip straight; sometimes by standing up tall or walking
- The leg can bother you more than the back, and there may be little back pain at all
- Sometimes the knee feels weak or gives way on stairs

## homeCare
- Find the positions that ease it: often sitting, or lying on your side with the hips and knees a little bent
- Keep walking in short, comfortable spells and change position often
- For now, avoid long strides, deep lunges and stretches that bring on the thigh tingling
- Use the handrail on stairs if the knee feels weak

## seePhysioIf
- Numbness around your saddle area or genitals, new trouble starting or controlling urine, a new bowel accident, or new loss of feeling during sex: go to the emergency department now
- Weakness in both legs, or weakness getting worse day by day: go to the emergency department now
- The knee giving way, or numbness that does not go away: book promptly, and see a doctor the same day if it is getting worse
- A past cancer, a fever, unexplained weight loss, or pain that is constant whatever you do: see your doctor this week (the same day with a fever)
- Diabetes, with sudden severe thigh pain then weakness and weight loss: see your doctor promptly (diabetic amyotrophy)
- Leg symptoms limiting walking, sleep or work, or not easing after two to three weeks: a physiotherapy assessment can check the nerve and give you a plan

## clinicNotes
- Upper lumbar roots (L2-L4) and the femoral nerve. Less common than L5-S1 and more often older patients; anterior thigh pain is also referred from the hip, so clear the hip first.
- Tests: femoral nerve stretch / prone knee bend (crude: many structures); slump knee bend (side-lying slump, knee about 90 degrees, extend the hip, differentiate with the neck; Trainor and Pinnington 2011 pilot, small sample: sensitivity 100% (40-100), specificity 83% (52-98), LR+ 6.0, LR- 0, kappa 0.71 - promising, not yet confirmed); the femoral nerve stretch test can be falsely positive from tight or injured anterior thigh muscles and from the hip; normal femoral slump responses (Lai et al. 2012). Add hip abduction for the obturator nerve, adduction for the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (Butler). Hip lateral and medial rotation bias the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves.
- Neurology: L3-L4 dermatomes (anterior thigh, medial knee and shin), quadriceps and hip flexor strength, knee reflex; femoral nerve palpation at the inguinal ligament.
- Look-alikes: hip OA or labral pain (groin, hip rotation reproduces, no thigh tingling: hip guide); meralgia paraesthetica (lateral thigh only, sensory only, tight belt: thigh guide); diabetic amyotrophy (severe pain then proximal weakness and weight loss: GP); femoral neuropathy after hip or pelvic surgery, iliacus haematoma on anticoagulants (urgent); retroperitoneal mass; upper lumbar disc in a patient with a past cancer (red flags).
- Exam level and interface reading: as for neural mechanosensitivity (lowback-neural.md, src/data/neurodynamics.js).
- Management: as for radicular pain (JOSPT 2012 classification, 2021 interventions): education, directional preference, nerve sliders for the femoral tract (half push-up with neck slider, Butler), graded activity; refer with progressive weakness.
