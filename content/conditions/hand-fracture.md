## blurb
After a broken bone in the hand or a finger, or an operation such as a tendon repair, the hand needs protecting while it heals, and the fingers stiffen quickly. Following your fracture clinic's or surgeon's plan, with a splint and carefully chosen exercises at each stage, can help bring back a good fist and grip. Recovery usually takes a few months.

## noticed
- Fingers that will not fully bend into a fist or straighten
- A weak grip, and swelling of the hand or fingers
- A tight or sensitive scar after an operation

## homeCare
- Follow your fracture clinic's or surgeon's limits; wear the splint exactly as you were told, and do only the exercises given for your stage
- Do not grip, lift or test a repaired finger until your surgeon or hand therapist says you can
- Raise the hand when it is swollen, and keep the joints you are allowed to move moving several times a day
- Once the wound has healed and your surgeon or hand therapist agrees, gently massage the scar for a few minutes several times a day
- Only use a compression glove or wrap if your surgeon or hand therapist gave you one, and take it off if your fingers tingle, go pale or throb

## seePhysioIf
- You felt a snap or pop after a tendon repair, or the finger suddenly cannot bend or straighten as it did: contact your surgeon or hand clinic today
- The wound is red, hot, leaking or smells, or you have a fever: see a doctor today
- When you make a fist, a finger crosses over or points towards another: see your fracture clinic or doctor before starting exercises
- Burning pain, colour or temperature changes, or skin so sensitive that light touch hurts: see your doctor and book physiotherapy now
- You have had a tendon repair and have not yet started hand therapy: book within the first few days after surgery
- You have not been told whether you can start moving the hand: check with your fracture clinic first
- The fingers stay stiff, the grip weak, or the scar tight after the splint comes off

## clinicNotes
- Fractures (form 25; Hardy 2004 JOSPT; Skirven 2011): type, fixation, the surgeon's restrictions, healing on X-ray; oedema (circumference); AROM/PROM at each joint, TAM; malrotation; tendon glide and adhesion; intrinsic tightness (Bunnell-Littler); intrinsic-plus splinting (wrist 30 degrees extension, MCP 70 degrees flexion, IPs extended) 4-8 weeks; strengthen when healed; DASH/PRWHE. Differentials: CRPS, tendon adhesion, oedema and scar.
- Flexor tendon repair (form 31): zone (I-V), repair type and strand count, the surgeon's protocol; FDS/FDP isolation tests; dorsal blocking splint; early active or passive motion by repair strength (place-and-hold, tendon glides); adhesion vs rupture; protected 0-6 weeks, blocking and light grip 6-8 weeks, strengthening 8-12 weeks; TAM, grip, DASH/MHQ; jersey finger (FDP avulsion) needs surgery within days (injury screen I5) (Neiduski and Powell 2019; Novak and von der Heyde 2015).
- Extensor tendon repair (form 32): zones I-VIII; extensor lag at each joint; sagittal band; EPL (thumb retropulsion); splinting by zone (volar, dynamic, relative motion), short-arc motion; composite flexion and strengthening from 6-8 weeks; DASH (Newport and Tucker 2005). Mallet (zone I) stays on hand-fingerinjury.
- Nerve repair (form 35): median, ulnar or radial; regrowth about 1 mm a day over 12-24 months; protect numb skin; sensory re-education; anti-claw, opponens or dynamic extension splints; Tinel's advancing sign. "Ape hand", "claw hand" and "paralysed" stay out of patient text. A weakening hand without a known repair is doctor first (hnd-numb).
- Oedema and scar (form 36): figure-of-8 or volumetric measures; elevation, retrograde massage, light compression (Coban) once cleared; a rising swelling means re-check for infection or CRPS; scar massage, silicone, desensitisation once healed; scars mature over 6 to 12 months (Villeco 2012).
