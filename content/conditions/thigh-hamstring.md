## blurb
This is an injury to the muscle group at the back of your thigh, usually happening during a sprint, kick, or sudden change of direction. It causes a sharp pain right at the moment of injury and can leave the area sore and weak for days to weeks afterward.

## noticed
- A sudden, sharp pain at the back of the thigh, often during running or kicking (pain right at the sit bone with a large bruise needs checking quickly)
- Tenderness when pressing along the back of the thigh, and sometimes bruising or swelling
- Pain or tightness when trying to bend forward, stretch the leg straight, or push hard with that leg, plus a limp right after the injury

## homeCare
- Ease off sprinting, kicking, and hard running for the first few days, switching to gentle walking as comfort allows
- Apply ice and keep the leg gently moving within a pain-free range early on rather than staying completely still
- Gradually add gentle stretching and light strengthening (such as slow, controlled lowering exercises) over the following weeks, building back up to running and sport drills step by step

## seePhysioIf
- The pain isn't easing after a couple of weeks, or you're still limping when walking
- You've strained this area before and want a structured plan to get back to sport safely and lower the chance of it happening again
- A pop or tearing high in the back of the thigh or at the sit bone, with a large bruise, or a gap you can feel: see a doctor the same day, as a torn tendon is repaired early

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check your thigh and explain how bad the strain looks.
- A step-by-step strengthening programme, with gentle movement early on, can help the muscle heal.
- You get a gradual return-to-sport plan, with exercises that may help lower the chance of another strain.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 1 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Diagnosis (Martin 2022 CPG, B): sudden onset of posterior thigh pain, with pain on hamstring stretch or activation, palpation tenderness and loss of function. The CPG notes that evidence on the accuracy of the clinical exam is limited. Mechanism (Martin 2022): overloading in a lengthened position (high-speed running) – typically biceps femoris; overstretching with combined hip flexion and knee extension (kicking, reaching) – typically proximal semimembranosus. Impairment measures (Martin 2022, A): knee flexor strength by HHD or isokinetic dynamometer; hamstring length by inclinometer as knee extension deficit at 90° hip flexion. Descriptive grading in the CPG text by AKE deficit: <15° ≈25.9 days of rehab; 16–25° ≈30.7 days; 26–35° ≈75.0 days. CPA tests: passive SLR, adding tibial ER to bias the medial hamstrings and tibial IR to bias biceps femoris long head (MP 294); functional hamstring length in sitting (MP 295); resisted knee flexion prone at 80–90° with tibial IR (SM/ST) or ER (BF), less flexion if cramping (MP 277). Prognosis (Martin 2022): hamstring strength, pain at time of injury, days to pain-free walking (>1 day linked to longer RTP) and area of tenderness (B); length of tenderness and proximity to the ischial tuberosity (C). Recurrence 13.9–63.3% over the same and subsequent seasons. Outcomes (Martin 2022, B): FASH (Functional Assessment Scale for Acute Hamstring Injuries) before and after interventions; objective walk, run and sprint measures. Taking-off-the-shoe test: level IV study, 100% accurate vs US for grade I/II biceps femoris injuries (as reported in the CPG). Imaging and referral (Martin 2022 text): usually not needed for grade I–II; MRI recommended for suspected grade III, with referral to a physician; radiographs may rule out avulsion fracture with proximal symptoms. Surgery rarely indicated except for proximal or distal tendon avulsion (Lamplot 2016). CPG differentials: lumbar radiculopathy, SIJ, deep gluteal syndrome, ischial tunnel syndrome, adductor strain, contusion, compartment syndrome, thrombosis.
- Diagnosis (B): sudden posterior thigh pain during activity, reproduced by stretch and/or resisted contraction, with local tenderness and loss of function; sudden onset is the most useful history item. Sprint type (late swing) is usually biceps femoris; overstretch type (hip flexion with knee extension) usually proximal semimembranosus, with a longer recovery.
- Grading by active knee extension deficit (hip at 90 degrees): under 15 degrees about 26 days, 16-25 about 31 days, 26-35 about 75 days. Complete tear: diffuse bruising, palpable gap, cannot walk; refer suspected grade III or proximal tendon avulsion to a physician and imaging (radiographs for proximal symptoms). Imaging is not needed for grades I-II.
- Maximal tenderness at the origin or insertion suggests tendon pathology (outside this CPG); a direct blow suggests contusion; vague, insidious onset suggests lumbar referral. Differentials: lumbar radiculopathy, SIJ, deep gluteal and ischial tunnel syndromes, adductor strain, contusion, compartment syndrome, DVT, apophysitis, avulsion fracture.
- Exam: knee flexor strength by dynamometer (A); active knee extension by inclinometer (A); length of tenderness and distance from the ischial tuberosity (C; the closer, the longer the recovery); trunk and pelvic control (F). Outcome: FASH (B).
- Prognosis (B): previous hamstring injury is the biggest risk (re-injury within 8 weeks OR 13.1); also age over 23, previous ACL injury or calf strain. Estimate return from strength, pain at injury, days to pain-free walking and tenderness area.
- Treatment: eccentric training to tolerance added to stretching, strengthening, stabilisation and progressive running speeds (B); progressive agility and trunk stabilisation lowers re-injury (B: 7.7% vs 70%); neural mobilisation and early modalities (F). Prevention: the Nordic hamstring exercise within a multicomponent programme (A).
- Source: Martin RL et al., JOSPT 2022;52(3):CPG1-CPG44 (hamstring strain injury in athletes), grades in brackets.
