## blurb
After a broken or dislocated elbow, the elbow easily stiffens, especially in straightening. Early, guided movement within the limits your fracture clinic sets, then strengthening, brings most of the movement back. Progress continues for months.

## noticed
- The elbow will not fully straighten or bend, compared with the other side
- Weakness lifting, carrying and turning the forearm
- Aching after use, easing with rest

## homeCare
- Follow your fracture clinic's advice on the sling, splint or brace, for as long as you were told
- Move the elbow, wrist and fingers gently and often, within comfort
- Use the arm for light daily tasks as you are allowed; avoid forcing the elbow straight
- Avoid pushing up with the palm facing up, or leaning on a straight arm, until you are cleared

## seePhysioIf
- The hand becomes numb, tingly or weak, or the fingers will not straighten: see a doctor the same day
- The wound is red, hot or leaking, or you have a fever: see a doctor the same day
- The pain in the forearm is climbing and no longer helped by pain relief, with the forearm tight and swollen: go to the emergency department now
- Movement has stopped improving, or the elbow is getting stiffer
- You would like a structured movement and strengthening programme
- Your elbow is stiff or weak after the cast or splint comes off
- After a dislocation, starting guided movement in the first week or two can help limit stiffness
- The elbow clunks or feels like it could slip out when you push up or lean on it: please book promptly; a surgeon's opinion may be needed if it keeps happening
- A hard lump is forming in the muscle and the elbow is getting stiffer: tell your physiotherapist or doctor
- A cast or splint keeps getting tighter: get it checked the same day

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check your elbow and work within the limits your fracture clinic has set.
- Guided movement, then strengthening, aims to bring back bending, straightening and arm strength.
- You get a step-by-step plan back to daily tasks, work and sport as the bone heals.

## clinicNotes
- Early active movement after a simple dislocation gives better early function than plaster (FuncSiE, Iordens 2017). Post-traumatic stiffness: loss of extension most common; heterotopic ossification after severe injuries; ulnar nerve symptoms are common. Measure flexion-extension arc (functional 30-130 degrees), pronation-supination, QuickDASH or MEPS.
- After a dislocation that reduced: posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI) if clunking pushing up from a chair (chair and push-up signs); refer.
- Safety: compartment syndrome early (erf-compartment), tight cast (erf-cast), nerve injury (injury screen I7, I8), infection, CRPS.
- Sources: FuncSiE trial; BOAST 11; AIM Theory Manual 2023.
- Form 20 (19 Oct 2026): fracture type, fixation, surgeon's range and weight-bearing limits; accessory glides (ulnohumeral, radiohumeral, PRUJ) once cleared; static progressive or dynamic splinting for persistent stiffness; post-immobilisation capsular pattern (flexion lost more than extension): graded mobilisation, contract-relax, frequent self-assisted range (ACOEM 2013; Pike 2009; Nandi 2009).
- Dislocation (form 13): simple vs complex (terrible triad); neurovascular screen (ulnar, median, brachial artery); early active range in pronation within the stable arc; valgus, varus and PLRI tests after 3-6 weeks; hinged brace if lax; PSFS, DASH (Kuhn 2008; Fusaro 2014). PLRI (form 12): lateral pivot shift awake Sn about 38%, chair push-up and tabletop relocation tests (O'Driscoll 1991), brace in pronation with an extension block early; surgical opinion if clunking persists (elbow-lcl). A reduced dislocation can leave a non-capsular, single-direction restriction from ligament adhesion (form 17): check stability first.
- Myositis ossificans / heterotopic ossification (form 7): a firm tender mass 2-4 weeks after the injury with progressive loss of extension; X-ray from about 3-4 weeks; avoid frictions and forced stretching; refer.
