## blurb
Osteoporosis means the bones have become less dense and less strong than expected for your age, so they can break with a smaller force than usual: a fall from standing height, or in the spine, a bend, a lift or a cough. On its own it causes no pain; the pain people notice usually comes from a fracture, most often a squashed (compressed) vertebra in the mid back. Bone is living tissue that keeps responding to loading throughout life. A spinal compression fracture usually settles over several weeks, and with the right exercise, movement habits and fall-prevention plan most people keep doing the things that matter to them.

## doctorFirst
If this pain started suddenly after a small movement, a cough or a minor fall, please see your doctor in the next few days for an examination and an X-ray before starting treatment. Physiotherapy can then help you with a safe movement and exercise plan.

## noticed
- Sudden, sharp pain in one spot in the middle of the back after a small load: lifting a grocery bag, bending to make the bed, coughing or sneezing, or a minor stumble; sometimes no clear cause
- The spot is tender to press, and the pain can wrap around the ribs like a band
- Worse standing or walking for long, sitting upright without support, bending forward, lifting, coughing, and rolling over or getting out of bed
- Easier lying flat on your back, or on your side with your knees bent
- Later, a dull ache from back muscles working harder to hold your posture
- Some spinal fractures cause little pain and show only as height loss or a more stooped posture

## homeCare
- Keep moving in short, frequent bouts; long rest in bed tends to make back pain and stiffness worse
- Lift and bend from the hips and knees, keep the load close to your body, and avoid heavy lifting and fast twisting while the back is sore
- Make your home fall-safe: clear floors and cords, good lighting (especially at night), non-slip mats, rails on stairs and in the bathroom, well-fitting shoes
- Eat calcium-rich foods daily and ask your doctor about a vitamin D supplement; limit alcohol and do not smoke

## seePhysioIf
- You have been told you have osteoporosis or low bone density, have broken a bone after a minor fall, have lost height, or are unsure about your balance: an assessment can help you build a safe, progressive exercise and fall-prevention plan
- After a spinal fracture, once your doctor has checked you: for pain relief, safe ways to move, and strength and balance training
- If sudden mid-back pain started after a small movement and you are over 50, see your doctor first for an X-ray, then book
- If your thin bones or a fracture have no obvious cause, ask your doctor whether your vitamin D, calcium and parathyroid hormone have been checked (a simple blood test)

## clinicNotes
- Unexplained osteoporosis or fragility fracture: check calcium and PTH (NICE NG132; "Hyperparathyroidism" document, signed 3 Oct 2026, content/reference/hyperparathyroidism.md), and 25-OH vitamin D, phosphate and ALP: a low DXA can be osteomalacia, and antiresorptives before vitamin D repletion are inappropriate ("Osteomalacia" document, content/reference/osteomalacia.md). PHPT doubles forearm and triples spine fracture risk; FRAX underestimates it.
- Refer-first when a fresh vertebral fracture is possible: GP for thoracolumbar X-ray and DXA. Osteoporosis Canada 2023: BMD for everyone 70 or over, and 50-69 with a risk factor; a hip or vertebral fracture after 50 generally meets the threshold to discuss medication. Co-manage calcium, vitamin D and medication with the GP.
- Assess: fragility-fracture history; measured vs recalled height (2 cm or more measured, or 6 cm or more historical, loss suggests vertebral fracture); occiput-to-wall and rib-to-pelvis distance (kyphosis); midline percussion tenderness; neuro screen (myotomes, dermatomes, reflexes, gait); falls (TUG, 30-s chair stand, 4-stage balance or single-leg stance); fracture risk (CAROC or FRAX) with the GP.
- Manage (Too Fit to Fracture, Giangregorio 2014; Gibbs 2019 Cochrane): early, positions of ease, pacing, walking, gentle hands-on care; spine-sparing movement (hip hinge, loads close, log roll); progressive strength at least twice a week including the back extensors and trunk; balance and functional training at least twice a week; posture and breathing work for kyphosis; home and footwear check.
- Avoid in known or suspected osteoporosis: thrust manipulation of the spine or ribs, forceful end-range mobilisation, and loaded or repeated end-range spinal flexion and twisting (Too Fit to Fracture).
- Risk factors (AIM manual pp. 622-623): fixed, age over 50, female, family history (parent with hip fracture), previous fracture (risk up 86%, Kanis 2004), menopause or oophorectomy, long-term corticosteroids (30-50% fracture in their lifetime), RA and endocrine disease, male hypogonadism; modifiable, alcohol (4 or more drinks a day doubles hip fracture risk), smoking, low body weight or weight loss, eating disorders, low vitamin D and calcium, inactivity, falls. Also anticonvulsants, some hormonal treatments and antidepressants; hyperthyroidism, coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis, Cushing's; relative energy deficiency in young athletes.
- Look-alikes: mechanical mid-back pain (one-sided, eases with movement and heat, no risk factors); early shingles (burning band, rash in days); cancer or myeloma (night pain, weight loss, past cancer); inflammatory back pain (under 45, long morning stiffness, better with exercise).
- Hip fracture link (McDonough 2021, JOSPT 51(2)): over 90% of hip fractures follow a fall, usually sideways, and fall risk predicts fracture independently of bone density; 4-8% have another fracture within a year. Assess and document fall risk (A) and make sure osteoporosis is assessed and treated after a hip fracture (F). See content/reference/hip-fracture.md.
- Sources: Morin 2023 (Osteoporosis Canada guideline, CMAJ 195:E1333); Giangregorio 2014 (Too Fit to Fracture, Osteoporos Int 25:821); Gibbs 2019 (Cochrane CD008618); Kanis 2004-2005; AIM Theory Manual 2023 pp. 621-625.
