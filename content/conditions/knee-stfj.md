## blurb
A small joint sits on the outer side of the knee, where the top of the thin outer shin bone (the fibula) meets the main shin bone. It moves a little with every knee and ankle movement, and it can become stiff, sore or too loose, often after an ankle sprain or a misstep, giving pain on the bony bump below the outer knee. It is easy to overlook but simple to check by hand.

## noticed
- A sore spot on the bony bump on the outer side, just below the knee
- Often started after an ankle sprain, a twist, or stepping unexpectedly off a curb
- A tight feeling in the outer hamstring at the back of the thigh
- Brought on by running, squatting, twisting, rising up on your toes, or hamstring curls

## homeCare
- Ease off twisting on the leg and deep squats while it is sore
- Heat or ice may help ease soreness
- Stay active in ways that do not bring on the pain, such as stationary cycling or pool exercise
- If the back of the thigh feels tight, gentle hamstring stretches held for 15 to 30 seconds may help; stop if they bring on tingling in the leg

## seePhysioIf
- Pain on the outer knee keeps coming back, especially after an ankle sprain or misstep
- You would like the joint, the outer hamstring and the nerve around the knee checked
- Your foot has become weak (it catches, drops or slaps when you walk), or the outer shin or top of the foot is numb: see a doctor the same day
- After a fall, twist or blow, the bump is very sore to press or looks out of place: see a doctor the same day, as an X-ray may be needed

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 26 Oct 2026 (in chat; written from the knee document form 11) -->
- A physiotherapist can gently move this small joint, compare it with the other knee and explain what is going on.
- Gentle joint techniques and muscle work for the outer hamstring may help it move normally again.
- Strength and balance exercises for the whole leg help keep the joint well supported.

## clinicNotes
- No CPA Theory chapter covers it (Ch. 2.7 names it only as an ITBS look-alike, p. 448); content from the Lower Quadrant Clinical Manual Ch. 6 L2/L3 STFJ (pp. 449-456, 467-470). No diagnostic accuracy data exist for any STFJ test. No CPG exists (searched 26 Oct 2026).
- Observation: fibular head position A/P and S/I, local swelling, biceps femoris tone. Mobility: no independent physiological motion; assess via ankle PF/DF, inversion/eversion and knee flex/ext and IR/ER while palpating; functional: standing body twist, heel/toe raise, squat. Cyriax capsular pattern: pain with biceps femoris contraction. Accessory glides at about 30 degrees: anterolateral and posteromedial.
- Stability: anterior and posterior fibular head translation in crook lying, knee 90 degrees; normal up to about 3 mm (Scott 2007); positive = increased mobility, abnormal end feel and/or clunk. Provocation: compression of the fibular head into the tibia (avoid the CPN).
- Muscles: biceps femoris length, strength and tone (hypertonic with L5, S1 or S2 facilitation: screen the lumbar spine); soleus, tibialis posterior, peroneus longus, EDL. Neural: the common peroneal nerve is often injured in STFJ injuries; check DF/eversion strength and sensation.
- Thrust restriction patterns (p. 467): hamstring facilitation holds the head posterior; a lateral ankle sprain pulls it anterior; forced eversion pushes it posterior. Safety: reduced peroneal mobility or raised mechanosensitivity; avoid CPN compression; if lax after manipulation, refer to the doctor.
- After acute trauma, fibular head tenderness is an Ottawa criterion: X-ray first (injury screen I4).
