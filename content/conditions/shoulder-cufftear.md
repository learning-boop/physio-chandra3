## blurb
One of the tendons that lift and turn the arm can tear through, either suddenly after a fall or a heavy pull, or gradually with age, often in people over 60 and sometimes without much pain. The main sign is weakness: the arm is hard to lift or turn outwards. A sudden tear is best checked by a doctor early, as a repair, if needed, works best within a few weeks; a gradual tear in an older adult often does well with exercise.

## noticed
- Weakness lifting the arm out or up, or turning it outwards, sometimes with the arm only going up when helped
- Pain in the outer upper arm, worse at night or lying on that side
- Either a clear start (a fall, a heavy pull, a dislocation) or a slow build-up over months

## homeCare
- Keep the arm moving gently within comfort, supported if needed
- Avoid heavy lifting with the arm away from the body for now
- Sleep with a pillow supporting the arm

## seePhysioIf
- After a fall, a heavy pull or a dislocation, your arm became much weaker or you cannot lift it: see a doctor within a few days
- The arm will not lift on its own but goes up when you help it: see a doctor or physiotherapist within a week
- The shoulder is hot, red or swollen, or you have a fever: see a doctor the same day
- Pain that comes on with effort, chest tightness or breathlessness: call 911
- Weakness that came on slowly: a physiotherapy programme is a good first step, with an ultrasound if it is not improving

## clinicNotes
- Clusters (AIM manual p.286): painful arc + drop arm + external rotation weakness, +LR 15.6; age over 65 + external rotation weakness + night pain, +LR 9.8. External rotation lag sign and drop arm for full-thickness supraspinatus and infraspinatus tears; belly press and lift-off for subscapularis.
- Traumatic tear (fall, heavy pull, first dislocation over 40): ultrasound or MRI and early orthopaedic review; repair within about 3 weeks gives lower retear rates and better function (meta-analysis 2025), though one cohort found no difference at 3 months. BESS/BOA 2015 lists an acute traumatic tear as needing urgent referral.
- Symptomatic atraumatic degenerative tear (SADRC, age over 40-60): rehabilitation first (AIM manual pp.287-292 progression, RCP1; AAOS 2025: physical therapy is meaningful for symptomatic full-thickness tears; supervised not better than unsupervised); refer if not improving at 12 weeks or with pseudoparalysis, progressive weakness or a young active patient.
- Differentials: rotator cuff tendinopathy or partial tear, nerve problems (axillary, suprascapular, neuralgic amyotrophy, C5 radiculopathy), frozen shoulder, glenohumeral OA.
- Sources: AIM Theory Manual 2023 Ch. 2.2 pp.284-292; Kulkarni R et al. (BESS/BOA), Shoulder Elbow 2015; AAOS rotator cuff CPG 2025; early-repair meta-analysis 2025 (PMC12347289); Chandra's pre-operative rotator cuff protocol.
