## blurb
One of the tendons that lift and turn the arm can tear through, either suddenly after a fall or a heavy pull, or gradually with age, often in people over 60 and sometimes without much pain. The main sign is weakness: the arm is hard to lift or turn outwards. A sudden tear is best checked by a doctor early, as a repair, if needed, works best within a few weeks; a gradual tear in an older adult often does well with exercise. Many people over 60 have a tear on a scan and feel nothing; when one becomes painful, it is usually because the shoulder was asked to do more than it was ready for.

## noticed
- Weakness lifting the arm out or up, or turning it outwards, sometimes with the arm only going up when helped
- Pain in the outer upper arm, worse at night or lying on that side
- Either a clear start (a fall, a heavy pull, a dislocation) or a slow build-up over months
- Shrugging the shoulder up to get the arm lifted

## homeCare
- Keep the arm moving gently within comfort, supported if needed
- Avoid heavy lifting with the arm away from the body for now
- Lift with your elbow close to your body and your palm up where you can
- Break overhead jobs into shorter spells rather than stopping them
- If the arm is in a sling for comfort, keep the hand, wrist and elbow moving
- Sleep on the other side with a pillow supporting the sore arm

## seePhysioIf
- After a fall, a heavy pull or a dislocation, your arm became much weaker or you cannot lift it: see a doctor within a few days
- The arm will not lift on its own but goes up when you help it: see a doctor or physiotherapist within a week
- The shoulder is hot, red or swollen: see a doctor the same day; with a fever or feeling unwell, go to the emergency department now
- Pain that comes on with effort, chest tightness or breathlessness: call 911
- Numbness, pins and needles or weakness spreading down the arm or into the hand: see a doctor
- Weakness that is getting worse week by week: see a doctor
- Weakness that came on slowly: physiotherapy is a good first step; if it is not improving after 4 to 6 weeks, see your doctor, who may suggest an ultrasound

- A physiotherapist can check your shoulder strength and help you know if a doctor should see it soon.
- A guided strengthening programme can help many people with a gradual tear lift and use the arm again.
- You get a step-by-step plan for daily tasks, with regular checks on how you are progressing.

## clinicNotes
- Clusters (AIM manual p.286): painful arc + drop arm + external rotation weakness, +LR 15.6; age over 65 + external rotation weakness + night pain, +LR 9.8. External rotation lag sign and drop arm for full-thickness supraspinatus and infraspinatus tears; belly press and lift-off for subscapularis.
- Traumatic tear (fall, heavy pull, first dislocation over 40): ultrasound or MRI and early orthopaedic review; repair within about 3 weeks gives lower retear rates and better function (meta-analysis 2025), though one cohort found no difference at 3 months. BESS/BOA 2015 lists an acute traumatic tear as needing urgent referral.
- Symptomatic atraumatic degenerative tear (SADRC, age over 40-60): rehabilitation first (AIM manual pp.287-292 progression, RCP1; AAOS 2025: physical therapy is meaningful for symptomatic full-thickness tears; supervised not better than unsupervised); refer if not improving at 12 weeks or with pseudoparalysis, progressive weakness or a young active patient.
- Partial-thickness degenerative tears are managed on the rotator cuff tendinopathy pathway (JOSPT 2025, shoulder-rc).
- Observation (form 1): thoracic kyphosis, scapular downward rotation or anterior tilt, anterior humeral head, wasting in the infraspinatus fossa; hitching on elevation; limited ER at the side. Traumatic (form 3): hornblower's, IR lag and belly-press tests; axillary nerve screen; X-ray to exclude fracture or an unreduced dislocation.
- Rehabilitation (form 1): RCP1 six-phase programme (Bleichert 2017): scapular setting, humeral head control, graded range, loaded strengthening, activity-specific work. Most atraumatic full-thickness tears avoid surgery at 2 years (MOON cohort, Kuhn 2013; Kukkonen 2014 RCT); small and medium traumatic tears: Moosmayer 2010. Prevalence rises each decade (Yamamoto 2010). Pain and function often improve by 6-12 weeks; full rehabilitation 12-18 months. Measures: WORC (MCID 11.7%), PSFS.
- Differentials: rotator cuff tendinopathy or partial tear, nerve problems (axillary, suprascapular, neuralgic amyotrophy, C5 radiculopathy), frozen shoulder, glenohumeral OA.
