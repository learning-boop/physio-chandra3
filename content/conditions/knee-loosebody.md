## blurb
Sometimes a small piece of cartilage or bone comes free inside the knee joint, often after an injury such as a kneecap dislocation. It can move around and now and then get caught between the joint surfaces, giving a sudden sharp pain that stops you mid-step, a jamming feeling, or the knee giving way. Between catches the knee often moves fully and feels close to normal.

## noticed
- Sudden, severe, sharp twinges in the knee that stop you mid-step
- The knee may give way with the pain, then feel fine again
- The knee has locked and freed up when you shook or wiggled it
- More likely after a kneecap dislocation, a cartilage injury, or osteochondritis dissecans

## homeCare
- If the knee catches, gently shaking it or easing it into a comfortable position may help it free up; do not force it
- Avoid sharp twisting and deep squatting on that knee for now
- Stationary cycling or exercise in a pool can keep you active

## seePhysioIf
- Your knee is catching, locking or giving way with sharp pain: a physiotherapy assessment can help, and can tell you whether a medical opinion is needed
- The catching or locking keeps coming back: your doctor can arrange imaging and a specialist opinion
- The knee is locked and will not free up or straighten: see a doctor the same day
- The pain is there all the time rather than in sudden catches: see us or your doctor, as something else may be going on

## physioHelps

- A physiotherapist can talk through your catching episodes, check that the knee is stable and explain what may be happening.
- With your consent, a specific hands-on technique that gently pulls and turns the knee may help free a catching knee.
- Exercises keep the knee moving and strong, and we can tell you if you need to see a doctor for imaging.

## clinicNotes
- Presentation (Lower Quadrant Clinical Manual Ch. 6 pp. 413-414): severe sudden twinges, worse with walking; sudden halt and giving way, with each step or inconsistent; generally full ROM unless locked; springy or early capsular end feel; may shift to a non-blocking position (no twinges, discomfort after a long walk); previous locking unlocked by shaking the knee.
- Sources of a fragment: osteochondral lesions or loose bodies in about a third after patellar dislocation (Vetrano 2017; Theory p. 489); chondral and osteochondral lesions (haemarthrosis at 0-2 h with an osteochondral fracture; Theory p. 503); knee OCD at 12-19 y can lock acutely with loose bodies: orthopaedic referral (Theory p. 538).
- Loose body manipulation (manual pp. 413-415): for articular restriction of flexion or extension; seated at the end of the plinth, patient stabilising the femur; traction plus tibial rotation to the most comfortable rotation, slack to R2, pre-manipulative hold, consent, then strong traction alternating rotation, taking the knee away from the blocked direction (prone alternative). Before: neural mobility (slump, SLR), tibiofemoral stability (cruciates), pain on the hold, compromised articular surfaces; rule out ligament, meniscal and fracture causes. After: reassess, tape or brace if needed, warn of soreness, recommend imaging and possibly an orthopaedic opinion.
- No physiotherapy CPG exists (searched 26 Oct 2026); the JOSPT meniscal and articular cartilage CPG 2018 criteria help screen the fragment's source.
