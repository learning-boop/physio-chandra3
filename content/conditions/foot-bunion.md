## blurb
A bunion is a bony bump on the inside of the big toe joint, with the big toe leaning towards the others. It develops slowly, often runs in families, and is made more painful by narrow or high-heeled shoes rubbing on the bump. Footwear, toe spacers and exercises can ease the pain but change the shape very little; surgery is the option if the pain stays high.

## noticed
- A bump on the inside of the big toe joint
- The big toe leaning towards the second toe
- Sore where shoes rub, sometimes red over the bump

## homeCare
- Wear shoes with a wide, deep toe box and a low heel
- Toe spacers or bunion pads can ease rubbing
- Keep the big toe moving with gentle exercises

## seePhysioIf
- The big toe joint is suddenly hot, red and very painful: see a doctor the same day; with a fever or feeling unwell, go to the emergency department now
- The bump is very red and swollen, or the skin over it breaks or blisters, especially with diabetes: see a doctor the same day
- Pain is limiting walking or shoe choice
- The toe is crossing over or under the second toe
- You want footwear advice, or are considering surgery

## physioHelps

- A physiotherapist can look at your foot and explain in plain words what the bunion means for you.
- Footwear advice, toe spacers and toe and foot exercises can help ease the pain.
- You get a plan to stay active, and advice on when a surgeon's opinion may be worth considering.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 17 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Definition and epidemiology (Theory p. 533): metatarsus primus varus, abducted phalanx and often bunion; OA often develops. Prevalence 23% aged 18–65 (Roddy 2011); 35% of women >65 (Nix 2012). Pathomechanics (Reid 1991, Theory p. 533): MTP angle >20°; intermetatarsal angle >8° = metatarsus primus varus; MTP angle correlates with forefoot width and medial sesamoid position; medial collateral capsule attenuates; abductor hallucis brevis lengthened and weak, adductor hallucis short and hypertonic; insufficient tib post–peroneus longus force couple → increased, prolonged pronation; bunion (dorsomedial exostosis, bursitis, callus); lateral subluxation of the sesamoids, FHL and extensor tendons. Risk factors (Roddy 2011, Theory p. 533): typically inherited, adult onset (Magee 2009); longer 2nd MT; first-ray hypermobility and pronated foot; narrow or high-heeled shoes; inconsistent BMI association (lower prevalence with higher BMI in women, none in men). Measurement (Theory p. 534): X-ray is the standard (angle of deviation, exostosis). Digital photographs are a good alternative – inter- and intra-rater ICC >0.96, validity Pearson r = .96 (Nix 2012); a repeat study showed the same reliability but consistently underestimated the radiographic angle by 5.3° (Yamaguchi 2019, self-photography). Forefoot exam (MP 525–530): toe position WB and NWB; abductor hallucis most often lengthened and weak, indicated by a valgus 1st MTP (MP 529); adductor hallucis length (great toe MTP abduction/varus); resisted abductor and adductor hallucis, FHL, FHB; MTP dorsal/plantar and medial/lateral glides; Level 3 first ray and hallux functional stability test – note ↑ WB on the medial 1st MTP (MP 548). Foot posture and intrinsics: FPI-6 (Table 7-6: intra-rater ICC 0.75–0.99, inter-rater 0.53–0.66; side-to-side difference ≤2 points normal; MP 522); navicular drop (MP 524); intrinsic foot muscle test (Table 7-5: intra-rater kappa −0.03 to 0.41, inter-rater 0.25 to 0.60). Treatment (Theory p. 534): strengthen abductor hallucis brevis and FHL; lengthen adductor hallucis and EHL; FHL strengthening, gait retraining (dynamic arch control) and sesamoid mobilisation beneficial in a small study (Shamus 2004 – a hallux limitus trial); medial and plantar MTP glides for medial capsular mobility; pain menu (joint traction, trigger point/soft tissue to foot and distal leg, heat/cold); adduction bracing at night or rest to unload the joint and stretch adductor hallucis (Plaass 2019); toe spacer for daily use and walking; orthotics for load transfer and alignment; surgery when conservative care does not address pain and function. JOSPT muscle-strengthening case report linked (Ward 2016). Short foot exercise progression: sitting → bipedal → single-leg → functional → hops (MP 543).
- Prevalence about 23% at ages 18-65 and about 35% in women over 65 (AIM manual). Conservative care: toe separators, splints and exercise give small, low-quality reductions in the hallux valgus angle (2021 network meta-analysis, PMC8038851); footwear for pain. A bunion shifts the sesamoids laterally.
