## blurb
This is a stretch or tear of the ligament on the outer side of your knee, typically from a force pushing the knee outward or a direct blow to the inner leg.

## noticed
- Pain and tenderness along the outer side of the knee after an injury
- Localized puffiness right over the outer knee
- The knee gives way, or feels like it bends backwards or bows outward when you walk

## homeCare
- Keep walking and moving the knee within comfort; ice can help in the first couple of days
- Ease off movements that push the knee outward or twist it
- If an outer-corner injury is suspected, a physiotherapist can check whether a hinged brace is needed
- Gradually rebuild movement and strength as symptoms ease

## seePhysioIf
- Your knee bends backwards or gives way when you stand or walk after the injury: get it checked within a few days, as some outer-knee injuries need an early surgeon's opinion
- Since the injury, your foot is weak or your toes catch when you walk: see a doctor today
- Your foot is cold, pale or numb, or the knee looks out of shape: go to the emergency department now
- The bony bump on the outer side just below the knee is very sore to press: an X-ray is needed first
- A milder sprain is not settling after a couple of weeks, or the knee feels loose or shifts to the side during activity

## clinicNotes
- Diagnosis (ligament CPG 2017): varus force; local swelling and tenderness over the LCL; lateral pain and laxity on varus stress at 0 and 30 degrees. LCL injury is the least common knee ligament injury (incidence about 4%, CPG). Suspect a posterolateral corner (PLC) injury with hyperextension plus varus or a posterolateral blow to the medial tibia near extension; it can be part of a knee dislocation. Dial test and external rotation recurvatum for the PLC (not in the CPG).
- Neurovascular check: peroneal nerve injury occurs in about 13-44% of PLC or multiligament injuries (PMC8475172; not stated in the CPG). Ottawa fibular head tenderness (CPG p.a16; fibular head avulsion, the arcuate sign) is checked in clinic.
- Acute PLC repair or reconstruction within about 3 weeks does better than chronic reconstruction (surgical case series, e.g. Stannard 2005; the CPG reports acute versus chronic outcomes, p.a9): refer suspected PLC injuries early.
- Bracing for posterolateral corner injury (F); neuromuscular re-education with strengthening (A); measures as for MCL (B). Differentials (clinical reasoning, not from the CPG): ITB syndrome in runners, lateral meniscus, superior tibiofibular joint. The AIM manual has no LCL section (PLC with ACL tears, p.57).
