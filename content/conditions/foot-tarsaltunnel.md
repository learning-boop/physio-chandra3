## blurb
A nerve running behind the inner ankle bone can become squeezed, sending burning or tingling sensations into the sole of the foot.

## noticed
- Burning, tingling, or numbness along the inner ankle or bottom of the foot
- Symptoms that may worsen with standing or at night
- A different feel to typical heel pain, more electrical or nerve-like than achy

## homeCare
- Choose supportive shoes that don't press on the inner ankle
- Rest from activities that bring on the tingling
- Gentle, pain-free ankle movement through the day
- Supportive shoes, or an arch support if your feet are flat

## seePhysioIf
- Numbness or burning in both feet, or it is spreading up the legs: see your doctor
- The toes or foot are getting weak or thinner, or there is a lump or swelling behind the inner ankle bone: see a doctor
- Tingling, numbness, or burning hasn't settled within a couple of weeks
- The sensations are spreading or becoming more intense

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check whether a squeezed nerve at the inner ankle fits your symptoms.
- Footwear or arch support advice and gentle ankle and nerve movement can help ease the pressure.
- You get a plan to pace your activity, and a referral if checks show it is needed.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 15 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Features (Theory pp. 568–569): posterior tibial nerve compression in the tarsal tunnel (flexor retinaculum, medial calcaneus, posterior talus, medial malleolus); burning, numbness and tingling in the posteromedial ankle, heel and sole (medial/lateral plantar nerves) ± toes; possible local tenderness behind the medial malleolus; worse with standing, walking, running and forced DF + eversion; toe abductor/flexor weakness or atrophy in severe or chronic cases. Higher prevalence in women, any age (Llanos 1999). Dorsiflexion-eversion test (Kinoshita 2001; MP 509): knee flexed past 20°, maximal ankle DF + maximal eversion + passive extension of all MTPs; positive = reproduction of usual local neurogenic symptoms. 18% of 44 surgically treated ankles tested negative; none of 100 normal feet tested positive. Theory p. 569: Sn 25% (increase in numbness), 57% (increase in pain), 98% (increase in tenderness); Sp 100%. A positive Tinel plus local tenderness over the tibial nerve in the test position confirmed the diagnosis; MTP extension ROM as outcome measure (Alshami 2007). Triple compression stress test: Sn 85.9%, Sp 100% (Abouelela & Zohiery 2012; Theory p. 569). Tinel test (MP 508): light percussion over the tibial nerve posterior to the medial malleolus; Sn 17%, Sp 81% vs nerve conduction (Datema 2016); considerable intra- and inter-rater variability in force (Lifchez 2010); validity and reliability reported as poor. A positive Tinel predicted long-term (3.5 yr) post-surgical relief and recovery of plantar sensation in 628 diabetes patients with chronic tibial nerve compression (Dellon 2012). Neural mobility: tibial, medial calcaneal, medial and lateral plantar nerves (MP 502, 512); LQ scan incl. lumbar ROM, repeated movements and SLR with DF + eversion bias to rule out lumbar radiculopathy (Theory p. 564). Neural injury can follow grade II–III sprains (McKeon 2012; Theory p. 528). Screen (Theory pp. 563–565): peripheral neuropathy, nerve entrapment elsewhere, neuroma, vascular causes, tumour; plantar fasciopathy (CPG criterion: negative tarsal tunnel tests); FDL, FHL and tibialis posterior tendinopathy (tenderness behind the medial malleolus without neurogenic symptoms; resisted toe flexion, inversion/PF). External evidence (Vij 2022 systematic review: 3 conservative, 2 minimally invasive and 14 surgical studies; no formal risk-of-bias assessment): conservative care (orthoses/arch supports, physiotherapy with stretching and strengthening, rest, medicines or injection via the physician) can resolve symptoms in many; surgery reserved for refractory cases. Neural mobilisation is listed as an L2/L3 treatment option for reduced neural mobility and tarsal tunnel syndrome (MP 540, 549, 563).
- Heel pain CPG 2014 and 2023: negative tarsal tunnel, nerve tension and sensory tests are required to classify plantar fasciitis (B); a positive result redirects to nerve entrapment, and lumbopelvic referral must also be excluded (F). Source: Martin RL et al., JOSPT 2014; Koc TA et al., JOSPT 2023.
- Tests: Tinel's behind the medial malleolus; dorsiflexion-eversion test (Kinoshita 2001); triple compression test (AIM manual p. 569: sensitivity 85.9%, specificity 100%, Abouelela 2012). Kinoshita figures not checked against the primary paper.
- Causes include a flat or collapsing foot (tibialis posterior), ganglion or other space-occupying lesions, and swelling after ankle injury (your protocol). Refer: progressive weakness or intrinsic wasting, a growing mass, both feet (neuropathy).
