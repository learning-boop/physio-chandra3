## blurb
This is an injury to one of the main ligaments deep inside the knee, usually from a sudden twist, pivot or awkward landing, and sometimes from a tackle or a blow. Many people do well with a structured exercise programme first; surgery is usually considered if the knee keeps giving way or you want to return to pivoting sport. A physiotherapist can talk through the options with you.

## noticed
- A twisting, pivoting or landing movement at the moment it happened, often without anyone touching you, or a tackle or blow to the knee
- A pop felt or heard at the time
- Fast swelling within the first couple of hours after the injury
- A feeling that the knee gives way, buckles or does not feel trustworthy, especially when changing direction

## homeCare
- From the first days, gently work on getting the knee fully straight and bending it as pain allows, and tighten the thigh muscle; ice and raising the leg can ease swelling
- Use crutches for the first few days if walking is painful or the knee gives way, and put weight through it as it allows
- Avoid sports with quick pivots, cutting or jumping until it has been checked
- If you play pivoting or jumping sports, a warm-up injury-prevention programme (such as FIFA 11+) done regularly through the season lowers the chance of knee injuries, including ACL injuries, especially for girls and young women

## seePhysioIf
- You heard or felt a pop, or the knee swelled within 2 hours of the injury: see a doctor the same day to rule out a break
- The knee is out of shape, or your foot is cold, pale or numb: go to the emergency department now
- You could not take 4 steps straight after the injury, cannot bend the knee to a right angle, or you are 55 or over: an X-ray is needed first, the same day
- Your knee is stuck and will not straighten fully: see a doctor soon, as it may need an early surgical opinion
- Your knee gave way or buckled after a twisting or landing injury, or keeps giving way: book a physiotherapy assessment

## clinicNotes
- Diagnosis (ligament CPG 2017): non-contact deceleration or cutting with valgus near extension (about 70%; about 30% contact, including a blow to the back of the knee, AIM manual p.55), a pop, swelling within 0-12 hours (haemarthrosis), giving way. Lachman sens 85%, spec 94%; pivot shift sens 24%, spec 98% (rules in); anterior drawer. The AIM manual's 2016 pooled figures (p.56): Lachman sens 0.87, spec 0.94; pivot shift sens 0.49, spec 0.98, +LR 16; anterior drawer sens 0.73, spec 0.93; lever sign close to 100% sensitive; in acute or partial tears Lachman 0.42, anterior drawer 0.29, pivot shift 0.11.
- Ottawa knee rule (CPG p.a16): age 55 or over, isolated kneecap tenderness, fibular head tenderness, cannot flex to 90 degrees, cannot bear weight for 4 steps. The patient screen asks only age, steps and flexion: palpate the kneecap and fibular head in clinic. Validated in adults (Bachmann 2004: sens 98.5%, -LR 0.05); the Pittsburgh rule under 12. Check MCL, menisci (medial meniscus involved in about 50%), the posterolateral corner, a Segond fracture, and the look-alikes: kneecap dislocation and osteochondral fracture (x-ray, AIM manual p.57).
- Instability markers: 6-m timed hop under 80% of the other leg, quadriceps index under 80%, giving way in daily activities. Measures (B): IKDC 2000 or KOOS, Tegner or Marx, hop tests, laxity, quadriceps strength, effusion (stroke test), ROM; ACL-RSI for fear of re-injury. Coper screening (timed hop, giving way, KOS-ADLS, global rating) changes with training.
- Treatment choice: neuromuscular re-education with strengthening (A); functional brace for ACL deficiency (C). Rehabilitation with optional delayed reconstruction matched early reconstruction at 2 and 5 years (Frobell) and at 11 years (KANON: similar KOOS4; 52% of the delayed group reconstructed; applies to young active adults, not professional athletes). Panther consensus 2020: rehab first is acceptable; reconstruction for persistent giving way or a return to pivoting sport; partial tears progress in about 39% of young pivoting athletes. ACL SNNAP (Lancet 2022): for non-acute injury with ongoing instability, surgery was better at 18 months.
- After reconstruction: motion within the first week (B), cryotherapy (B), early weight bearing as tolerated (C), supervised plus home programme (B), weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing strengthening 2-3 times a week from 4-6 weeks for 6-10 months (A), NMES for 6-8 weeks (A). Functional brace after reconstruction (D): elicit and document patient preferences, as evidence exists for and against.
- Return to sport (Delaware-Oslo, Grindem 2016): reinjury risk fell 51% for each month of delay up to 9 months; criteria of quadriceps and hop symmetry of 90% or more lowered reinjury. After reconstruction about 81% return to some sport, 65% to pre-injury level, 55% to competition (Ardern 2014). Second ACL injury: about 15% overall and 21-23% under 25 who return to sport (Wiggins); the AIM manual gives about 10%, doubling to 20% on return to sport, and one in four under 25 in pivoting sport. Fear and low self-efficacy predict non-return.
- Prevention (Arundale 2018; revision Arundale 2023, JOSPT 53(1), not yet checked against the 2018 grades): recommend an exercise-based programme (PEP, Sportsmetrics, HarmoKnee, Knakontroll for ACL; FIFA 11+ for all knee injuries) to all young athletes, not only those screened as high risk (A); multicomponent (strength, plyometrics, proximal control), graded A for programmes in women; more than once a week, over 20 minutes a session and more than 30 minutes a week, preseason and in season, with good adherence (A); balance training alone is not enough (B). Highest benefit: female athletes, especially under 18, and ages 12-25 in pivoting sports (A).
