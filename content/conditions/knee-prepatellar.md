## blurb
A small fluid sac sits in front of the kneecap to let the skin glide over it. A lot of kneeling, or a knock, can irritate it so that it fills with fluid into a soft swelling on the front of the kneecap. It is common in people who kneel for work. A hot, red or very tender swelling needs a doctor, as it may be infected, even without a fever.

## noticed
- A soft swelling on the front of the kneecap
- Tender to kneel on
- Often after a lot of kneeling, such as flooring, gardening, or cleaning, or after a knock

## homeCare
- Avoid kneeling on it; use thick knee pads if you must kneel
- A cold pack can ease the swelling
- Keep the knee moving normally

## seePhysioIf
- It becomes hot, red, or very tender, there is a cut or graze over it, the redness is spreading, or you feel unwell: see a doctor the same day
- The whole knee is swollen and it hurts to bend, rather than a lump on the front: see a doctor
- The swelling has not gone down after 2 to 3 weeks
- It keeps coming back with work

## clinicNotes
- Superficial bursa between the skin and the kneecap; repeated kneeling or a direct blow. The knee joint itself moves freely and there is no joint effusion (a joint effusion points to the joint, not the bursa).
- Septic bursitis: warmth, redness, peri-bursal tenderness, cellulitis, a skin breach; risk higher with diabetes, immunosuppression, alcohol use and kneeling occupations. Fever is present in fewer than half, and clinical features alone cannot exclude infection: refer for aspiration and culture if suspected (StatPearls, septic bursitis).
- Differential: infrapatellar bursitis ("clergyman's knee", below the kneecap over the tendon), gout or other crystal arthritis, joint effusion.
- Sources: StatPearls, Septic Bursitis (NBK557508).
