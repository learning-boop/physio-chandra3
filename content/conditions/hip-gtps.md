## blurb
The tendons of the buttock muscles, where they attach at the bony point of the outer hip, become sensitive to load and to pressure; a nearby fluid cushion (bursa) can be involved too. It is the classic 'can't lie on that side' hip, most common in women between 40 and 60, and in runners.

## noticed
- Pain over the outer hip bone, tender to lie on
- Worse with stairs, hills, standing on one leg
- Aches after long walking or standing
- Sometimes spreads down the outer thigh
- Stiff and sore getting up after sitting, or after sitting with legs crossed
- Sometimes a snap or clunk over the outer hip

## homeCare
- Avoid sustained hip 'hanging' postures (standing on one hip) and crossing legs
- Sleep on your back, or on your good side with a pillow between your knees and ankles
- Choose higher chairs, with your hips above your knees
- Avoid stretching across the outer hip (for example IT band stretches) or rolling hard on the sore spot: these press on the tendon
- Gradual gluteal strengthening — tendons here respond to load, not rest

## seePhysioIf
- Night pain on that side persists beyond 2 weeks
- Walking distance or stairs are limited
- You want a progressive loading program, the evidence-based treatment
- Deep pain at night that does not change with position, weight loss you cannot explain, or a past cancer: see your doctor
- A sudden pop at the outer hip, then a marked limp or you cannot lift the leg out to the side: see a doctor (possible tendon tear)
- You run or train hard and have a deep groin ache that is worse with hopping or aches at night: see a doctor before more running

## clinicNotes
- Lateral hip pain also fits the hip OA criteria (Koc 2025) and FAI (Enseki 2014): check IR at 90 degrees of flexion and FADIR before calling it isolated GTPS; concurrent OA is common over 50. Source: Koc 2025; Enseki 2014.
- Mainly a tendinopathy, not a bursitis (AIM manual p.376; Chandra's gluteal tendinopathy protocols); peak age 40-60, women up to 4 times more often (p.377). Kinsella 2024 (JOSPT meta-analysis): tenderness then pain on resisted abduction raises the probability from 59% to 96%, both negative lowers it to 14%. Palpation sens 80%, spec 47% (Grimaldi 2017; p.380). Lequesne 2008 single-leg stance and resisted de-rotation figures come from a small refractory cohort and overestimate accuracy. Outcome: VISA-G (Fearon 2015; the manual p.379 says there is no validated measure).
- Management: education plus a progressive loading programme beat a corticosteroid injection and wait-and-see at 8 and 52 weeks (LEAP, Mellor, BMJ 2018;361:k1662; exact percentages not verified); injection or focused shockwave as add-ons (Bremer 2025). Avoid adduction stretches and strong figure-4 stretches (p.382). External snapping hip belongs here (p.375).
- Protocol differences: the gluteal CPG summary calls ITB and piriformis stretching contraindicated with strong evidence (it rests on mechanism and expert opinion); the trochanteric bursitis protocol uses bursitis framing and ITB release, and the snapping hip protocol recommends ITB stretching, both against the gluteal protocols; some references could not be verified.
