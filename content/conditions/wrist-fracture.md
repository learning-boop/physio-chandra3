## blurb
After a broken wrist, whether treated in a cast or with surgery, the wrist and fingers are usually stiff and weak at first. Guided movement, then strengthening and a gradual return to daily tasks, bring most people back to good use of the hand over a few months.

## noticed
- A stiff wrist that will not bend or turn as far as the other one
- A weak grip, and aching after use
- Swelling of the hand and fingers, worse at the end of the day
- A firm stop when bending the wrist back or turning the palm up

## homeCare
- Follow your fracture clinic's advice on the cast, splint and lifting, for as long as you were told
- Keep the fingers, elbow and shoulder moving every day, even while in the cast
- Raise the hand when it swells, and use it for light tasks as you are allowed
- Build up gripping and taking weight through the hand gradually, as your fracture clinic allows
- Move the wrist gently to the end of its range several times a day: little and often works best
- Once the wound has healed and your surgeon or hand therapist agrees, gently massage the scar for a few minutes several times a day
- Only use a compression glove or wrap if your surgeon or hand therapist gave you one, and take it off if your fingers tingle, go pale or throb

## seePhysioIf
- The cast feels tighter and more painful, or the fingers are numb, pale or very swollen: see a doctor the same day
- Burning pain, colour or temperature changes, or skin so sensitive that light touch hurts: see your doctor and book physiotherapy now
- You are 50 or over: ask your doctor about a bone health check, as a broken wrist can be the first sign of thinner bones
- Movement or grip has not improved for 4 to 6 weeks: see your fracture clinic or doctor, and book physiotherapy
- Weeks after a broken wrist, you suddenly cannot lift your thumb up: see your doctor within a few days
- After the forearm was very swollen and painful, the fingers are being pulled into a bent position and will not straighten: see your surgeon or doctor soon, and book physiotherapy
- The hand stays puffy and stiff, or a scar is tight, raised or so sensitive that it stops you using the hand
- You would like a structured movement and strengthening programme

- Guided movement, then strengthening, aims to bring back wrist movement and grip strength.
- You get a gradual plan back to daily tasks, work and sport over the coming months.

## clinicNotes
- Distal radius fracture rehabilitation: early finger and shoulder ROM; wrist ROM and strengthening after immobilisation or as the surgeon allows; measure PRWE, grip, ROM. CRPS screen (W9). Acute carpal tunnel (injury screen I4).
- Bone health: a distal radius fracture at 50 or over warrants osteoporosis assessment (fracture liaison; PMC11427598).
- Scaphoid: a missed or non-united fracture causes persisting snuffbox pain after a fall (wrf-oldscaphoid); BSSH standards.
- Form 8 (20 Oct 2026): fracture type, fixation and surgeon's restrictions; oedema (circumference); wrist and forearm A/PROM; accessory glides once cleared; grip and pinch; joint position sense; monitor EPL rupture and CRPS; exercise and advice plus MWM after cast removal (Reid 2020, J Physiother 66:105); PRWHE (MCID 11.5), DASH. Sensorimotor deficits after healing (form 5; Karagiannopoulos 2013): grip, pinch, endurance, muscle length, joint position sense, as treatment targets.
- Capsular pattern after immobilisation (form 6): flexion and extension limited about equally; capsular end-feel; radiocarpal, midcarpal and DRUJ glides, MWM; post-traumatic OA: X-ray for SLAC/SNAC (Weiss and Rodner 2007; Magee 2014). Non-capsular pattern (form 7): one direction or an unusual combination after injury; carpal accessory glides; ligament stability tests (Skirven 2011). Medication and injection are the doctor's, not patient text.
- Scaphoid (form 9): snuffbox and tubercle tenderness and longitudinal thumb compression, all three positive Sn 100%, Sp 74% (Parvizi 1998; Mallee 2014); X-ray negative with positive signs: immobilise and re-X-ray at 2 weeks, or MRI/CT; wrf-oldscaphoid now also asks after a normal first X-ray.
- Volkmann's ischaemic contracture (form 11): a sequel of unrelieved forearm compartment syndrome; finger extension limited and improved with wrist flexion; Tsuge grades; median and ulnar exam; serial or dynamic extension splinting; surgical referral (Stevanovic and Sharpe 2006). Fear words ("permanent", "claw") stay out of patient text.
- Nerve injury with fracture (form 35): median (thenar, opposition), ulnar (interossei, Froment's), radial (wrist and finger extensors); regrowth about 1 mm a day; splints; protect numb skin; sensory re-education; NCS (Novak and von der Heyde 2015). Oedema and scar (form 36): figure-of-8 or volumetric measures; elevation, retrograde massage, light compression once cleared; a rising swelling means re-check for infection or CRPS; scar massage, silicone, desensitisation; scars mature over 6 to 12 months (Villeco 2012). Intrinsic tightness (form 34): Bunnell-Littler, ORL and extrinsic tests.
