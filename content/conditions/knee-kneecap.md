## blurb
This happens when the kneecap shifts partly or fully out of its groove, usually to the outside, often with the knee bent and twisting inward over a planted foot. It is most common in teenagers and young adults, especially girls, and it often pops back by itself.

## noticed
- A feeling that the kneecap has shifted, popped, or moved out to the side, then gone back
- Quick swelling after it happened, and tenderness on the inner side of the kneecap
- The knee giving way when twisting or pivoting, and feeling nervous when the kneecap is pushed sideways

## homeCare
- Avoid twisting or pivoting movements that set it off while it settles
- Once things settle, gentle strengthening of the thigh and hip muscles can help
- Ease back into sport gradually and with controlled movements

## seePhysioIf
- The kneecap is still out of place, or the knee looks out of shape: go to the emergency department now
- The kneecap popped out and went back, especially for the first time, or the knee swelled quickly: see a doctor the same day for an X-ray
- You keep getting the feeling that the kneecap might slip, or it has slipped more than once: book a physiotherapy assessment

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can check your knee and explain what happened when the kneecap shifted.
- Thigh and hip strengthening, with short-term brace advice if needed, aims to help support the kneecap.
- You get a step-by-step return-to-sport plan to help lower the chance of it happening again.

## clinicNotes
- Knee document form 10 (Theory pp. 488-492; Lower Quadrant Clinical Manual pp. 432-437, checked): 70% under 20; girls 10-17 at highest risk; spontaneous reduction in 80%; MPFL lesion in 83%; osteochondral fragments or loose bodies in a third (knee-loosebody). Moving patellar apprehension test (Bank 2017): sens 100%, spec 88% under anaesthesia. Recurrence (Parikh 2018): 2 or more risk factors over 50%, 3 or more 74.8% (young age, skeletal immaturity, female, bilateral or family history, trochlear dysplasia, patella alta, TT lateralisation); falls 8% per year of age, none after 40 (Lewallen). Ipsilateral 36% vs contralateral 5.4% at 20 years (Christensen 2017). Return to play 84-100% by functional criteria, 3-12 months (Sherman 2018). ESSKA 2024 first-time dislocation consensus (checked 26 Oct 2026; KSSTA, Part 1 PMC12022820, Part 2 PMC12684363; no grade A statement): X-ray (AP, lateral, axial) and MRI, or MRI alone, for every first-time dislocation, as soon as available (C); haemarthrosis is a warning sign for an osteochondral fracture (C); never decide on nonoperative care from examination alone (C); low trauma intensity suggests more severe anatomy (C). Nonoperative care only with low recurrence risk in the skeletally mature (C); surgery to be discussed with every skeletally immature patient and family (C) and considered first with several risk factors (C); no brace is better than another (B); physiotherapy-guided exercise is a necessary supplement (C); repair a patellofemoral fragment of 1 cm2 or more (C); without a repairable fragment, immediate surgery is not needed (D). No statement on return to sport. Note: the approved physioHelps line "short-term brace advice if needed" sits against ESSKA's brace statement (B): for Chandra to decide.
- Presentation (AIM manual pp.63-66): knee flexed with internal rotation of the femur over a planted foot (valgus); the kneecap usually goes laterally and about 80% reduce spontaneously; haemarthrosis, medial (MPFL) tenderness overlapping the femoral MCL origin, apprehension, difficulty walking. 70% are under 20; highest risk in girls aged 10-17.
- Recurrence: about 36% at 20 years; 60-70% with trochlear dysplasia under 25; two or more Parikh risk factors give over 50% risk. Past dislocation is associated with later patellofemoral OA (OR 3.2; p.64).
- Imaging: x-ray in all suspected dislocations (osteochondral fragment); MRI for young first-time dislocations (p.66). Differentials: ACL tear (pivot, pop, haemarthrosis), MCL sprain, osteochondral fracture.
- Management: first-time dislocations are usually managed without surgery (bracing short term, quadriceps and hip strengthening, return-to-play criteria, pp.66-74); surgery for an osteochondral fragment or recurrent instability.
- Sources: AIM Theory Manual 2023 Ch. 2.7 pp.63-74; Willy RW et al., JOSPT 2019 (OR 3.2).
