## blurb
A feeling of being off-balance or unsteady that may come from the neck. Your brain uses signals from the joints and muscles of the upper neck, together with your eyes and inner ear, to know where your head is. When the neck is sore, stiff or has been injured, those signals can become less accurate, and the brain may feel "off". Once other causes have been checked, it is not dangerous in itself, and it often eases as the neck settles and moves better. Because dizziness has several possible causes, these are checked first, so you get the right care; if your family doctor has not yet looked at your dizziness, see them alongside physiotherapy.

## noticed
- Feeling unsteady, "floaty" or off-balance, rather than the room spinning
- It started with neck pain or stiffness: after a whiplash injury or concussion, or gradually alongside long-standing neck pain
- Turning your head or holding it in one position (desk work, driving, looking up at a shelf) brings it on
- Episodes that last minutes to hours, often with neck stiffness and a headache at the back of the head
- It follows the neck: better when the neck is better, worse when neck pain flares
- Eyes tiring easily, needing to concentrate to read, or trouble judging distances
- Often worse when you are tired or stressed, and stronger when the neck hurts more

## homeCare
- Change position every 30 to 45 minutes during desk work or driving, and set screens at eye level
- Keep the neck moving gently, within comfort
- If you feel dizzy, stop, sit down, and fix your eyes on a still point
- Avoid driving, ladders or heights while you feel dizzy

## seePhysioIf
- You feel off-balance or dizzy along with neck pain or stiffness, especially after a neck injury: an assessment can help work out whether your neck is involved, and we will involve your doctor if another cause needs checking
- You would like a plan to retrain neck position sense, eye and head coordination, and balance
- The dizziness keeps coming back with desk work, screens or driving

## clinicNotes
- Diagnosis of exclusion (Reiley 2017): no single confirming test. Care route: physio-led after screening; if other causes of dizziness have not been checked, family doctor alongside.
- Screen first: Canadian C-spine rule (if trauma); IFOMPT cervical framework for vascular pathology; neuro screen (5 Ds, 3 Ns).
- Vestibular: Dix-Hallpike, supine roll, head impulse, spontaneous and gaze-evoked nystagmus, head-neck differentiation (body-on-head rotation).
- Cervical: AROM, upper-cervical PAIVMs (C0-C3), flexion-rotation test, palpation reproducing dizziness.
- Sensorimotor: joint position error (over 4.5 degrees abnormal), smooth pursuit neck torsion test, balance (Romberg, tandem, single-leg).
- Management: education; upper-cervical mobilisation and SNAGs after screening (low to very low certainty; Reid 2015; "Carrasco-Uribarren 2025" could not be verified); position-sense and eye-head retraining, balance; neck strength and endurance; ergonomics; add vestibular rehab if an inner-ear problem coexists. No thrust manipulation without full vascular screening (IFOMPT 2023).
- Outcomes: Dizziness Handicap Inventory and Neck Disability Index.
- AIM Theory Manual 2023 (pp. 236-254; content/reference/cervical-conditions-manual.md): Reiley 2017 stepwise algorithm (history, triage, vestibular, detailed cervical exam, then torsion, relocation and movement sense tests). JPE over 7.1 cm at 90 cm; tracing over 9 (zigzag) or 10 (figure-of-8) errors. Clinical SPNTT sens 27%, spec 79%: a positive helps rule in. Cervical torsion test and head relocation help separate CGD from BPPV (L'Heureux-Lebeau 2014): torsion test sens 72%, spec 92%; SPNTT (laboratory) 56% / 88% (AIM). The "nystagmus over 20/s" threshold comes from the OCR of the manual and could not be verified: check the printed page or the paper before using it. Torsion and SPNTT findings are supporting impairment information, not diagnostic.
- Visual symptoms common with neck pain: needing to concentrate to read, visual fatigue, judging distances, light sensitivity. Double vision, red eyes or words moving point elsewhere. Torsion balance test is possibly the only balance test that suggests a neck source (AIM: "other than perhaps").
- Management evidence: SNAGs or Maitland to the upper cervical spine plus home exercise, benefit to 12 months (Reid 2014, 2015); neck exercise with a cognitive behavioural approach in chronic WAD dizziness (Treleaven 2016); head relocation practice 85% vs vestibular rehab 18% in dizzy mTBI with abnormal proprioception (Hammerle 2019). Tailor manual therapy and exercise plus sensorimotor training to the impairments found.
- Post-concussion dizziness (JOSPT concussion CPG 2020): history cannot separate the sources; examine cervical and thoracic (C/F), vestibulo-oculomotor (B), and orthostatic HR and BP (B); Dix-Hallpike when BPPV is suspected and repositioning if positive (A). In the sport-concussion Delphi, cervical tests for dizziness (flexion-rotation, torsion, head-neck differentiation, motor control) rated weak and vestibular tests (head impulse, nystagmus, positional, saccades, VOR cancellation, head shake, pursuits) strong: treat neck tests as supporting, not confirming. Source: Quatman-Yates, JOSPT 2020.
- JOSPT Neck Pain CPG 2017 (Blanpied et al.) does not review dizziness (a stated limitation); its only relevant content is the chronic WAD flow chart (vestibular rehabilitation, eye-head-neck and neuromuscular coordination).
- Barany Society (2022): no evidence linking neck pathology to vertigo and no endorsed diagnostic criteria for "cervical dizziness"; record it as a possible cervical sensorimotor contribution (Cervical Clinic Manual 2026 §9, §10.6: contested).
- HINTS (Kattah 2009) applies only in acute continuous vertigo: a NORMAL head impulse there is a central (stroke) warning, with direction-changing nystagmus or skew: emergency. Needs training and correct syndrome selection (Cervical Clinic Manual 2026). The 5 Ds and 3 Ns are prompts; their absence does not clear vascular risk (§14).
- Look-alikes asked on the site: spinal cord (clumsy hands, walking: cordSign card), vestibular migraine (throbbing headache with nausea or light sensitivity), orthostatic or presyncope (on standing: dizzyStanding card), posterior circulation or artery dissection (brief double vision, slurred speech, swallowing, drop attacks, face numbness: dizzyVascular card, doctor today or 911).
