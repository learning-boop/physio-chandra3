## blurb
The space between the bone you sit on and the top of the thigh bone is narrower in some people. A small, deep buttock muscle can get squeezed there each time the leg swings back, giving a deep buttock ache with long strides or fast walking. It is often overlooked, but many people improve once the stride and the hip muscles are worked on.

## noticed
- A deep ache in the buttock, just to the outside of the bone you sit on
- Brought on by long strides, fast walking or running, or a long step back or lunge
- Eases when you take shorter steps or slow down
- Sometimes a snap at the back of the hip, or tingling down the back of the thigh

## homeCare
- Take shorter steps when walking fast, and slow down on hills for a while
- Avoid long lunges and big steps backward for now
- Stay active with comfortable activities such as cycling or swimming
- Gentle strengthening of the outer hip and trunk muscles, within comfort

## seePhysioIf
- Deep buttock pain is limiting your walking or running, or has been around for several weeks
- You would like help to change your stride and a strengthening plan
- Pain or pins and needles spreading below the knee, or back pain with it: see us so the low back can be checked
- New numbness around your bottom or between your legs, or new bladder or bowel trouble: go to the emergency department
- It is not easing after a few months of a guided programme: your doctor can arrange a scan or an opinion about an injection

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 26 Oct 2026 (in chat; written from the hip document form 5) -->
- A physiotherapist can watch you walk with short and long steps and explain what is squeezing at the back of the hip.
- Changes to your stride and a step-by-step strengthening programme for the hip and trunk can help ease the pain.
- If it does not settle, we can talk with you about seeing your doctor for a scan.

## clinicNotes
- Tests (Gomez-Hoyos 2016, endoscopically confirmed): IFI test (side-lying passive extension in neutral or adduction reproduces pain lateral to the ischium, relieved in abduction) sens 0.82, spec 0.85, +LR 5.35, -LR 0.21; long-stride walking test (pain with long strides, relieved by short strides or slight abduction) sens 0.94, spec 0.85, +LR 6.12, -LR 0.07. Both match the AIM Theory Manual 2023 pp. 389-390.
- Trendelenburg sign is often positive (Martin 2016); look for frontal-plane pelvic drift (adduction and external rotation in gait).
- Imaging: MRI ischiofemoral space 17 mm or less, quadratus femoris space 8 mm or less (Torriani 2009; not for children); quadratus femoris oedema or fatty change. An image-guided injection can confirm.
- Who: about 3 in 4 adult cases are women; bilateral in about a third; acquired after hip surgery or a proximal femur fracture. Differentials: proximal hamstring tendinopathy, deep gluteal syndrome, sciatica, quadratus femoris tear, adductor tendinopathy (Singer 2015).
- Management: stride shortening and gait retraining, abductor and trunk strengthening, a heel lift or insert if leg length or foot posture contributes; refer for imaging or injection if it persists. No CPG exists (searched 26 Oct 2026); the JOSPT nonarthritic hip CPG 2023 does not grade it.
- Sources: AIM Theory Manual 2023 Ch. 2.6 pp. 387-395; Gomez-Hoyos J et al., Arthroscopy 2016;32(7):1279-84; Torriani M et al., AJR 2009;193(1):186-90; Singer AD et al., Skeletal Radiol 2015;44(6):831-7.
