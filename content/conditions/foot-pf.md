## blurb
Irritation of the strong tissue band supporting the arch, where it anchors into the heel — the classic sharp heel pain with the first steps of the morning.

## noticed
- Sharp bottom-of-heel pain on first morning steps
- Aches after long standing or walking, often worse after activity than during
- Stiffness and tenderness in the arch and heel

## homeCare
- Calf and plantar-fascia stretches (toes pulled up) morning and evening
- Supportive footwear; avoid long barefoot walking on hard floors for now
- A frozen-bottle roll under the arch can soothe; build activity gradually
- Simple strengthening once it is less sore, such as slow heel raises
- Ask us about taping, or a night splint if the first steps in the morning stay painful

## seePhysioIf
- Pain when you squeeze the sides of the heel or hop, or heel pain at night or at rest: see a doctor first (possible stress fracture)
- Pain in both heels, with back stiffness in the mornings, psoriasis, sore red eyes or other swollen joints: see your doctor
- The heel is hot, red and swollen, or you have a fever: see a doctor the same day
- Numbness or burning in both feet, or it is spreading up the legs: see your doctor
- Heel pain lasts more than 2 weeks
- Mornings or long standing remain painful
- You want a loading and footwear plan — most cases settle with the right progression
- Burning on the inner heel that builds through the day, rather than first-step pain: see us, as a small nerve may be involved

## physioHelps
<!-- Approved by Chandra Matla, 17 Oct 2026 (review: content/review/physioHelps-review-2026-10-17.docx) -->
- A physiotherapist can confirm what is causing your heel pain and explain it in plain words.
- Calf and foot stretches, hands-on care and taping can help ease the first-step pain.
- You get a plan for footwear, a night splint if needed, and building back activity.

## clinicNotes
- Thigh, leg, ankle and foot document form 13 (signed v1.0 by Chandra, 26 Oct 2026): Diagnosis (CPG 2023, B; Theory p. 566): plantar-medial heel pain most noticeable with first steps after inactivity and after prolonged WB; onset after a recent increase in WB; pain on palpation of the proximal plantar fascia insertion; positive windlass; negative tarsal tunnel tests; limited active/passive talocrural DF; abnormal FPI; high BMI in non-athletes. Windlass test (MP 516): stabilise calcaneus, passively extend the MTPs (1st or all), ankle neutral; positive = plantar fascia pain near the calcaneus; high Sp, low Sn. Sn 33% non-weight-bearing, Sp 100% weight-bearing (Cole 2005; Theory p. 543); Sn 32%, Sp 100% (De Garceau 2003; Theory p. 567). It also tensions the plantar nerves, so it cannot separate nerve causes (Alshami 2007). US plantar fascia thickening Sn 80.9%, Sp 85.7% (Mohseni-Bandpei 2014; Theory p. 542). No gold standard; calcification on X-ray not indicative; ~50% have heel spurs, mostly incidental (McMillan 2009; Theory p. 567). Screen: tarsal tunnel (Tinel, DF-eversion test; MP 508–509); fat pad atrophy (central heel tenderness; Yi 2011: prolonged-standing pain OR 20.91, night pain OR 20.94, bilateral pain OR 24.95, without first-step pain; Theory p. 567); calcaneal stress fracture (point tenderness; Theory p. 568); lumbar radiculopathy (LQ scan, SLR with DF + eversion bias; Theory p. 564); gout, RA, spondyloarthropathy, infection, tumour, vascular causes (Theory pp. 563–564). Consider spondyloarthritis, fat pad atrophy or proximal plantar fibroma (25% of recalcitrant specimens; Hafner 2011) if the picture does not fit or is not resolving (Theory p. 567). Risk factors (Theory pp. 543, 566): limited DF, high BMI (non-athletes), work/activity WB, poor shock absorption (flooring, footwear), running, pes planus/cavus, hindfoot varus, hamstring tightness, LLD (longer limb), pronated FPI-6. Pain-related fear of movement is the strongest single contributor to foot and ankle disability (Theory p. 565). Management evidence (Theory pp. 544–547): high-load PF with toes extended (8–12 RM; Rathleff 2015), short-foot and toe-flexor exercise; daily may be more effective than twice weekly (Young 2018); calcaneal and low-dye taping short term; orthoses help at 7–12 weeks, custom = prefabricated; ESWT for recalcitrant (>6 months) cases; corticosteroid injection not first line; surgery last (failure >15%). Outcomes: FAAM, FAOS, MFPDI or LEFS (MP 482); DF ROM incl. lunge test (MP 487–488; Table 7-1); 1st MTP extension; FPI (MP 522; Table 7-6); intrinsic foot muscle test (MP 520–521).
- Diagnosis (B, 2014; unchanged 2023): plantar medial heel pain worst on the first steps and after long weight bearing; a recent increase in weight-bearing activity; pain on palpation of the proximal plantar fascia insertion; positive windlass test; NEGATIVE tarsal tunnel, nerve tension and sensory tests; limited dorsiflexion; abnormal Foot Posture Index; high BMI in non-athletes. Rule out lumbopelvic referral (F).
- Risk factors (B): limited dorsiflexion, high BMI in non-athletes, running, work-related weight bearing on hard surfaces.
- Differentials: graded C in 2014: spondyloarthritis (heel pain in 47% of SpA, the first symptom in 16%), fat pad atrophy, proximal plantar fibroma. From the 2023 introduction and examination outline (not graded): tarsal tunnel, calcaneal stress fracture (palpate the body of the calcaneus), Baxter's nerve (inferior calcaneal nerve: medial heel burning, worse as the day goes on, abductor digiti minimi weakness; up to about 20% of chronic heel pain in secondary sources).
- Measures: FAAM, FHSQ or FFI (A); first-step pain VAS (B; MCID 19 mm); dorsiflexion ROM (B); FPI-6 (C).
- Treatment, 2023 revision (Koc TA et al., JOSPT 2023;53(12); grades read from the orthopt.org stakeholder draft, so check against the published text): manual therapy A; plantar fascia and calf stretching A; taping, rigid or elastic, with other treatment for 1 to 6 weeks A; night splints 1 to 3 months for consistent first-step pain A; foot orthoses B against using them alone for short-term pain, C combined with other care; dry needling of calf and plantar trigger points B; low-level laser B; resistance training C (draft; B in secondary summaries); local heat to calf trigger points C; phonophoresis C; iontophoresis or interferential D; weight education E; therapeutic ultrasound A against (as an add-on to stretching). Shockwave not graded: outside the scope of physical therapist practice. Corticosteroid injection: limited benefit, with risk of fat pad atrophy and fascia rupture. Imaging is rarely needed; a heel spur does not explain the pain.
- Your plantar fasciitis protocol differs from the 2023 revision: Phase 1 pulsed ultrasound (2023: A against); night splints from Phase 2 (2023: from the start when first-step pain is consistent); taping for 1 to 3 days (2023: 1 to 6 weeks).
- Sources: Martin RL et al., JOSPT 2014;44(11):A1-A23; Koc TA et al., JOSPT 2023;53(12):CPG1-CPG39.
