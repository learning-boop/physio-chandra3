## blurb
A small nerve runs under the inner side of the heel, close to where the thick band under the foot (the plantar fascia) attaches. When it is pinched or irritated, heel pain can burn or ache and build up through the day, rather than being worst on the first steps in the morning as plantar fasciitis usually is. The two often occur together, and both usually settle with the right load, footwear and exercises.

## noticed
- Burning, aching or sometimes tingling on the inner side of the heel
- Builds up the longer you stand, walk or run, and can still ache at rest in the evening
- Less of the sharp "first steps in the morning" pain that plantar fasciitis brings
- Common in runners and in people who stand all day

## homeCare
- Cushioned, supportive shoes; avoid long spells barefoot on hard floors
- Break up long periods of standing where you can
- Ease back running or long walks for now rather than stopping all activity
- Gentle calf and foot mobility within comfort

## seePhysioIf
- The heel pain has not started to ease after 2 to 3 weeks, or keeps returning
- Burning or numbness spreads into the sole or toes
- Pain on one spot of bone that is worse with every step or hopping, or when the sides of the heel are squeezed: see a doctor first, as it could be a stress fracture
- Heel pain with other swollen joints, back stiffness in the morning, or psoriasis: mention it to your doctor as well

## clinicNotes
- Baxter's nerve (first branch of the lateral plantar nerve) is caught between abductor hallucis and quadratus plantae, near the medial calcaneal tubercle; the medial calcaneal nerve supplies the heel skin. Features: medial heel burning that builds through the day, tenderness over the nerve (medial heel, deeper and more medial than the fascia insertion), sometimes abductor digiti minimi weakness (difficulty spreading the little toe); numbness is uncommon.
- Neurodynamics (Butler; Shacklock): tibial nerve test DF/EV then SLR, differentiating heel symptoms with the SLR (Shacklock's "focused sequence": ankle first); nerve palpation behind the medial malleolus and at the medial heel, compared with the other side; swelling over the tarsal tunnel.
- Management: footwear and load, treat the interface (Shacklock heel pain: static and dynamic openers in plantarflexion / inversion and forefoot adduction, then gentle closers), tibial nerve sliders (toe flexion with SLR; hip and knee flexion with toe extension), nerve massage at the medial ankle (Butler), progress to tensioners. Neural mobilisation helps plantar heel pain and tarsal tunnel (Basson et al. 2017). Imaging or nerve studies if not settling: MRI can show fatty atrophy of abductor digiti minimi.
- Look-alikes: plantar fasciitis (first-step pain, windlass; foot-pf.md, the two often coexist); tarsal tunnel syndrome (tingling into the sole and toes); calcaneal stress fracture (squeeze test, the footStress card); fat pad (central bruised feeling); lumbar S1 radiculopathy (back, below-knee pain); spondyloarthritis (heel pain in 47%).
- Sources: Butler DS, NOI neurodynamic techniques workbook (tibial nerve); Shacklock NDS lower-quarter manual 2017-18 (heel pain); Koc TA et al., JOSPT 2023 plantar heel pain CPG (differentials); Basson A et al., JOSPT 2017.
